3.2 Equivariant Estimators

Some models have a symmetry, and it is natural to ask that the estimator have the same symmetry.

Definition 3.2.1. A model \(\{f_\theta (x) : \theta \in \mathbb {R}\}\) with \(f_\theta (x) = f_0(x - \theta )\) for a known density \(f_0\) is a location family, and \(\theta \) is a location parameter.

Example 3.2.2. Show that \(N(\theta , 1)\), the Cauchy distribution with location \(\theta \), and the shifted exponential \(f_\theta (x) = e^{-(x - \theta )}\), \(x>\theta \), are location families.

Solution. In each case \(f_\theta (x) = f_0(x - \theta )\), with respectively \[f_0(z) = \frac {1}{\sqrt {2\pi }}e^{-z^2/2},\qquad f_0(z) = \frac {1}{\pi (1 + z^2)},\qquad f_0(z) = e^{-z}I(z>0) .\] In each, \(X = \theta + Z\) where \(Z\) has the fixed density \(f_0\).

If \(X_i = \theta + Z_i\), shifting every observation by \(a\) is the same as shifting the parameter by \(a\). A sensible estimator should therefore shift by \(a\) too.

Definition 3.2.3. An estimator \(\tilde \theta (X_1, \dots , X_n)\) is location equivariant if \[\tilde \theta (x_1 + a, \dots , x_n + a) = \tilde \theta (x_1, \dots , x_n) + a\] for every constant \(a\) and every sample \((x_1, \dots , x_n)\).

Example 3.2.4. Show that the sample mean, the sample median and \(X_{(1)}\) are location equivariant.

Solution. \(\frac 1n\sum (x_i + a) = \frac 1n\sum x_i + \frac 1n\cdot na = \bar x + a\). Adding \(a\) to every observation does not change their order, so every order statistic, and in particular the median and \(X_{(1)}\), increases by \(a\). By contrast, \(\frac 1n\sum x_i^2\) is not equivariant.

Theorem 3.2.5. In a location family, the bias, variance and mean square error of a location equivariant estimator do not depend on \(\theta \).

Proof. Write \(X_i = \theta + Z_i\), where \(Z_1, \dots , Z_n\) are independent with the known density \(f_0\). By equivariance with \(a = \theta \), \[\tilde \theta (X_1, \dots , X_n) - \theta = \tilde \theta (Z_1, \dots , Z_n),\] whose distribution involves \(f_0\) only. Hence the distribution of the error \(\tilde \theta - \theta \), and every moment of it, is the same for all \(\theta \). □

Since every equivariant estimator has constant risk, they can be compared by a single number, and there is a best one. It is called the Pitman estimator.

Example 3.2.6. For a random sample from the shifted exponential \(f_\theta (x) = e^{-(x - \theta )}\), \(x>\theta \), find the best estimator, in mean square error, of the form \(X_{(1)} - c\).

Solution. Each such estimator is equivariant. By example 2.5.7, \(X_{(1)} - \theta \) is exponential with mean \(\frac 1n\) and variance \(\frac 1{n^2}\), so \[\MSE \left (X_{(1)} - c\right ) = E\left (X_{(1)} - \theta - c\right )^2 = \frac {1}{n^2} + \left (\frac 1n - c\right )^2 ,\] which, as theorem 3.2.5 says, is free of \(\theta \). It is minimised at \(c = \frac 1n\), giving the unbiased estimator \(X_{(1)} - \frac 1n\) with mean square error \(\frac {1}{n^2}\), half that of the ML estimator \(X_{(1)}\).

Definition 3.2.7. A model \(\{f_\theta (x) : \theta >0\}\) with \(f_\theta (x) = \frac 1\theta f_1\left (\frac x\theta \right )\) for a known density \(f_1\) is a scale family, and \(\theta \) is a scale parameter. An estimator \(\tilde \theta _k\) of \(\theta ^k\) is scale equivariant if \[\tilde \theta _k(cx_1, \dots , cx_n) = c^k\,\tilde \theta _k(x_1, \dots , x_n)\] for every \(c>0\) and every sample.

In a scale family \(X = \theta Z\) with \(Z\) having density \(f_1\), so changing the unit of measurement multiplies \(\theta \) by \(c\) and \(\theta ^k\) by \(c^k\).

Example 3.2.8. Let \(X_1, \dots , X_n\) be a random sample from \(N(0, \theta ^2)\), \(\theta >0\). Show that

(a)
this is a scale family;
(b)
the UMVUE of \(\theta ^2\) is scale equivariant.

Solution.

(a)
\(f_\theta (x) = \frac {1}{\sqrt {2\pi }\,\theta }e^{-\frac 12(x/\theta )^2} = \frac 1\theta f_1\left (\frac x\theta \right )\) with \(f_1(z) = \frac {1}{\sqrt {2\pi }}e^{-z^2/2}\).
(b)
The family is a REF with natural sufficient statistic \(\sum X_i^2\), which is complete. Since \(E(X_i^2) = \theta ^2\), the UMVUE of \(\theta ^2\) is \(\tilde \theta _2 = \frac 1n\sum X_i^2\), and \[\tilde \theta _2(cx_1, \dots , cx_n) = \frac 1n\sum (cx_i)^2 = c^2\,\tilde \theta _2(x_1, \dots , x_n),\] so it is scale equivariant with \(k = 2\).

Problem 3.2.1. Show that the \(UNIF(\theta - 1, \theta + 1)\) distribution is a location invariant family.

Show solution

Solution. The UNIF\((\theta -1,\theta +1)\) density is \[f_{\theta }(x) = \frac 12\,I\left (\theta -1<x<\theta +1\right ) = \frac 12\,I\left (-1<x-\theta <1\right ) = f_0(x-\theta ),\] where \(f_0\) is the UNIF\((-1,1)\) density. Since every member of the family is obtained from the single density \(f_0\) by a shift, the family is location invariant with location parameter \(\theta \).

Equivalently: if \(X\sim \) UNIF\((\theta -1,\theta +1)\) then \(X+c\sim \) UNIF\((\theta +c-1,\ \theta +c+1)\), so translating the data by \(c\) moves the parameter by \(c\) and leaves the family as a whole unchanged.

Problem 3.2.2. Let \(X_1, \, \cdots \, , \, X_n\) be a random sample the \(UNIF(0,\theta )\) distribution. Show that then

(a)
\(UNIF(0,\theta )\) densities is a scale invariant family.
(b)
\(UMVUE\) of \(\theta \) is a scale equivariant estimator.

Show solution

Solution.

(a)

The UNIF\((0,\theta )\) density is \[f_{\theta }(x) = \frac {1}{\theta }\,I\left (0<x<\theta \right ) = \frac {1}{\theta }\,I\left (0<\frac {x}{\theta }<1\right ) = \frac {1}{\theta }f_1\!\left (\frac {x}{\theta }\right ),\] with \(f_1\) the UNIF\((0,1)\) density. Every member is obtained from \(f_1\) by the scaling \(x\mapsto x/\theta \), so the family is scale invariant. Equivalently, if \(X\sim \) UNIF\((0,\theta )\) and \(c>0\) then \(cX\sim \) UNIF\((0,c\theta )\).

(b)

Since \(E\left (X_{(n)}\right )=n\theta /(n+1)\) and \(X_{(n)}\) is complete and sufficient, the UMVUE is \[\widehat {\theta } = \frac {n+1}{n}X_{(n)} .\] Scale equivariance asks that \(\widehat {\theta }(cX_1,\dots ,cX_n) = c\,\widehat {\theta }(X_1,\dots ,X_n)\) for every \(c>0\). The maximum of the rescaled sample is \(c\,X_{(n)}\), so \[\widehat {\theta }(cX) = \frac {n+1}{n}\,cX_{(n)} = c\,\widehat {\theta }(X),\] and the estimator is scale equivariant.

Note. Equivariance is a consistency requirement, not an optimality one: it says only that measuring the data in different units should change the estimate in the same units. It is worth checking because an estimator that fails it is answering a different question depending on whether the lifetimes were recorded in days or in hours.
