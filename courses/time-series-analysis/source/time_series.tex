\section{Introduction}
\begin{defn}
A \textbf{time series} is an ordered sequence of observations $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$. The ordering is usually in
time, but it may be in another dimension, such as distance along a line.
\end{defn}

What makes a time series a subject of its own is that its observations are \emph{dependent}: this month's
value is related to last month's. In most of statistics observations are assumed independent, and their order
does not matter; in a time series the order is the most important thing about the data. Time series arise in
every field:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Agriculture}: annual crop production, livestock numbers, crop prices.
\item \textbf{Business and economics}: annual sales of a product, commodity prices, price indices such as
inflation, stock-exchange earnings, exchange rates.
\item \textbf{Engineering}: electrical signals, voltage, sound.
\item \textbf{Geophysics}: the height of ocean waves, seismic records.
\item \textbf{Meteorology}: sea-surface temperature, rainfall, wind speed.
\item \textbf{Medicine}: annual mortality rates, failure rates of a surgical procedure.
\item \textbf{Quality control}: measurements monitoring a production process.
\item \textbf{Social sciences}: birth rates, mortality rates, accident and crime rates.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Types of time series}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A time series recorded continuously in time is said to be \textbf{continuous}, for example an electrical
signal or a voltage trace.
\item A time series recorded only at specific times, usually equally spaced, is said to be \textbf{discrete}, for
example a monthly interest rate, an annual crop yield or a daily closing price.
\end{enumerate}
A discrete series may arise by \emph{sampling} a continuous one at intervals (the temperature at noon each
day) or by \emph{aggregating} over intervals (the rainfall in each month). These notes deal with discrete series
observed at equal intervals, written $x_t$ for $t = 1, \dots, n$.

\subsection{Objectives of time-series analysis}
A major objective is to understand and describe the mechanism that generates the series. Because the
observations are dependent, this mechanism can be exploited for:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Description}: computing descriptive measures, and above all plotting the series, to characterise its
main features.
\item \textbf{Explanation}: when observations are taken on two or more variables, using the variation in one
series to explain the variation in another, for example rainfall and crop yield.
\item \textbf{Prediction}: forecasting future values from the past of the series.
\item \textbf{Control}: adjusting a process, especially in quality control in manufacturing, so that it stays on
target.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Descriptive techniques}
Simple descriptive methods are the first step of any analysis. They classify the main properties of a series,
and often suggest the model to use.

\subsection{Components of a time series}
Traditionally a series is analysed by \emph{decomposing} it into components, each a source of variation:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Trend}: a long-term change in the mean level.
\item \textbf{Seasonal effects}: variation that repeats with a fixed period, usually annual, such as sales that
rise every December.
\item \textbf{Cyclic effects}: variation with a longer, less regular period, from some other physical or economic
cause; business cycles, for example, often have a period of five to seven years.
\item \textbf{Irregular fluctuations}: what is left when the trend, seasonal and cyclic variation have been
removed. These residuals may or may not be random.
\end{enumerate}
The components are usually combined in one of two ways. With trend $m_t$, seasonal effect $s_t$ and irregular term
$\varepsilon_t$:
\[
\text{additive:}\quad x_t = m_t + s_t + \varepsilon_t , \qquad\qquad \text{multiplicative:}\quad x_t = m_t\,s_t\,\varepsilon_t .
\]
In the additive model the seasonal swings have the same size whatever the level of the series; in the
multiplicative model they grow in proportion to the level. Figure~\ref{fig:ts-components} shows how the
components add up to a series.

\subsection{Plotting the series and stationarity}
The first and most important step in any time-series analysis is to plot the observations against time. The
plot shows the important features of the series, such as trend, seasonality, outliers and sudden changes
(discontinuities), and it helps in formulating a sensible model.

Loosely, a series is \textbf{stationary} if it has no systematic change in mean (no trend), no systematic change in
variance, and no strictly periodic variation. Most of the theory of later sections is for stationary series, so a
common strategy is to remove the trend and seasonal effects first and then model what is left. Stationarity is
defined precisely in the next section.

\subsection{Transformations}
A plot may suggest transforming the series, for example by taking logarithms or square roots. There are three
main reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
\item to stabilise the variance;
\item to make the seasonal effect additive;
\item to make the data closer to normally distributed.
\end{enumerate}
The logarithm does the first two jobs at once when the series is multiplicative, as the next result shows.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:ts-log}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item If $x_t = m_t\,s_t\,\varepsilon_t$ with all factors positive, then $\log x_t = \log m_t + \log s_t + \log\varepsilon_t$, an additive model.
\item If the standard deviation of $x_t$ is proportional to its mean $\mu_t$, say $\operatorname{sd}(x_t) = c\,\mu_t$, then approximately
$\Var(\log x_t) \approx c^2$, which does not depend on $t$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
(i) is the rule $\log(abc) = \log a + \log b + \log c$. For (ii), by Taylor's theorem $\log x_t \approx \log\mu_t + (x_t - \mu_t)/\mu_t$ when $x_t$ is
close to $\mu_t$. The right-hand side has variance $\Var(x_t)/\mu_t^2 = c^2\mu_t^2/\mu_t^2 = c^2$.
\end{proof}

So when the seasonal swings, or the scatter, of a series grow with its level, take logarithms first.

\subsection{Estimating and removing the trend}
There are two broad approaches: estimate the trend and subtract it, or remove it by differencing.

\subhead{Fitting a curve}
A simple trend can be fitted by least squares as a function of time, for example a straight line $m_t = a + bt$, a
quadratic, or an exponential growth curve $m_t = ae^{bt}$ (linear after taking logarithms). This gives a formula,
but it imposes one shape on the whole series.

\subhead{Moving averages}
A \textbf{moving average} smooths the series by replacing each value with an average of its neighbours. The
simple moving average of order $2q + 1$ is
\[
\hat m_t = \frac{1}{2q + 1}\sum_{j=-q}^{q} x_{t+j} , \qquad t = q + 1, \dots, n - q .
\]

\begin{prop}\label{prop:ma-linear}
A moving average $\sum_{j=-q}^{q} w_jx_{t+j}$ with weights that add to $1$ and are symmetric ($w_{-j} = w_j$) leaves a linear trend
unchanged: if $x_t = a + bt$, then $\sum_j w_jx_{t+j} = a + bt$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
$\sum_j w_j(a + b(t + j)) = (a + bt)\sum_j w_j + b\sum_j jw_j = a + bt + 0$, since the terms $jw_j$ and $(-j)w_{-j}$ cancel in pairs.
\end{proof}

So a moving average keeps the trend while averaging out the irregular fluctuations, whose average over
several periods is close to zero. For seasonal data, the window should cover exactly one full season, so that
the seasonal effect is also averaged out. With quarterly data a season has $4$ observations, an even number,
so a simple average of four would be centred between two time points. The standard remedy is the
\textbf{centred moving average} (or $2\times4$ moving average):
\[
\hat m_t = \tfrac18x_{t-2} + \tfrac14x_{t-1} + \tfrac14x_t + \tfrac14x_{t+1} + \tfrac18x_{t+2} ,
\]
the average of the two four-term averages that straddle $t$. For monthly data the $2\times12$ average is built the
same way, with weights $\frac{1}{24}$ at the ends and $\frac{1}{12}$ in between.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:centred-ma}
Suppose $x_t = a + bt + s_t$, where $s_t$ has period $4$ ($s_{t+4} = s_t$) and $s_1 + s_2 + s_3 + s_4 = 0$. Then the centred moving average
gives $\hat m_t = a + bt$ exactly.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
The weights are symmetric and add to $\frac18 + \frac14 + \frac14 + \frac14 + \frac18 = 1$, so by Proposition~\ref{prop:ma-linear} the trend part
passes through unchanged. The seasonal part gives $\frac18s_{t-2} + \frac14s_{t-1} + \frac14s_t + \frac14s_{t+1} + \frac18s_{t+2}$. Since $s_{t+2} = s_{t-2}$, this is
$\frac14(s_{t-2} + s_{t-1} + s_t + s_{t+1})$, a quarter of the sum over four consecutive quarters, which is $0$.
\end{proof}

\subhead{Seasonal indices}
Once the trend has been estimated, the \emph{detrended} values $x_t - \hat m_t$ (additive model) contain the seasonal
effect plus noise. Averaging them over all the years for each season gives the raw seasonal indices, which
are adjusted, if necessary, to add to zero. Subtracting the indices from the data gives the \textbf{seasonally
adjusted} series. For a multiplicative model the same steps use ratios $x_t/\hat m_t$, and the indices are adjusted to
average $1$.

\begin{exa}\label{exa:quarterly}
The quarterly sales of a shop (in thousands) over four years were:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|cccc}
Year & Q1 & Q2 & Q3 & Q4 \\
\hline
1 & 19 & 22 & 26 & 23 \\
2 & 22 & 26 & 30 & 26 \\
3 & 26 & 30 & 35 & 31 \\
4 & 31 & 36 & 40 & 35 \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Estimate the trend by a centred moving average, find the seasonal indices of an additive model, and
seasonally adjust the series.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
\emph{The trend.} The centred moving average at $t$ is the sum of the two four-quarter totals that straddle $t$,
divided by $8$. The four-quarter totals, starting from quarters $1$--$4$, are
\[
90, \ 93, \ 97, \ 101, \ 104, \ 108, \ 112, \ 117, \ 122, \ 127, \ 133, \ 138, \ 142 .
\]
So the first centred average, at $t = 3$ (year~1, Q3), is $(90 + 93)/8 = 22.875$, the next is $(93 + 97)/8 = 23.75$, and so on:

\begin{tabular}{ccrrr}
$t$ & Quarter & $x_t$ & $\hat m_t$ & $x_t - \hat m_t$ \\
\hline
3 & Y1 Q3 & 26 & 22.875 & 3.125 \\
4 & Y1 Q4 & 23 & 23.750 & $-0.750$ \\
5 & Y2 Q1 & 22 & 24.750 & $-2.750$ \\

11 & Y3 Q3 & 35 & 31.125 & 3.875 \\

No centred average exists for the first two or last two quarters.

\emph{Seasonal indices.} Averaging the detrended values quarter by quarter:
\begin{align*}
\text{Q1}: \ & (-2.750 - 2.625 - 2.875)/3 = -2.75 , \\
\text{Q2}: \ & (0.375 + 0.125 + 1.000)/3 = 0.50 , \\

\subhead{Differencing}
Instead of estimating the trend, it can be removed by \textbf{differencing}. The difference operator is
$\nabla x_t = x_t - x_{t-1}$, and the seasonal difference of period $s$ is $\nabla_sx_t = x_t - x_{t-s}$.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:differencing}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item If $m_t = a + bt$, then $\nabla m_t = b$, a constant; if $m_t$ is a quadratic in $t$, then $\nabla^2m_t = \nabla(\nabla m_t)$ is a constant.
\item If $s_t$ has period $s$, then $\nabla_ss_t = 0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
(i) $\nabla(a + bt) = (a + bt) - (a + b(t - 1)) = b$. For $m_t = a + bt + ct^2$, $\nabla m_t = b + c(2t - 1)$, which is linear in $t$, so a second difference
gives the constant $2c$. (ii) $\nabla_ss_t = s_t - s_{t-s} = 0$ by periodicity.
\end{proof}

Differencing is the basis of the ARIMA models of later sections. For the quarterly sales, the seasonal
differences $\nabla_4x_t$ are $3, 4, 4, 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 5, 6, 5, 4$: the seasonal pattern has gone, and what remains varies around
the yearly growth of about $4$ (four quarters at one thousand each).

\subsection{The sample autocorrelation function}\label{ssec:sample-acf}
Sample autocorrelation coefficients are an important guide to the properties of a time series. They measure
the correlation between observations a given time apart.

Recall that for $n$ pairs of observations $(x_i, y_i)$, the correlation coefficient is
\[
r = \frac{\sum(x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}{\sqrt{\sum(x_i - \bar x)^2\sum(y_i - \bar y)^2}} .
\]
Given $n$ observations $x_1, \dots, x_n$ of a time series, we can form the $n - 1$ pairs
\[
(x_1, x_2), \ (x_2, x_3), \ \dots, \ (x_{n-1}, x_n) ,
\]
and
treat the first member of each pair as one variable and the second as another. Their correlation is
\[
r_1 = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}(x_t - \bar x_{(1)})(x_{t+1} - \bar x_{(2)})}{\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}(x_t - \bar x_{(1)})^2\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}(x_{t+1} - \bar x_{(2)})^2}} ,
\]
where $\bar x_{(1)}$ is the mean of the first $n - 1$ observations and $\bar x_{(2)}$ the mean of the last $n - 1$. For a long series both means
are close to the overall mean $\bar x$, and both sums of squares in the denominator are close to $\sum_{t=1}^n(x_t - \bar x)^2$. This
gives the simpler and standard form
\[
r_1 = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}(x_t - \bar x)(x_{t+1} - \bar x)}{\sum_{t=1}^{n}(x_t - \bar x)^2} ,
\]
the \textbf{autocorrelation coefficient} at lag $1$. Similarly, the correlation between observations a
distance $k$ apart is the \textbf{autocorrelation coefficient at lag $k$},
\[
r_k = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n-k}(x_t - \bar x)(x_{t+k} - \bar x)}{\sum_{t=1}^{n}(x_t - \bar x)^2} .
\]
In practice the autocorrelations are calculated from the \textbf{sample autocovariance} at lag $k$,
\[
c_k = \frac1n\sum_{t=1}^{n-k}(x_t - \bar x)(x_{t+k} - \bar x) ,
\]
as $r_k = c_k/c_0$, for $k = 1, 2, \dots$, with $k < n$. (The divisor $n$ rather than $n - k$ is deliberate: it keeps the sequence of
$c_k$ a valid autocovariance function, a property needed in Section~3.)

\begin{prop}\label{prop:rk-bound}
$|r_k| \le 1$ for every $k$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Write $u_t = x_t - \bar x$. By the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality,
\[
\Bigl|\sum_{t=1}^{n-k}u_tu_{t+k}\Bigr| \le \sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{n-k}u_t^2}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{n-k}u_{t+k}^2} \le \sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^nu_t^2}\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^nu_t^2} = \sum_{t=1}^nu_t^2 ,
\]
since each partial sum of squares is at most the full one. Dividing by $\sum u_t^2$ gives $|r_k| \le 1$.
\end{proof}

\subhead{The correlogram}
A plot of $r_k$ against $k$ is called a \textbf{correlogram}. It is one of the most useful descriptive tools, and
its shape is a strong clue to the nature of the series (Figure~\ref{fig:correlograms}):
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Random series.} If the observations are independent, $r_k$ is approximately $N(0, 1/n)$ for each $k \ne 0$, so about $95\%$
of the coefficients should lie within $\pm2/\sqrt n$. (This result is justified later, with the partial autocorrelation function.)
\item \emph{Short-term correlation.} A fairly large $r_1$, followed by a few smaller positive coefficients that die
away towards zero, indicates that an observation above the mean tends to be followed by a few more.
\item \emph{Alternating series.} If successive observations tend to lie on opposite sides of the mean, the
correlogram alternates in sign, starting with a negative $r_1$.
\item \emph{Non-stationary series.} If the series has a trend, $r_k$ comes down only slowly and stays positive for large
lags: the trend dominates everything else. The correlogram is then of little use except to show that the
trend must be removed first.
\item \emph{Seasonal series.} If the series has a seasonal pattern, the correlogram oscillates with the same period:
for monthly data, large positive values at lags $12, 24, \dots$ and negative values around lags $6, 18, \dots$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{exa}\label{exa:sample-acf}
Compute $r_1$, $r_2$ and $r_3$ for the series $4, 6, 9, 7, 5, 3, 4, 6, 8, 8$.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
Here $n = 10$ and $\bar x = 60/10 = 6$, so the deviations $u_t = x_t - 6$ are
\[
-2, \ 0, \ 3, \ 1, \ -1, \ -3, \ -2, \ 0, \ 2, \ 2 ,
\]
with $\sum u_t^2 = 4 + 0 + 9 + 1 + 1 + 9 + 4 + 0 + 4 + 4 = 36$. The lagged products are:
\begin{align*}
\text{lag } 1: \ & 0 + 0 + 3 - 1 + 3 + 6 + 0 + 0 + 4 = 15 , \\
\text{lag } 2: \ & -6 + 0 - 3 - 3 + 2 + 0 - 4 + 0 = -14 , \\
\text{lag } 3: \ & -2 + 0 - 9 - 2 + 0 - 6 - 4 = -23 .
\end{align*}
So
\[
r_1 = \frac{15}{36} = 0.417 , \qquad r_2 = \frac{-14}{36} = -0.389 , \qquad r_3 = \frac{-23}{36} = -0.639 .
\]
Equivalently, $c_0 = 36/10 = 3.6$ and $c_1 = 1.5$, giving $r_1 = 1.5/3.6$. The positive $r_1$ and the negative $r_3$ reflect the slow
up-and-down swing in the series, with a period of about six observations: values three apart tend to lie on
opposite sides of the mean. With only $10$ observations the band $\pm2/\sqrt{10} = \pm0.63$ is wide, and only $r_3$ reaches it.
\end{solution}

\subsection{Practice problems}
\begin{problem}\label{prob:ts-ma3}
Compute the simple moving average of order $3$ for the series $5, 8, 6, 9, 7, 10, 8$, and explain why it cannot be
computed at the first and last time points.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
The averages at $t = 2, \dots, 6$ are $(5 + 8 + 6)/3 = 6.33$, $(8 + 6 + 9)/3 = 7.67$, $(6 + 9 + 7)/3 = 7.33$, $(9 + 7 + 10)/3 = 8.67$ and
$(7 + 10 + 8)/3 = 8.33$. The series zig-zags, and the moving average smooths it into a gently rising trend. At $t = 1$ there is
no $x_0$, and at $t = 7$ no $x_8$, so the symmetric window does not fit.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}\label{prob:ts-multiplicative}
Quarterly ratios $x_t/\hat m_t$ averaged over several years gave the raw multiplicative seasonal indices $0.90$, $1.05$, $1.20$,
$0.89$. Adjust them to average $1$, and seasonally adjust a third-quarter value of $48$.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
The raw indices add to $4.04$, an average of $1.01$, so divide each by $1.01$: $0.891$, $1.040$, $1.188$, $0.881$. The adjusted value
of the third-quarter observation is $48/1.188 = 40.4$: third quarters run about $19\%$ above trend.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}\label{prob:ts-12ma}
Show that the $2\times12$ centred moving average has weights $\frac{1}{24}$ on $x_{t\pm6}$ and $\frac{1}{12}$ on $x_{t-5}, \dots, x_{t+5}$, and that it removes any
seasonal pattern of period $12$ whose effects add to zero.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
The two twelve-term averages straddling $t$ cover $x_{t-6}, \dots, x_{t+5}$ and $x_{t-5}, \dots, x_{t+6}$. Averaging them, $x_{t-5}, \dots, x_{t+5}$ appear in
both, with weight $\frac12(\frac1{12} + \frac1{12}) = \frac1{12}$, and $x_{t-6}$, $x_{t+6}$ in one only, with weight $\frac1{24}$. For the seasonal part,
$s_{t+6} = s_{t-6}$, so the weighted sum is $\frac1{12}(s_{t-6} + s_{t-5} + \dots + s_{t+5})$, a twelfth of the sum over a whole year, which is $0$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}\label{prob:ts-trend-acf}
For the series $x_t = t$, $t = 1, \dots, 10$, compute $r_1$ and $r_5$, and comment.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$\bar x = 5.5$, and $u_t = t - 5.5$ runs from $-4.5$ to $4.5$ in steps of $1$, with $\sum u_t^2 = 82.5$. For lag $1$, $\sum_{t=1}^9u_tu_{t+1} = 57.75$, so
$r_1 = 0.70$. For lag $5$, $\sum_{t=1}^5u_tu_{t+5} = (-4.5)(0.5) + (-3.5)(1.5) + (-2.5)(2.5) + (-1.5)(3.5) + (-0.5)(4.5) = -21.25$, so $r_5 = -0.258$. The
coefficients of a short trending series fall steadily (here they turn negative because with $n = 10$ the two ends of the
series are paired); for a long trending series they stay positive and large for many lags. Either way the
correlogram mainly reflects the trend, which should be removed before the correlogram is interpreted.
\end{solution}

\section{Stochastic processes}
Time series are modelled as \emph{random processes}. A \textbf{stochastic process} is a family of random
variables $X(\omega, t)$ indexed by time $t$, where $\omega$ belongs to a sample space and $t$ to an index set (here the integers).
For a fixed $t$, $X(\omega, t)$ is a random variable. For a fixed $\omega$, $X(\omega, t)$ as a function of $t$ is called a \emph{sample
function} or a \textbf{realisation}. The population of all possible realisations is called the \emph{ensemble}.
An observed time series is thus one realisation of a stochastic process, and the aim of the analysis is to
learn about the process from that single realisation. We write $X_t$ for $X(\omega, t)$.

A process is described completely by the joint distributions of every finite set of its variables: for any
times $t_1, \dots, t_n$, the joint distribution function $F(x_{t_1}, \dots, x_{t_n})$ of $X_{t_1}, \dots, X_{t_n}$. This is usually far more than can be
known, and a simpler description uses the first and second moments:
\begin{itemize}
\item the \textbf{mean function} $\mu(t) = E(X_t)$;
\item the \textbf{variance function} $\sigma^2(t) = \Var(X_t) = E[(X_t - \mu(t))^2]$;
\item the \textbf{autocovariance function} $\gamma(t_1, t_2) = E[(X_{t_1} - \mu(t_1))(X_{t_2} - \mu(t_2))] = \Cov(X_{t_1}, X_{t_2})$.
\end{itemize}
The variance function is the special case $\sigma^2(t) = \gamma(t, t)$.

\subsection{Strict stationarity}
A process is \emph{first-order stationary} if the distribution of $X_t$ is the same for every $t$: $F(x_{t_1}) = F(x_{t_1+k})$ for all $t_1$, $k$. It is
\emph{second-order stationary in distribution} if $F(x_{t_1}, x_{t_2}) = F(x_{t_1+k}, x_{t_2+k})$ for all $t_1, t_2, k$, and \emph{$n$th-order stationary} if
\[
F(x_{t_1}, x_{t_2}, \dots, x_{t_n}) = F(x_{t_1+k}, x_{t_2+k}, \dots, x_{t_n+k})
\]
for all $t_1, \dots, t_n$ and $k$.

\begin{defn}
A process is \textbf{strictly stationary} if it is $n$th-order stationary for every $n = 1, 2, \dots$: the joint distribution of
$X_{t_1}, \dots, X_{t_n}$ is the same as that of $X_{t_1+k}, \dots, X_{t_n+k}$ for every $t_1, \dots, t_n$ and every shift $k$.
\end{defn}

In words, shifting the time origin by any amount $k$ has no effect on the distributions.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:strict-moments}
If $\{X_t\}$ is strictly stationary with finite variance, then
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item $\mu(t) = \mu$ and $\sigma^2(t) = \sigma^2$ are constants;
\item $\gamma(t_1, t_2)$ depends only on the lag $t_2 - t_1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
(i) With $n = 1$, every $X_t$ has the same distribution, hence the same mean and variance. (ii) With $n = 2$, $(X_{t_1}, X_{t_2})$ has
the same joint distribution as $(X_{t_1+k}, X_{t_2+k})$ for every $k$. Taking $k = -t_1$, it is the same as that of $(X_0, X_{t_2-t_1})$, so its
covariance is a function of $t_2 - t_1$ alone.
\end{proof}

So for a stationary process we write $\gamma(k) = \Cov(X_t, X_{t+k})$, the \textbf{autocovariance at lag $k$}, and
\[
\rho(k) = \frac{\gamma(k)}{\gamma(0)} ,
\]
the \textbf{autocorrelation function} (ac.f.), which measures the correlation between $X_t$ and $X_{t+k}$. Here $\gamma(0) = \sigma^2$.

\subsection{Second-order stationarity}
Strict stationarity is a strong condition, and hard to check from data. In practice a weaker one is used.

\begin{defn}
A process is \textbf{second-order stationary} (or \textbf{weakly stationary}) if its mean is constant and its
autocovariance depends only on the lag:
\[
E(X_t) = \mu , \qquad \Cov(X_t, X_{t+k}) = \gamma(k) \quad\text{for all } t \text{ and } k ,
\]
with $\mu$ and $\gamma(0)$ finite.
\end{defn}

Taking $k = 0$, the variance $\gamma(0) = \sigma^2$ is also constant. Throughout these notes ``stationary'' means second-order
stationary unless stated otherwise. The two kinds are related as follows.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:strict-weak}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item A strictly stationary process with finite variance is second-order stationary.
\item A second-order stationary process need not be strictly stationary.
\item A second-order stationary process is strictly stationary if, for every choice of times, its
variables have a multivariate normal joint distribution (a \emph{Gaussian process}).
\end{enumerate}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
(i) is Proposition~\ref{prop:strict-moments}. (ii) Let the $X_t$ be independent, with $X_t \sim N(0, 1)$ for odd $t$ and $X_t$ uniform on $(-\sqrt3, \sqrt3)$
for even $t$. Both distributions have mean $0$ and variance $1$, and different variables are uncorrelated, so $\mu = 0$ and
$\gamma(k) = 0$ for $k \ne 0$, $\gamma(0) = 1$: the process is second-order stationary. But $X_1$ and $X_2$ have different distributions, so it is not
first-order stationary. (iii) A multivariate normal distribution is determined by its mean vector and covariance
matrix. For a weakly stationary process, the mean vector of $(X_{t_1+k}, \dots, X_{t_n+k})$ is $(\mu, \dots, \mu)$ and its covariance matrix has entries
$\gamma(t_j - t_i)$, neither depending on $k$, so the joint distribution does not depend on $k$.
\end{proof}

\begin{exa}\label{exa:harmonic}
Let $X_t = A\cos(\lambda t) + B\sin(\lambda t)$, where $\lambda$ is a fixed frequency and $A$, $B$ are uncorrelated random variables with mean $0$
and variance $\sigma^2$. Show that $\{X_t\}$ is second-order stationary and find its ac.f.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
$E(X_t) = \cos(\lambda t)E(A) + \sin(\lambda t)E(B) = 0$. Since $\Cov(A, B) = 0$,
\begin{align*}
\Cov(X_t, X_{t+k}) &= \cos(\lambda t)\cos(\lambda(t + k))\Var(A) \\
&\qquad + \sin(\lambda t)\sin(\lambda(t + k))\Var(B) \\
&= \sigma^2\bigl[\cos(\lambda t)\cos(\lambda t + \lambda k) + \sin(\lambda t)\sin(\lambda t + \lambda k)\bigr] \\
&= \sigma^2\cos(\lambda k) ,
\end{align*}
by the identity $\cos a\cos b + \sin a\sin b = \cos(b - a)$. The mean is constant and the covariance depends only on $k$, so the
process is stationary, with $\gamma(0) = \sigma^2$ and $\rho(k) = \cos(\lambda k)$. Each realisation is a sinusoid with a random amplitude and
phase, and its correlations never die away: values a whole period apart are perfectly correlated. Processes
built from such components are the basis of the frequency-domain view in Section~\ref{sec:frequency}.
\end{solution}

\subsection{Properties of the autocorrelation function}
Suppose $\{X_t\}$ is stationary with mean $\mu$, variance $\sigma^2$, autocovariance $\gamma(k)$ and ac.f. $\rho(k)$. Then $\rho(k) = \gamma(k)/\gamma(0) = \gamma(k)/\sigma^2$,
and the following properties hold.

\begin{thm}\label{thm:acf-properties}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item $\rho(0) = 1$.
\item The ac.f. is an even function: $\rho(-k) = \rho(k)$, and likewise $\gamma(-k) = \gamma(k)$.
\item $|\rho(k)| \le 1$ for every $k$.
\item $\gamma$ is \textbf{non-negative definite}: for any times $t_1, \dots, t_n$ and real numbers $a_1, \dots, a_n$,
\[
\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n a_ia_j\gamma(t_i - t_j) \ge 0 .
\]
Equivalently, every matrix $\boldsymbol\Gamma_n$ with $(i, j)$ entry $\gamma(i - j)$ is non-negative definite.
\end{enumerate}
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
(i) $\rho(0) = \gamma(0)/\gamma(0) = 1$.

(ii) By stationarity, $\gamma(-k) = \Cov(X_t, X_{t-k}) = \Cov(X_{t-k}, X_t)$, and shifting time by $k$ gives $\Cov(X_t, X_{t+k}) = \gamma(k)$.

(iii) For either sign, $0 \le \Var(X_t \pm X_{t+k}) = \sigma^2 + \sigma^2 \pm 2\gamma(k) = 2\sigma^2(1 \pm \rho(k))$, so $1 + \rho(k) \ge 0$ and $1 - \rho(k) \ge 0$.

(iv) The left-hand side is $\Var\bigl(\sum_ia_iX_{t_i}\bigr)$, since
$\Var(\sum_ia_iX_{t_i}) = \sum_i\sum_ja_ia_j\Cov(X_{t_i}, X_{t_j}) = \sum_i\sum_ja_ia_j\gamma(t_j - t_i)$, and $\gamma(t_j - t_i) = \gamma(t_i - t_j)$ by (ii). A variance is never negative.
\end{proof}

Property (iv) is a real restriction. Not every even function with $\rho(0) = 1$ and $|\rho(k)| \le 1$ is the ac.f. of some
stationary process, and (iv) is the test. Its most usable form is that all the matrices $\mathbf{R}_n = \boldsymbol\Gamma_n/\sigma^2$, with entries
$\rho(i - j)$, must have non-negative determinants.

Note also that the ac.f. does not determine the process. Different processes can have the same ac.f.; an
example appears in Section~\ref{sec:invertibility}, on invertibility. And a process with a trend or with a changing variance has
no ac.f. in this sense at all, which is why trend and seasonality are removed before the correlogram is used to
choose a model.

\begin{exa}\label{exa:not-acf}
Can $\rho(0) = 1$, $\rho(\pm1) = 0.8$ and $\rho(k) = 0$ for $|k| \ge 2$ be the autocorrelation function of a stationary process?
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
The function is even, with $\rho(0) = 1$ and $|\rho(k)| \le 1$, so (i)--(iii) of Theorem~\ref{thm:acf-properties} hold. Check (iv) with $n = 3$:
\begin{align*}
\det\mathbf{R}_3 &= \det\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.8 & 0 \\ 0.8 & 1 & 0.8 \\ 0 & 0.8 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \\
&= 1(1 - 0.64) - 0.8(0.8 - 0) = 0.36 - 0.64 = -0.28 < 0 .
\end{align*}
So $\mathbf{R}_3$ has a negative eigenvalue, and there are weights $a_1, a_2, a_3$ for which $\Var(a_1X_1 + a_2X_2 + a_3X_3)$ would be negative.
For instance $\mathbf{a} = (1, -1, 1)$ gives $\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{R}_3\mathbf{a} = 3 - 4(0.8) = -0.2$. The function is not an ac.f. Intuitively, if $X_1$ and $X_2$ are
strongly correlated, and $X_2$ and $X_3$ are too, then $X_1$ and $X_3$ cannot be uncorrelated. (Section~\ref{sec:ma} shows that a
function of this shape is an ac.f. exactly when $|\rho(1)| \le \frac12$.)
\end{solution}

\section{Purely random process}
\subsection{Definition and properties}
\begin{defn}
A discrete process $\{Z_t\}$ is \textbf{purely random} if it consists of a sequence of mutually independent
random variables, all with the same distribution, with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma_Z^2$.
\end{defn}

\begin{prop}\label{prop:white-noise}
A purely random process is strictly stationary, with
\[
\gamma(k) = \begin{cases} \sigma_Z^2 , & k = 0 , \\ 0 , & k = \pm1, \pm2, \dots , \end{cases}
\qquad
\rho(k) = \begin{cases} 1 , & k = 0 , \\ 0 , & k = \pm1, \pm2, \dots \end{cases}
\]
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
For any times $t_1, \dots, t_n$, the joint distribution of $Z_{t_1}, \dots, Z_{t_n}$ is the product of $n$ copies of the
common distribution, so it is unchanged by a shift. Independent variables are uncorrelated, so $\gamma(k) = 0$ for $k \ne 0$, and $\gamma(0) = \sigma_Z^2$.
\end{proof}

A purely random process is often called \textbf{white noise}; strictly, ``white noise'' only requires the
variables to be uncorrelated with constant mean and variance, which is enough for most of the theory. If the
$Z_t$ are also normal, independence and uncorrelatedness coincide, and the process is \emph{Gaussian white
noise}. White noise has no memory at all: knowing past values does not help to predict future ones. It is
important not for itself but as the building block from which the moving average, autoregressive and ARMA
processes of the following sections are constructed, usually with $\mu = 0$. Its correlogram is the benchmark
for a series with no structure: the sample autocorrelations are approximately $N(0, 1/n)$, as noted in
Section~\ref{ssec:sample-acf}.

\section{Random walk}\label{sec:random-walk}
\subsection{Definition and properties}
\begin{defn}
Let $\{Z_t\}$ be a purely random process with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma_Z^2$. The process $\{X_t\}$ is a \textbf{random walk} if
\[
X_t = X_{t-1} + Z_t .
\]
\end{defn}

The process is usually started at $X_0 = 0$, so that $X_1 = Z_1$ and, by repeated substitution,
\[
X_t = Z_1 + Z_2 + \dots + Z_t = \sum_{i=1}^tZ_i .
\]
When $\mu \ne 0$ the process is a \emph{random walk with drift}. Random walks are standard models for share prices
and exchange rates: tomorrow's price is today's plus an unpredictable change.

\begin{thm}\label{thm:random-walk}
For the random walk started at $X_0 = 0$, and $1 \le s \le t$,
\begin{gather*}
E(X_t) = t\mu , \qquad \Var(X_t) = t\sigma_Z^2 , \\
\Cov(X_s, X_t) = s\sigma_Z^2 , \qquad \Corr(X_s, X_t) = \sqrt{s/t} .
\end{gather*}
The process is not stationary, but its first difference $\nabla X_t = X_t - X_{t-1} = Z_t$ is purely random.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
$E(X_t) = \sum_{i=1}^tE(Z_i) = t\mu$, and since the $Z_i$ are independent, $\Var(X_t) = \sum_{i=1}^t\Var(Z_i) = t\sigma_Z^2$. For $s \le t$,
\begin{align*}
\Cov(X_s, X_t) &= \Cov\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^sZ_i, \ \sum_{j=1}^tZ_j\Bigr) = \sum_{i=1}^s\sum_{j=1}^t\Cov(Z_i, Z_j) \\
&= \sum_{i=1}^s\Var(Z_i) = s\sigma_Z^2 ,
\end{align*}
since only the terms with $i = j \le s$ are non-zero. Dividing by $\sqrt{s\sigma_Z^2 \cdot t\sigma_Z^2}$ gives the correlation. The variance grows with $t$ (and
the mean too, unless $\mu = 0$), so the process is not stationary; and $\nabla X_t = Z_t$ by definition.
\end{proof}

The correlation $\sqrt{s/t}$ depends on $s$ and $t$ separately, not just on their difference: $X_{10}$ and $X_{20}$ have correlation
$\sqrt{0.5} = 0.71$, while $X_{100}$ and $X_{110}$, also ten steps apart, have $\sqrt{100/110} = 0.95$. The paths wander further and
further from their starting point (Figure~\ref{fig:random-walk}), and the correlogram of an observed random walk
dies away only very slowly, like that of a series with a trend. Differencing turns it back into white noise:
this is the simplest example of the integrated models of Section~\ref{sec:integrated}.

\begin{exa}\label{exa:drift}
A share price is modelled as a random walk with drift, $X_t = X_{t-1} + Z_t$, with $X_0 = 0$ measured from today's price and
daily changes $Z_t \sim N(0.5, 4)$, independent. Find the mean and variance of the price change over ten days, the
probability that it exceeds $10$, and the correlation between the changes over ten and twenty days.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:random-walk}, $E(X_{10}) = 10(0.5) = 5$ and $\Var(X_{10}) = 10(4) = 40$, so $\operatorname{sd}(X_{10}) = 6.32$. As a sum of independent
normal variables, $X_{10}$ is normal, and
\[
P(X_{10} > 10) = P\Bigl(Z > \frac{10 - 5}{6.32}\Bigr) = P(Z > 0.79) = 0.215 .
\]
The correlation between $X_{10}$ and $X_{20}$ is $\sqrt{10/20} = 0.71$: half of the twenty-day change is the ten-day change, so the
two are strongly related. The drift adds $0.5$ a day on average, but after ten days its effect, $5$, is smaller than one
standard deviation of the noise, which is why the random walk is so hard to forecast.
\end{solution}

\subsection{Practice problems}
\begin{problem}\label{prob:ts-ma1-stationary}
Let $\{Z_t\}$ be white noise with mean $0$ and variance $\sigma_Z^2$, and $X_t = Z_t + 0.5Z_{t-1}$. Find $E(X_t)$, $\gamma(k)$ and $\rho(k)$, and show
that the process is stationary.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$E(X_t) = 0$. $\gamma(0) = \Var(Z_t) + 0.25\Var(Z_{t-1}) = 1.25\sigma_Z^2$. For $k = 1$, $\Cov(Z_t + 0.5Z_{t-1}, Z_{t+1} + 0.5Z_t) = 0.5\Var(Z_t) = 0.5\sigma_Z^2$. For $|k| \ge 2$ the
two variables involve no common $Z$, so $\gamma(k) = 0$. None of this depends on $t$, so the process is stationary, with
$\rho(1) = 0.5/1.25 = 0.4$ and $\rho(k) = 0$ for $|k| \ge 2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}\label{prob:ts-sign}
Let $Z$ be a single random variable with mean $0$ and variance $\sigma^2$, and $X_t = (-1)^tZ$. Is $\{X_t\}$ stationary? What do its
realisations look like?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$E(X_t) = 0$ and $\Cov(X_t, X_{t+k}) = (-1)^{2t+k}\Var(Z) = (-1)^k\sigma^2$, which depends only on $k$. So it is stationary, with $\rho(k) = (-1)^k$. Each
realisation simply alternates between $Z$ and $-Z$: one random number decides the whole path. Stationarity
does not mean the series is random-looking, only that its moments do not change with time.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}\label{prob:ts-rw-increment}
For a random walk with drift $\mu$ and variance $\sigma_Z^2$, show that $X_t - X_{t-k}$ has mean $k\mu$ and variance $k\sigma_Z^2$ for every $t$. Is $\{X_t - X_{t-k}\}$,
for fixed $k$, a stationary process?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$X_t - X_{t-k} = Z_{t-k+1} + \dots + Z_t$, a sum of $k$ independent terms, so its mean is $k\mu$ and its variance $k\sigma_Z^2$, whatever $t$. As a moving sum
of white noise it is stationary: $\Cov(X_t - X_{t-k}, X_{t+j} - X_{t+j-k})$ is $\sigma_Z^2$ times the number of $Z$'s the two sums share, which is $\max(k - |j|, 0)$,
a function of $j$ alone.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}\label{prob:ts-not-acf}
Let $\rho(\pm1) = 0.6$, $\rho(0) = 1$ and $\rho(k) = 0$ otherwise. The determinants $D_n = \det\mathbf{R}_n$ satisfy $D_n = D_{n-1} - 0.36D_{n-2}$, with $D_1 = 1$ and
$D_2 = 0.64$. Show that $D_3$ and $D_4$ are positive but $D_5$ is negative, and conclude.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Expanding $\det\mathbf{R}_n$ along its first row gives the recursion, since $\mathbf{R}_n$ is tridiagonal. Then
$D_3 = 0.64 - 0.36 = 0.28$, $D_4 = 0.28 - 0.36(0.64) = 0.0496$ and $D_5 = 0.0496 - 0.36(0.28) = -0.0512 < 0$. So $\mathbf{R}_5$ is not non-negative definite, and the
function is not an ac.f., even though the $3 \times 3$ and $4 \times 4$ checks pass. Checking a few small matrices is not enough in
general; for this shape of function, the exact condition is $|\rho(1)| \le \frac12$, and $0.6$ exceeds it.
\end{solution}

\section{Moving average processes}\label{sec:ma}
\subsection{Definition and moments}
\begin{defn}
Let $\{Z_t\}$ be a purely random process with mean $0$ and variance $\sigma_Z^2$. The process $\{X_t\}$ is a \textbf{moving average
process of order $q$}, written MA($q$), if
\[
X_t = \beta_0Z_t + \beta_1Z_{t-1} + \beta_2Z_{t-2} + \dots + \beta_qZ_{t-q} ,
\]
where the $\beta_j$ are constants. We normally take $\beta_0 = 1$ (this fixes the scale of $Z_t$).
\end{defn}

Each $X_t$ is a weighted sum of the current shock and the $q$ previous ones, so a shock affects the series for
$q + 1$ periods and then is forgotten. If the series has mean $\mu$, the model is $X_t = \mu + \beta_0Z_t + \dots + \beta_qZ_{t-q}$, which has the
same autocovariances.

\begin{thm}\label{thm:ma-acv}
The MA($q$) process has mean $0$ and autocovariance function
\[
\gamma(k) = \begin{cases} \sigma_Z^2\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{q-k}\beta_i\beta_{i+k} , & k = 0, 1, \dots, q , \\[2ex] 0 , & k > q , \end{cases}
\qquad \gamma(-k) = \gamma(k) .
\]
In particular $\Var(X_t) = \sigma_Z^2\sum_{i=0}^q\beta_i^2$. So the process is second-order stationary for every choice of the $\beta_i$, and its
ac.f. is
\[
\rho(k) = \frac{\sum_{i=0}^{q-k}\beta_i\beta_{i+k}}{\sum_{i=0}^q\beta_i^2} \quad (k = 1, \dots, q) , \qquad \rho(k) = 0 \quad (k > q) .
\]
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
$E(X_t) = \sum_i\beta_iE(Z_{t-i}) = 0$. For $k \ge 0$, since $X_t = \sum_i\beta_iZ_{t-i}$ and $X_{t+k} = \sum_j\beta_jZ_{t+k-j}$, covariance is
bilinear and
\[
\Cov(X_t, X_{t+k}) = \sum_{i=0}^q\sum_{j=0}^q\beta_i\beta_j\Cov(Z_{t-i}, Z_{t+k-j}) .
\]
Since $\Cov(Z_s, Z_u) = \sigma_Z^2$ if $s = u$ and $0$ otherwise, only the terms with $t - i = t + k - j$, that is $j = i + k$, survive. Such a $j$ lies in
$0, \dots, q$ only when $i \le q - k$, which gives the sum; if $k > q$ there are no such terms. Neither the mean nor $\gamma(k)$ depends on
$t$, so the process is stationary, and evenness is Theorem~\ref{thm:acf-properties}(ii).
\end{proof}

The key feature is that the ac.f. of an MA($q$) process \emph{cuts off} after lag $q$: it is exactly zero beyond. Two
observations more than $q$ apart share no shock. A sample correlogram that is clearly non-zero up to some lag
and negligible afterwards therefore suggests a moving average model of that order.

\begin{exa}\label{exa:ma2}
Find the ac.f. of the MA(2) process $X_t = Z_t + 0.5Z_{t-1} - 0.3Z_{t-2}$.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
Here $\beta_0 = 1$, $\beta_1 = 0.5$, $\beta_2 = -0.3$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:ma-acv},
\begin{align*}
\gamma(0) &= \sigma_Z^2(1 + 0.25 + 0.09) = 1.34\sigma_Z^2 , \\
\gamma(1) &= \sigma_Z^2(\beta_0\beta_1 + \beta_1\beta_2) = \sigma_Z^2(0.5 - 0.15) = 0.35\sigma_Z^2 , \\
\gamma(2) &= \sigma_Z^2\beta_0\beta_2 = -0.3\sigma_Z^2 ,
\end{align*}
and $\gamma(k) = 0$ for $k \ge 3$. So $\rho(1) = 0.35/1.34 = 0.261$, $\rho(2) = -0.3/1.34 = -0.224$, and $\rho(k) = 0$ for $k \ge 3$.
\end{solution}

\subsection{The first-order moving average}
The simplest case is the MA(1) process $X_t = Z_t + \beta Z_{t-1}$, for which Theorem~\ref{thm:ma-acv} gives $\gamma(0) = (1 + \beta^2)\sigma_Z^2$,
$\gamma(1) = \beta\sigma_Z^2$, and
\[
\rho(k) = \begin{cases} 1 , & k = 0 , \\ \dfrac{\beta}{1 + \beta^2} , & k = \pm1 , \\ 0 , & |k| \ge 2 . \end{cases}
\]
With $\beta > 0$ neighbouring values tend to be alike; with $\beta < 0$ they tend to alternate (Figure~\ref{fig:ma1}).

\begin{thm}\label{thm:ma1-bound}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item For the MA(1) process, $|\rho(1)| \le \frac12$, with equality only when $\beta = \pm1$.
\item Conversely, a function with $\rho(0) = 1$, $\rho(\pm1) = r$ and $\rho(k) = 0$ for $|k| \ge 2$ is the ac.f. of a stationary process if and only if
$|r| \le \frac12$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
(i) $1 + \beta^2 - 2|\beta| = (1 - |\beta|)^2 \ge 0$, so $|\beta| \le \frac12(1 + \beta^2)$ and $|\rho(1)| = |\beta|/(1 + \beta^2) \le \frac12$, with equality exactly when $|\beta| = 1$.

(ii) If $|r| \le \frac12$ and $r \ne 0$, the equation $\beta/(1 + \beta^2) = r$, that is $r\beta^2 - \beta + r = 0$, has the real root
$\beta = (1 - \sqrt{1 - 4r^2})/(2r)$, since $1 - 4r^2 \ge 0$; the MA(1) process with this $\beta$ has the required ac.f. (For $r = 0$, take white
noise.) Conversely, suppose the function is an ac.f. By Theorem~\ref{thm:acf-properties}(iv), for any $n$ and weights $a_1, \dots, a_n$,
\[
0 \le \sum_i\sum_ja_ia_j\rho(i - j) = \sum_ia_i^2 + 2r\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_ia_{i+1} .
\]
Taking $a_i = (-1)^i$ gives $0 \le n - 2r(n - 1)$, so $r \le \frac{n}{2(n - 1)}$ for every $n$, and letting $n \to \infty$, $r \le \frac12$. Taking all $a_i = 1$ gives
$0 \le n + 2r(n - 1)$, and in the same way $r \ge -\frac12$.
\end{proof}

So no MA(1) process, and indeed no process whose correlations stop after lag~1, can have a lag-one
autocorrelation above $\frac12$ in size. This explains Example~\ref{exa:not-acf} and Problem~\ref{prob:ts-not-acf}: there $\rho(1) = 0.8$ and
$0.6$, both too large.

models have exactly the same ac.f. The next section explains which one to choose: $\hat\beta = 0.5$.

\section{Invertibility}\label{sec:invertibility}
\subsection{The identification problem}
Example~\ref{exa:ma1-moments} is an instance of a general fact. Consider the two MA(1) processes
\[
\text{A}: \ X_t = Z_t + \beta Z_{t-1} , \qquad\qquad \text{B}: \ X_t = Z_t + \frac1\beta Z_{t-1} .
\]
They have the same ac.f., since
\[
\frac{1/\beta}{1 + 1/\beta^2} = \frac{\beta}{\beta^2 + 1} .
\]
Thus a moving average process cannot be identified uniquely from its ac.f. To choose between A and B,
express each shock in terms of the observations. From A, $Z_t = X_t - \beta Z_{t-1}$, and substituting repeatedly for the
earlier $Z$'s,
\[
\text{A}: \ Z_t = X_t - \beta X_{t-1} + \beta^2X_{t-2} - \beta^3X_{t-3} + \dots ,
\]
and similarly
\[
\text{B}: \ Z_t = X_t - \frac1\beta X_{t-1} + \frac1{\beta^2}X_{t-2} - \frac1{\beta^3}X_{t-3} + \dots .
\]
If $|\beta| < 1$ the series for A converges, while that for B diverges, since its weights grow. So if $|\beta| < 1$, model A is
\emph{invertible} and model B is not. Imposing invertibility ensures that there is a unique MA process for a
given ac.f.

\begin{defn}
A process $\{X_t\}$ is \textbf{invertible} if each shock $Z_t$ can be written as a convergent weighted
sum of the present and past observations, $Z_t = \sum_{j=0}^\infty\pi_jX_{t-j}$ with $\sum_j|\pi_j| < \infty$.
\end{defn}

\begin{prop}\label{prop:ma1-invert}
If $|\beta| < 1$, the MA(1) process $X_t = Z_t + \beta Z_{t-1}$ is invertible: with $S_n = \sum_{j=0}^n(-\beta)^jX_{t-j}$,
\[
E\bigl[(Z_t - S_n)^2\bigr] = \beta^{2(n+1)}\sigma_Z^2 \to 0 \quad\text{as } n \to \infty .
\]
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Substituting $X_{t-j} = Z_{t-j} + \beta Z_{t-j-1}$, the sum telescopes:
\[
S_n = \sum_{j=0}^n(-\beta)^jZ_{t-j} + \sum_{j=0}^n(-\beta)^j\beta Z_{t-j-1} = Z_t - (-\beta)^{n+1}Z_{t-n-1} ,
\]
because the second sum is minus the first sum without its $j = 0$ term, plus the extra term $-(-\beta)^{n+1}Z_{t-n-1}$. So $Z_t - S_n = (-\beta)^{n+1}Z_{t-n-1}$, whose
mean square is $\beta^{2(n+1)}\sigma_Z^2$, which tends to $0$ when $|\beta| < 1$. The weights $\pi_j = (-\beta)^j$ have $\sum|\beta|^j = 1/(1 - |\beta|) < \infty$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The backward shift operator}
The invertibility condition for a general MA($q$) is stated with the \textbf{backward shift operator} $B$, defined by
\[
BX_t = X_{t-1} , \qquad B^jX_t = X_{t-j} \quad\text{for all } j .
\]
So $B^2X_t = X_{t-2}$, and the MA($q$) process can be written
\[
X_t = (\beta_0 + \beta_1B + \beta_2B^2 + \dots + \beta_qB^q)Z_t = \theta(B)Z_t ,
\]
where $\theta(B) = \beta_0 + \beta_1B + \dots + \beta_qB^q$ is a polynomial of order $q$ in $B$. Operators like this can be added, multiplied and
factorised like ordinary polynomials.

\begin{thm}\label{thm:ma-invertible}
The MA($q$) process $X_t = \theta(B)Z_t$ is invertible if all the roots of the equation
\[
\theta(B) = \beta_0 + \beta_1B + \beta_2B^2 + \dots + \beta_qB^q = 0
\]
lie outside the unit circle, that is, have modulus greater than $1$.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
Let $r_1, \dots, r_q$ be the roots, possibly complex. With $\beta_0 = 1$, $\theta(B) = (1 - B/r_1)(1 - B/r_2)\cdots(1 - B/r_q)$, since both sides are
polynomials of degree $q$ with the same roots and the same constant term $1$. For each factor, $|1/r_j| < 1$, so as in
Proposition~\ref{prop:ma1-invert} (with $\beta = -1/r_j$), $(1 - B/r_j)^{-1} = \sum_{m=0}^\infty(B/r_j)^m$ is an operator with absolutely summable weights.
Applying the $q$ inverses in turn to $X_t = \theta(B)Z_t$ gives $Z_t = \pi(B)X_t$, where $\pi(B)$, a product of $q$ series with absolutely
summable weights, again has absolutely summable weights.
\end{proof}

For the MA(1), $\theta(B) = 1 + \beta B$, with the root $B = -1/\beta$, which lies outside the unit circle exactly when $|\beta| < 1$, in
agreement with Proposition~\ref{prop:ma1-invert}. Invertibility matters for two reasons: it makes the model unique for
a given ac.f., and it allows the past shocks to be recovered from the data, which is how forecasts are
computed (Section~\ref{sec:forecasting}).

\begin{exa}\label{exa:ma2-invert}
Determine whether each MA(2) process is invertible:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item $X_t = Z_t - 1.3Z_{t-1} + 0.4Z_{t-2}$;
\item $X_t = Z_t + 2.5Z_{t-1} + Z_{t-2}$.
\end{enumerate}
For any that is not, find an invertible MA(2) process with the same autocovariance function.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
(a) $\theta(B) = 1 - 1.3B + 0.4B^2 = (1 - 0.5B)(1 - 0.8B)$, with roots $B = 2$ and $B = 1.25$. Both exceed $1$ in modulus, so the process is
invertible.

(b) $\theta(B) = 1 + 2.5B + B^2 = (1 + 0.5B)(1 + 2B)$, with roots $B = -2$ and $B = -0.5$. The second lies inside the unit circle, so
the process is not invertible.

To find an equivalent invertible process, replace the offending factor $(1 + 2B)$ by $(1 + 0.5B)$, its root reflected
from $-0.5$ to $-2$, and multiply the noise variance by $2^2 = 4$, as in the MA(1) case. The candidate is
\[
X_t = (1 + 0.5B)^2Z_t^* = Z_t^* + Z_{t-1}^* + 0.25Z_{t-2}^* , \qquad \Var(Z_t^*) = 4\sigma_Z^2 .
\]
Check with Theorem~\ref{thm:ma-acv}. The original has $\gamma(0) = (1 + 6.25 + 1)\sigma_Z^2 = 8.25\sigma_Z^2$, $\gamma(1) = (2.5 + 2.5)\sigma_Z^2 = 5\sigma_Z^2$,
$\gamma(2) = \sigma_Z^2$. The new one has $\gamma(0) = 4\sigma_Z^2(1 + 1 + 0.0625) = 8.25\sigma_Z^2$, $\gamma(1) = 4\sigma_Z^2(1 + 0.25) = 5\sigma_Z^2$, $\gamma(2) = 4\sigma_Z^2(0.25) = \sigma_Z^2$. The
autocovariances agree, and both roots of $(1 + 0.5B)^2$ equal $-2$, outside the unit circle.
\end{solution}

\subsection{Practice problems}
\begin{problem}\label{prob:ma2-acf}
For $X_t = Z_t + 0.6Z_{t-1} + 0.2Z_{t-2}$, find the ac.f. and decide whether the process is invertible.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$\gamma(0) = (1 + 0.36 + 0.04)\sigma_Z^2 = 1.40\sigma_Z^2$, $\gamma(1) = (0.6 + 0.6 \times 0.2)\sigma_Z^2 = 0.72\sigma_Z^2$, $\gamma(2) = 0.2\sigma_Z^2$. So $\rho(1) = 0.514$, $\rho(2) = 0.143$,
and $\rho(k) = 0$ for $k \ge 3$. The equation $1 + 0.6B + 0.2B^2 = 0$ has discriminant $0.36 - 0.8 < 0$, so its roots are complex conjugates;
their product is $1/0.2 = 5$, so each has modulus $\sqrt5 > 1$. The process is invertible.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}\label{prob:ma1-negative}
A series believed to be MA(1) has $r_1 = -0.3$. Find the two values of $\beta$ that match it, and say which should be used.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$-0.3\beta^2 - \beta - 0.3 = 0$ gives $\beta = (1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 0.36})/(-0.6) = (1 \pm 0.8)/(-0.6)$, so $\beta = -\frac13$ or $\beta = -3$. They are
reciprocals, as they must be. Use the invertible value $\hat\beta = -0.333$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}\label{prob:ma1-flip}
Find the invertible MA(1) process with the same autocovariance function as $X_t = Z_t + 4Z_{t-1}$, $\Var(Z_t) = 1$.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
The original has $\gamma(0) = 1 + 16 = 17$ and $\gamma(1) = 4$. The candidate $X_t = Z_t^* + 0.25Z_{t-1}^*$ with $\Var(Z_t^*) = 16$ has
$\gamma(0) = 16(1 + 0.0625) = 17$ and $\gamma(1) = 16(0.25) = 4$. They agree, and $|0.25| < 1$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}\label{prob:ma-cutoff}
A sample correlogram from $n = 200$ observations has $r_1 = 0.45$, $r_2 = 0.31$, and $|r_k| < 0.1$ for $3 \le k \le 20$. Which MA order does this
suggest, and why could the model not be MA(1)?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
The band $\pm2/\sqrt{200} = \pm0.14$ contains every $r_k$ from lag $3$ on, while $r_1$ and $r_2$ lie well outside it: the correlogram cuts off after
lag~$2$, suggesting MA(2). An MA(1) would need $\rho(2) = 0$, but $r_2 = 0.31$ is more than twice the band. (By itself, $r_1 = 0.45$ would be possible for
an MA(1), since it is below $\frac12$.)
\end{solution}

\section{Autoregressive processes}\label{sec:ar}
\begin{defn}
Let $\{Z_t\}$ be a purely random process with mean $0$ and variance $\sigma_Z^2$. The process $\{X_t\}$ is an \textbf{autoregressive
process of order $p$}, written AR($p$), if
\[
X_t = \alpha_1X_{t-1} + \alpha_2X_{t-2} + \dots + \alpha_pX_{t-p} + Z_t .
\]
\end{defn}
The name says what the model is: a multiple regression of $X_t$ on its own past values rather than on
separate regressors, with $Z_t$ as the error. Each value is a fraction of the recent values plus a new shock, so
a shock is remembered for ever, though with fading weight.

\subsection{The first-order process}
For $p = 1$, $X_t = \alpha X_{t-1} + Z_t$. Since also $X_{t-1} = \alpha X_{t-2} + Z_{t-1}$, substituting repeatedly gives
\begin{align*}
X_t &= \alpha(\alpha X_{t-2} + Z_{t-1}) + Z_t = \alpha^2X_{t-2} + \alpha Z_{t-1} + Z_t \\
&= \alpha^2(\alpha X_{t-3} + Z_{t-2}) + \alpha Z_{t-1} + Z_t = \alpha^3X_{t-3} + \alpha^2Z_{t-2} + \alpha Z_{t-1} + Z_t ,
\end{align*}
and so on. So $X_t$ can be expressed in terms of the $Z$'s alone, as an MA process of infinite order,
\[
X_t = Z_t + \alpha Z_{t-1} + \alpha^2Z_{t-2} + \alpha^3Z_{t-3} + \dots ,
\]
provided $|\alpha| < 1$. This is the \emph{duality} between AR and MA processes: an AR(1) is an MA($\infty$), just as an
invertible MA(1) is an AR($\infty$) (Section~\ref{sec:invertibility}). With the backward shift operator, $X_t = \alpha X_{t-1} + Z_t$ reads
$(1 - \alpha B)X_t = Z_t$, and formally
\[
X_t = \frac{Z_t}{1 - \alpha B} = (1 + \alpha B + \alpha^2B^2 + \dots)Z_t = Z_t + \alpha Z_{t-1} + \alpha^2Z_{t-2} + \dots ,
\]
the same expansion, obtained by treating $(1 - \alpha B)^{-1}$ as a geometric series.

\begin{thm}\label{thm:ar1}
Suppose $|\alpha| < 1$, and let $X_t = \sum_{j=0}^\infty\alpha^jZ_{t-j}$. Then $\{X_t\}$ satisfies $X_t = \alpha X_{t-1} + Z_t$, it is stationary, and
\begin{gather*}
E(X_t) = 0 , \qquad \sigma_X^2 = \Var(X_t) = \frac{\sigma_Z^2}{1 - \alpha^2} , \\
\gamma(k) = \alpha^{|k|}\sigma_X^2 , \qquad \rho(k) = \alpha^{|k|} .
\end{gather*}
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
The series converges in mean square: the tail $\sum_{j>n}\alpha^jZ_{t-j}$ has variance $\sigma_Z^2\sum_{j>n}\alpha^{2j} = \sigma_Z^2\alpha^{2(n+1)}/(1 - \alpha^2) \to 0$. Then
$\alpha X_{t-1} + Z_t = \sum_{j\ge0}\alpha^{j+1}Z_{t-1-j} + Z_t = \sum_{j\ge0}\alpha^jZ_{t-j} = X_t$. The mean is $\sum_j\alpha^jE(Z_{t-j}) = 0$, and since the $Z$'s are
uncorrelated,
\[
\Var(X_t) = \sigma_Z^2(1 + \alpha^2 + \alpha^4 + \dots) = \frac{\sigma_Z^2}{1 - \alpha^2} .
\]
For $k \ge 0$, $X_t$ and $X_{t+k} = \sum_i\alpha^iZ_{t+k-i}$ share the shocks $Z_{t-j}$, $j \ge 0$, which appear with weights $\alpha^j$ and $\alpha^{j+k}$, so
\[
\gamma(k) = \sigma_Z^2\sum_{j=0}^\infty\alpha^j\alpha^{j+k} = \alpha^k\frac{\sigma_Z^2}{1 - \alpha^2} = \alpha^k\sigma_X^2 .
\]
None of this depends on $t$, so the process is stationary; dividing by $\gamma(0)$ gives $\rho(k) = \alpha^k$, and evenness gives $|k|$.
\end{proof}

The same result follows more quickly once stationarity is known. Multiply $X_t = \alpha X_{t-1} + Z_t$ by $X_{t-k}$, for $k > 0$, and take
expectations:
\[
E(X_{t-k}X_t) = \alpha E(X_{t-k}X_{t-1}) + E(X_{t-k}Z_t) .
\]
Here $E(X_{t-k}Z_t) = 0$, because $X_{t-k}$ depends only on shocks up to time $t - k$, all independent of $Z_t$. So $\gamma(k) = \alpha\gamma(k - 1)$ for
$k > 0$, and since $\gamma(0) = \sigma_X^2$, repeated use gives $\gamma(k) = \alpha^k\sigma_X^2$ and
\[
\rho(k) = \frac{\gamma(k)}{\gamma(0)} = \frac{\alpha^k\sigma_X^2}{\sigma_X^2} = \alpha^k , \qquad k \ge 0 .
\]
Since $|\rho(k)| \le 1$, we must have $|\alpha| \le 1$. If $|\alpha| = 1$, then $|\rho(k)| = 1$ for all $k$, a degenerate case (with $\alpha = 1$ the process is a
random walk, which is not stationary). Thus $|\alpha| < 1$ for a proper stationary series. The ac.f. of an AR(1) decays
geometrically: smoothly when $\alpha > 0$, alternating in sign when $\alpha < 0$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ar-acf}). Unlike the MA ac.f., it never
cuts off.

\subsection{The general order}
Write the AR($p$) process as
\[
(1 - \alpha_1B - \alpha_2B^2 - \dots - \alpha_pB^p)X_t = Z_t , \qquad\text{or}\qquad \phi(B)X_t = Z_t ,
\]
with $\phi(B) = 1 - \alpha_1B - \dots - \alpha_pB^p$. As for the AR(1), the process can be expressed as an MA process,
\[
X_t = \frac{Z_t}{\phi(B)} = f(B)Z_t , \qquad f(B) = \phi(B)^{-1} = 1 + \psi_1B + \psi_2B^2 + \dots ,
\]
and the weights $\psi_i$ can be found from the $\alpha_i$ by equating coefficients of powers of $B$ in $\phi(B)f(B) = 1$. Since $X_t$ is then written as
an MA process, $E(X_t) = 0$, and the variance $\sigma_Z^2\sum_i\psi_i^2$ is finite provided $\sum\psi_i^2$ converges. As in Theorem~\ref{thm:ma-acv}, the
autocovariance is
\[
\gamma(k) = \sigma_Z^2\sum_{i=0}^\infty\psi_i\psi_{i+k} , \qquad \psi_0 = 1 ,
\]
and a sufficient condition for this to converge is that $\sum|\psi_i|$ converges.

\begin{thm}[Yule--Walker equations]\label{thm:yule-walker}
For a stationary AR($p$) process,
\[
\rho(k) = \alpha_1\rho(k - 1) + \alpha_2\rho(k - 2) + \dots + \alpha_p\rho(k - p) , \qquad k = 1, 2, \dots ,
\]
and the variance is
\[
\sigma_X^2 = \frac{\sigma_Z^2}{1 - \alpha_1\rho(1) - \alpha_2\rho(2) - \dots - \alpha_p\rho(p)} .
\]
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
Multiply $X_t = \sum_{i=1}^p\alpha_iX_{t-i} + Z_t$ by $X_{t-k}$ and take expectations. For $k > 0$, $E(X_{t-k}Z_t) = 0$, because $X_{t-k}$ is a combination of
shocks up to time $t - k$ only; so $\gamma(k) = \sum_i\alpha_i\gamma(k - i)$, and dividing by $\gamma(0)$ gives the equations. For $k = 0$, $E(X_tZ_t) = E(Z_t^2) = \sigma_Z^2$,
since $X_t = Z_t + (\text{terms in earlier shocks})$; so $\gamma(0) = \sum_i\alpha_i\gamma(i) + \sigma_Z^2$, that is $\gamma(0)\bigl(1 - \sum_i\alpha_i\rho(i)\bigr) = \sigma_Z^2$.
\end{proof}

The Yule--Walker equations do two jobs. Taken for $k = 1, \dots, p$, with $\rho(-j) = \rho(j)$, they are $p$ linear equations linking
$\rho(1), \dots, \rho(p)$ to $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_p$: given the $\alpha$'s they give the first $p$ autocorrelations, and given estimated autocorrelations
they give estimates of the $\alpha$'s, one of several routes to fitting an AR model (Section~\ref{sec:estimation}). For $k > p$ they are a
difference equation that generates all the later autocorrelations.

\begin{thm}\label{thm:ar-acf-solution}
Let $\pi_1, \dots, \pi_p$ be the roots, assumed distinct, of the \textbf{auxiliary equation}
\[
y^p - \alpha_1y^{p-1} - \alpha_2y^{p-2} - \dots - \alpha_p = 0 .
\]
Then the general solution of the Yule--Walker difference equation is
\[
\rho(k) = A_1\pi_1^{|k|} + A_2\pi_2^{|k|} + \dots + A_p\pi_p^{|k|} ,
\]
with constants $A_i$ fixed by the initial conditions, in particular $\rho(0) = 1$, so $\sum A_i = 1$. Moreover, $\pi$ is a root of the auxiliary
equation exactly when $1/\pi$ is a root of $\phi(B) = 0$.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
Try $\rho(k) = \pi^k$ in $\rho(k) = \sum_i\alpha_i\rho(k - i)$: dividing by $\pi^{k-p}$ gives $\pi^p = \alpha_1\pi^{p-1} + \dots + \alpha_p$, the auxiliary equation. So each $\pi_i^k$ is a
solution, and since the equation is linear, so is any combination $\sum A_i\pi_i^k$. With distinct roots, these $p$ solutions are
independent, and any solution is determined by $p$ starting values, so every solution has this form. For the last
statement, $\phi(1/\pi) = 1 - \alpha_1\pi^{-1} - \dots - \alpha_p\pi^{-p} = \pi^{-p}(\pi^p - \alpha_1\pi^{p-1} - \dots - \alpha_p)$, which is zero exactly when $\pi$ solves the auxiliary
equation.
\end{proof}

The general form of $\rho(k)$ tends to zero as $k$ increases provided every $|\pi_i| < 1$, and this is the necessary and sufficient
condition for the process to be stationary. By the last part of the theorem, an equivalent way to express it is:

\begin{result}[Stationarity of AR($p$)]
The AR($p$) process is stationary if and only if all the roots of $\phi(B) = 1 - \alpha_1B - \dots - \alpha_pB^p = 0$ lie outside the unit
circle.
\end{result}

This is the same root condition as for the invertibility of an MA process (Theorem~\ref{thm:ma-invertible}), with $\phi$ in place of $\theta$:
the duality again. An AR($p$) process is always invertible, since $Z_t = \phi(B)X_t$ is already a finite sum of observations.

\subsection{The second-order process}
For the AR(2) process $X_t = \alpha_1X_{t-1} + \alpha_2X_{t-2} + Z_t$, the roots $\pi_1$, $\pi_2$ of the quadratic $y^2 - \alpha_1y - \alpha_2 = 0$ are
\[
\pi = \frac{\alpha_1 \pm \sqrt{\alpha_1^2 + 4\alpha_2}}{2} ,
\]
real if $\alpha_1^2 + 4\alpha_2 \ge 0$ and complex if $\alpha_1^2 + 4\alpha_2 < 0$.

\begin{thm}[The stationarity triangle]\label{thm:ar2-triangle}
The AR(2) process is stationary, that is $|\pi_1| < 1$ and $|\pi_2| < 1$, if and only if
\[
\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 < 1 , \qquad \alpha_2 - \alpha_1 < 1 , \qquad \alpha_2 > -1 .
\]
These three inequalities describe the triangle with vertices $(-2, -1)$, $(2, -1)$ and $(0, 1)$ in the $(\alpha_1, \alpha_2)$ plane
(Figure~\ref{fig:ar2-triangle}).
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
Let $g(y) = y^2 - \alpha_1y - \alpha_2 = (y - \pi_1)(y - \pi_2)$, so $\pi_1\pi_2 = -\alpha_2$, $g(1) = 1 - \alpha_1 - \alpha_2$ and $g(-1) = 1 + \alpha_1 - \alpha_2$.

\emph{Only if.} If both roots have modulus less than $1$, then $|\alpha_2| = |\pi_1\pi_2| < 1$. If the roots are real, they lie in $(-1, 1)$, and $g$ is
positive outside the interval between them, so $g(1) > 0$ and $g(-1) > 0$. If they are complex, $g$ has no real zero and
is positive everywhere, so again $g(\pm1) > 0$. These are the three inequalities.

\emph{If.} Suppose the inequalities hold. If the roots are complex they are conjugates, so $|\pi_1|^2 = \pi_1\bar\pi_1 = \pi_1\pi_2 = -\alpha_2 < 1$. If they are
real, the inequalities give $\alpha_1 < 1 - \alpha_2 < 2$ and $\alpha_1 > \alpha_2 - 1 > -2$, so the vertex $y = \alpha_1/2$ of the parabola lies in $(-1, 1)$. Since
$g(-1) > 0$, $g(1) > 0$ and $g$ dips to its minimum (which is $\le 0$, as there are real roots) between them, both roots lie
in $(-1, 1)$.
\end{proof}

\subhead{The ac.f. of an AR(2)}
From the first Yule--Walker equation, $\rho(1) = \alpha_1\rho(0) + \alpha_2\rho(-1) = \alpha_1 + \alpha_2\rho(1)$, so
\[
\rho(1) = \frac{\alpha_1}{1 - \alpha_2} ,
\]
and the later values follow from $\rho(k) = \alpha_1\rho(k - 1) + \alpha_2\rho(k - 2)$. When the roots are real and distinct, $\rho(k) = A_1\pi_1^{|k|} + A_2\pi_2^{|k|}$,
and the constants follow from $\rho(0) = 1$, that is $A_1 + A_2 = 1$, and $\rho(1) = A_1\pi_1 + A_2\pi_2$. Substituting $A_2 = 1 - A_1$,
\[
\frac{\alpha_1}{1 - \alpha_2} = A_1\pi_1 + (1 - A_1)\pi_2 ,
\]
so
\[
A_1 = \frac{\dfrac{\alpha_1}{1 - \alpha_2} - \pi_2}{\pi_1 - \pi_2} , \qquad A_2 = 1 - A_1 .
\]
When the roots are complex, $\pi = re^{\pm i\theta}$ with $r = \sqrt{-\alpha_2}$ and $\cos\theta = \alpha_1/(2\sqrt{-\alpha_2})$, and the two complex terms combine into
a damped cosine wave, $\rho(k) = r^k(a\cos k\theta + b\sin k\theta)$, with period $2\pi/\theta$.

\begin{exa}\label{exa:ar2-complex}
Consider the AR(2) process $X_t = X_{t-1} - \frac12X_{t-2} + Z_t$. Is it stationary? If so, find its ac.f. and variance.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
The roots of $\phi(B) = 1 - B + \frac12B^2 = 0$ are $B = 1 \pm i$, with modulus $\sqrt2 > 1$, so both lie outside the unit circle and the process is
stationary. (Equivalently, $\alpha_1 = 1$, $\alpha_2 = -\frac12$ satisfy $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 = \frac12 < 1$, $\alpha_2 - \alpha_1 = -\frac32 < 1$ and $\alpha_2 > -1$.)

To find the ac.f., use the first Yule--Walker equation, $\rho(1) = \rho(0) - \frac12\rho(-1) = 1 - \frac12\rho(1)$, giving $\rho(1) = \frac23$. For $k \ge 2$,
$\rho(k) = \rho(k - 1) - \frac12\rho(k - 2)$, and successive substitution gives
\begin{gather*}
\rho(2) = \tfrac23 - \tfrac12 = \tfrac16 , \qquad \rho(3) = \tfrac16 - \tfrac13 = -\tfrac16 , \\
\rho(4) = -\tfrac16 - \tfrac1{12} = -\tfrac14 , \qquad \rho(5) = -\tfrac14 + \tfrac1{12} = -\tfrac16 ,
\end{gather*}
then $\rho(6) = -\frac1{24}$, $\rho(7) = \frac1{24}$, $\rho(8) = \frac1{16}$. The roots of the auxiliary equation $y^2 - y + \frac12 = 0$ are $\frac12(1 \pm i)$, the reciprocals of
$1 \pm i$, with $r = 1/\sqrt2$ and $\theta = \pi/4$. So $\rho(k) = 2^{-k/2}(a\cos\frac{k\pi}4 + b\sin\frac{k\pi}4)$, and $\rho(0) = 1$ gives $a = 1$, while $\rho(1) = \frac23$ gives
$\frac1{\sqrt2}(\frac1{\sqrt2} + \frac b{\sqrt2}) = \frac23$, so $b = \frac13$:
\[
\rho(k) = 2^{-k/2}\Bigl(\cos\frac{k\pi}4 + \frac13\sin\frac{k\pi}4\Bigr) , \qquad k \ge 0 ,
\]
a damped cosine with period $8$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ar-acf}, panel~4). By Theorem~\ref{thm:yule-walker}, the variance is
\[
\sigma_X^2 = \frac{\sigma_Z^2}{1 - (1)\frac23 - (-\frac12)\frac16} = \frac{\sigma_Z^2}{5/12} = 2.4\sigma_Z^2 .
\]
\end{solution}

\begin{exa}\label{exa:ar2-real}
Find the ac.f. of $X_t = 0.5X_{t-1} + 0.24X_{t-2} + Z_t$.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
The auxiliary equation $y^2 - 0.5y - 0.24 = 0$ has roots $\pi_1 = 0.8$ and $\pi_2 = -0.3$, both inside the unit circle, so the process is stationary.
Then $\rho(1) = 0.5/(1 - 0.24) = 0.6579$, and
\[
A_1 = \frac{0.6579 - (-0.3)}{0.8 - (-0.3)} = \frac{0.9579}{1.1} = 0.8708 , \qquad A_2 = 0.1292 ,
\]
so
\[
\rho(k) = 0.8708(0.8)^{|k|} + 0.1292(-0.3)^{|k|} .
\]
As a check, the Yule--Walker recursion gives $\rho(2) = 0.5(0.6579) + 0.24 = 0.5689$, and the formula gives $0.8708(0.64) + 0.1292(0.09) = 0.5689$. The
root $0.8$ dominates, and the ac.f. decays much like that of an AR(1) with $\alpha = 0.8$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ar-acf}, panel~3).
\end{solution}

\subsection{Practice problems}
\begin{problem}\label{prob:ar1-moments}
For the AR(1) process $X_t = 0.6X_{t-1} + Z_t$ with $\sigma_Z^2 = 1.28$, find $\Var(X_t)$, $\rho(3)$ and $\Cov(X_t, X_{t+2})$.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$\Var(X_t) = 1.28/(1 - 0.36) = 2$; $\rho(3) = 0.6^3 = 0.216$; $\Cov(X_t, X_{t+2}) = 0.6^2(2) = 0.72$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}\label{prob:ar2-check}
Which of these AR(2) processes are stationary?
(a) $X_t = 1.2X_{t-1} - 0.32X_{t-2} + Z_t$; (b) $X_t = 0.5X_{t-1} + 0.6X_{t-2} + Z_t$; (c) $X_t = -0.4X_{t-1} - 0.9X_{t-2} + Z_t$.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
(a) $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 = 0.88 < 1$, $\alpha_2 - \alpha_1 = -1.52 < 1$, $\alpha_2 = -0.32 > -1$: stationary. The auxiliary roots are $0.8$ and $0.4$.
(b) $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 = 1.1 > 1$: not stationary; one auxiliary root is $1.064$.
(c) $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 = -1.3 < 1$, $\alpha_2 - \alpha_1 = -0.5 < 1$, $\alpha_2 = -0.9 > -1$: stationary. Since $\alpha_1^2 + 4\alpha_2 = 0.16 - 3.6 < 0$ the roots are complex, with
modulus $\sqrt{0.9} = 0.95$, close to $1$: the ac.f. oscillates and dies away slowly.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}\label{prob:ar2-variance}
For the process of Example~\ref{exa:ar2-real} with $\sigma_Z^2 = 1$, find $\Var(X_t)$.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:yule-walker}, $\sigma_X^2 = 1/(1 - 0.5(0.6579) - 0.24(0.5689)) = 1/(1 - 0.3289 - 0.1365) = 1/0.5345 = 1.871$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}\label{prob:yw-reverse}
A stationary series is believed to be AR(2), with $\rho(1) = 0.6$ and $\rho(2) = 0.5$. Use the Yule--Walker equations to find $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
The equations for $k = 1, 2$ are $\rho(1) = \alpha_1 + \alpha_2\rho(1)$ and $\rho(2) = \alpha_1\rho(1) + \alpha_2$, that is $0.6 = \alpha_1 + 0.6\alpha_2$ and $0.5 = 0.6\alpha_1 + \alpha_2$.
Solving,
\begin{align*}
\alpha_1 &= \frac{\rho(1)(1 - \rho(2))}{1 - \rho(1)^2} = \frac{0.6(0.5)}{0.64} = 0.469 , \\
\alpha_2 &= \frac{\rho(2) - \rho(1)^2}{1 - \rho(1)^2} = \frac{0.5 - 0.36}{0.64} = 0.219 .
\end{align*}
These satisfy the triangle conditions. Replacing $\rho(1)$, $\rho(2)$ by sample values is how AR models are estimated by the
Yule--Walker method, and $\alpha_2$ here is the partial autocorrelation at lag $2$ (Section~\ref{sec:pacf}).
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}\label{prob:ar1-psi}
Find the $\psi$ weights of the AR(2) process $X_t = 0.5X_{t-1} + 0.24X_{t-2} + Z_t$ up to $\psi_3$, by equating coefficients in $\phi(B)f(B) = 1$.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$(1 - 0.5B - 0.24B^2)(1 + \psi_1B + \psi_2B^2 + \psi_3B^3 + \dots) = 1$. The coefficient of $B$: $\psi_1 - 0.5 = 0$, so $\psi_1 = 0.5$. Of $B^2$:
$\psi_2 - 0.5\psi_1 - 0.24 = 0$, so $\psi_2 = 0.49$. Of $B^3$: $\psi_3 - 0.5\psi_2 - 0.24\psi_1 = 0$, so $\psi_3 = 0.245 + 0.12 = 0.365$. In general
$\psi_j = 0.5\psi_{j-1} + 0.24\psi_{j-2}$, the same recursion as the ac.f.
\end{solution}

\section{Mixed ARMA models}\label{sec:arma}
\subsection{Definition}
The MA and AR models can be combined. A mixed process with $p$ AR terms and $q$ MA terms is an \textbf{ARMA process of
order $(p, q)$}, written ARMA($p, q$):
\[
X_t = \alpha_1X_{t-1} + \alpha_2X_{t-2} + \dots + \alpha_pX_{t-p} + Z_t + \beta_1Z_{t-1} + \dots + \beta_qZ_{t-q} ,
\]
or, with the backward shift operator,
\[
\phi(B)X_t = \theta(B)Z_t ,
\]
where $\phi(B)$ and $\theta(B)$ are polynomials of order $p$ and $q$:
\begin{align*}
\phi(B) &= 1 - \alpha_1B - \alpha_2B^2 - \dots - \alpha_pB^p , \\
\theta(B) &= 1 + \beta_1B + \beta_2B^2 + \dots + \beta_qB^q .
\end{align*}
The conditions on the two parts are those found separately in Sections~\ref{sec:ar} and~\ref{sec:invertibility}:
\begin{itemize}
\item the process is \emph{stationary} if the roots of $\phi(B) = 0$ lie outside the unit circle;
\item it is \emph{invertible} if the roots of $\theta(B) = 0$ lie outside the unit circle.
\end{itemize}
ARMA processes are important because a stationary series can often be described by an ARMA model with fewer
parameters than a pure MA or pure AR model would need: the principle of \textbf{parsimony}.

One condition is easily overlooked: $\phi(B)$ and $\theta(B)$ must have \emph{no common factor}. If they share a factor, it cancels
from both sides of $\phi(B)X_t = \theta(B)Z_t$, and the process is really of lower order. For example,
$X_t = 0.4X_{t-1} + Z_t - 0.4Z_{t-1}$ is $(1 - 0.4B)X_t = (1 - 0.4B)Z_t$, which is just $X_t = Z_t$, white noise. Fitting such an over-specified
model gives unstable estimates, since any common factor fits equally well.

\subsection{The $\psi$ and $\pi$ weights}
It is useful to express an ARMA process as a pure MA process,
\[
X_t = \psi(B)Z_t , \qquad \psi(B) = \sum_{i=0}^\infty\psi_iB^i = \frac{\theta(B)}{\phi(B)} ,
\]
an MA operator that may be of finite or infinite order, and as a pure AR process,
\[
\pi(B)X_t = Z_t , \qquad \pi(B) = \frac{\phi(B)}{\theta(B)} = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^\infty\pi_iB^i ,
\]
so that
\[
X_t = \sum_{i=1}^\infty\pi_iX_{t-i} + Z_t .
\]
From $X_t = \psi(B)Z_t$ and $\pi(B)X_t = Z_t$, we have $\psi(B)\pi(B) = 1$, as it must be, since
\[
\frac{\theta(B)}{\phi(B)}\cdot\frac{\phi(B)}{\theta(B)} = 1 .
\]
The $\psi$ weights describe how a shock propagates: a shock $Z_t$ of size $1$ changes $X_{t+j}$ by $\psi_j$, so the sequence $\psi_0, \psi_1, \dots$ is the
\emph{impulse response} of the process. The $\pi$ weights describe how the current value depends on the past.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:psi-recursion}
The $\psi$ weights satisfy $\psi_0 = 1$ and
\[
\psi_j = \alpha_1\psi_{j-1} + \alpha_2\psi_{j-2} + \dots + \alpha_p\psi_{j-p} + \beta_j , \qquad j \ge 1 ,
\]
where $\psi_i = 0$ for $i < 0$ and $\beta_j = 0$ for $j > q$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Equate coefficients of $B^j$ in $\phi(B)\psi(B) = \theta(B)$. The coefficient of $B^j$ on the left is $\psi_j - \alpha_1\psi_{j-1} - \dots - \alpha_p\psi_{j-p}$, and on the
right it is $\beta_j$ (with $\beta_0 = 1$, giving $\psi_0 = 1$).
\end{proof}

The $\pi$ weights are found the same way from $\theta(B)\pi(B) = \phi(B)$. For $j > q$ the $\psi$ weights obey the AR recursion
$\psi_j = \sum_i\alpha_i\psi_{j-i}$, so they die away geometrically, as an AR ac.f. does.

\begin{exa}\label{exa:arma11-weights}
Find the $\psi$ and $\pi$ weights for the ARMA(1,1) process $X_t = 0.5X_{t-1} + Z_t - 0.3Z_{t-1}$.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
Here $\phi(B) = 1 - 0.5B$ and $\theta(B) = 1 - 0.3B$. The root of $\phi(B) = 0$ is $B = 1/0.5 = 2$, and that of $\theta(B) = 0$ is $B = 1/0.3 = 3.33$; both lie
outside the unit circle, so the process is stationary and invertible. Then
\begin{align*}
\psi(B) &= \frac{\theta(B)}{\phi(B)} = (1 - 0.3B)(1 - 0.5B)^{-1} \\
&= (1 - 0.3B)(1 + 0.5B + 0.5^2B^2 + 0.5^3B^3 + \dots) \\
&= 1 + (0.5 - 0.3)B + (0.25 - 0.15)B^2 + (0.125 - 0.075)B^3 + \dots \\
&= 1 + 0.2B + 0.1B^2 + 0.05B^3 + \dots ,
\end{align*}
so $\psi_i = 0.2 \times 0.5^{i-1}$ for $i = 1, 2, 3, \dots$. Similarly,
\begin{align*}
\pi(B) &= \frac{\phi(B)}{\theta(B)} = (1 - 0.5B)(1 + 0.3B + 0.09B^2 + \dots) \\
&= 1 - 0.2B - 0.06B^2 - 0.018B^3 - \dots ,
\end{align*}
so $\pi_i = 0.2 \times 0.3^{i-1}$ for $i = 1, 2, \dots$. (Proposition~\ref{prop:psi-recursion} gives the same $\psi$'s: $\psi_1 = 0.5 - 0.3 = 0.2$, then $\psi_j = 0.5\psi_{j-1}$.)
\end{solution}

\subsection{The autocorrelation function}
\begin{thm}\label{thm:arma11-acf}
For the stationary ARMA(1,1) process $X_t = \alpha X_{t-1} + Z_t + \beta Z_{t-1}$,
\begin{gather*}
\gamma(0) = \sigma_Z^2\frac{1 + 2\alpha\beta + \beta^2}{1 - \alpha^2} , \qquad \gamma(1) = \sigma_Z^2\frac{(1 + \alpha\beta)(\alpha + \beta)}{1 - \alpha^2} , \\
\gamma(k) = \alpha\gamma(k - 1) \quad (k \ge 2) .
\end{gather*}
So
\[
\rho(1) = \frac{(1 + \alpha\beta)(\alpha + \beta)}{1 + 2\alpha\beta + \beta^2} , \qquad \rho(k) = \alpha^{k-1}\rho(1) \quad (k \ge 1) .
\]
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:psi-recursion}, $\psi_0 = 1$ and $\psi_j = (\alpha + \beta)\alpha^{j-1}$ for $j \ge 1$. Then, as for an MA process,
\begin{align*}
\gamma(0) &= \sigma_Z^2\sum_{j\ge0}\psi_j^2 = \sigma_Z^2\Bigl[1 + (\alpha + \beta)^2\sum_{j\ge1}\alpha^{2(j-1)}\Bigr] \\
&= \sigma_Z^2\Bigl[1 + \frac{(\alpha + \beta)^2}{1 - \alpha^2}\Bigr] = \sigma_Z^2\frac{1 + 2\alpha\beta + \beta^2}{1 - \alpha^2} , \\
\gamma(1) &= \sigma_Z^2\sum_{j\ge0}\psi_j\psi_{j+1} = \sigma_Z^2\Bigl[(\alpha + \beta) + (\alpha + \beta)^2\sum_{j\ge1}\alpha^{2j-1}\Bigr] \\
&= \sigma_Z^2(\alpha + \beta)\Bigl[1 + \frac{\alpha(\alpha + \beta)}{1 - \alpha^2}\Bigr] ,
\end{align*}
and $1 - \alpha^2 + \alpha^2 + \alpha\beta = 1 + \alpha\beta$ gives the stated $\gamma(1)$. For $k \ge 2$, multiply the model by $X_{t-k}$ and take expectations: $X_{t-k}$ involves only
shocks up to time $t - k$, so $E(X_{t-k}Z_t) = E(X_{t-k}Z_{t-1}) = 0$, leaving $\gamma(k) = \alpha\gamma(k - 1)$.
\end{proof}

So the ac.f. of an ARMA(1,1) starts from a value $\rho(1)$ fixed by both parameters and then decays geometrically
at the AR rate $\alpha$. In general the ac.f. of an ARMA($p, q$) satisfies the AR Yule--Walker recursion for $k > q$, so it tails
off like that of an AR process, after $q$ irregular starting values. Neither the ac.f. nor (as Section~\ref{sec:pacf}
shows) the partial ac.f. cuts off, which is what makes mixed models harder to identify.

\begin{exa}\label{exa:arma11-acf}
Find the ac.f. and variance of the process of Example~\ref{exa:arma11-weights}.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
With $\alpha = 0.5$ and $\beta = -0.3$, $1 + 2\alpha\beta + \beta^2 = 1 - 0.3 + 0.09 = 0.79$ and $(1 + \alpha\beta)(\alpha + \beta) = 0.85 \times 0.2 = 0.17$. So
\begin{gather*}
\gamma(0) = \frac{0.79}{0.75}\sigma_Z^2 = 1.053\sigma_Z^2 , \qquad \rho(1) = \frac{0.17}{0.79} = 0.215 , \\
\rho(k) = 0.215(0.5)^{k-1} .
\end{gather*}
As a check, $\sum\psi_j^2 = 1 + 0.04/0.75 = 1.0533$. The MA term with $\beta = -0.3$ partly cancels the AR term, so $\rho(1)$ is much smaller than
the $0.5$ of the AR(1) alone.
\end{solution}

\section{Integrated ARIMA models}\label{sec:integrated}
\subsection{Definition}
When a process is non-stationary, the non-stationary sources of variation must be removed. A common
approach, which goes back to Box and Jenkins, is to difference the series. If in the ARMA model
\[
X_t = \alpha_1X_{t-1} + \dots + \alpha_pX_{t-p} + Z_t + \beta_1Z_{t-1} + \dots + \beta_qZ_{t-q}
\]
$X_t$ is replaced by its $d$th difference $\nabla^dX_t$, the model is capable of describing a certain type of non-stationary
series. Writing
\[
W_t = \nabla^dX_t = (1 - B)^dX_t ,
\]
the model is the ARMA process
\[
W_t = \alpha_1W_{t-1} + \dots + \alpha_pW_{t-p} + Z_t + \beta_1Z_{t-1} + \dots + \beta_qZ_{t-q} ,
\]
or $\phi(B)W_t = \theta(B)Z_t$,
or, in terms of the original series,
\[
\phi(B)(1 - B)^dX_t = \theta(B)Z_t .
\]
Thus $W_t$ is a stationary ARMA($p, q$) process, while $X_t$, described through its $d$th difference, is an \textbf{autoregressive
integrated moving average} process of order $(p, d, q)$, ARIMA($p, d, q$). The name ``integrated'' is explained by the
case $d = 1$: then $X_t = X_0 + W_1 + W_2 + \dots + W_t$, a sum (the discrete analogue of an integral) of the stationary $W$'s.

The model for $X_t$ is non-stationary: its generalised AR operator $\phi(B)(1 - B)^d$ has $d$ roots \emph{on} the unit circle, at
$B = 1$, rather than outside it. In practice $d$ is usually $1$, occasionally $2$. The second difference is
\begin{align*}
\nabla^2X_t &= \nabla(X_t - X_{t-1}) \\
&= (X_t - X_{t-1}) - (X_{t-1} - X_{t-2}) \\
&= X_t - 2X_{t-1} + X_{t-2} ,
\end{align*}
which is $(1 - B)^2X_t = (1 - 2B + B^2)X_t$, as the binomial expansion says. Special cases are familiar: ARIMA(0,1,0) is the
random walk of Section~\ref{sec:random-walk}, and ARIMA(0,0,$q$) and ARIMA($p$,0,0) are the MA and AR processes.

\begin{exa}\label{exa:arima110}
Write the ARIMA(1,1,0) model $(1 - 0.6B)(1 - B)X_t = Z_t$ as a difference equation in $X_t$, and find the roots of its
generalised AR operator. Given $x_{n-1} = 50$ and $x_n = 54$, find the value of $X_{n+1}$ if the next shock is $0$.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
$(1 - 0.6B)(1 - B) = 1 - 1.6B + 0.6B^2$, so
\[
X_t = 1.6X_{t-1} - 0.6X_{t-2} + Z_t .
\]
The roots of $1 - 1.6B + 0.6B^2 = 0$ are $B = 1$ and $B = 1/0.6 = 1.67$: one unit root, from the differencing, and one stationary root. As an
AR(2) this would fail the triangle condition, since $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 = 1.6 - 0.6 = 1$, exactly on the boundary. With $Z_{n+1} = 0$,
$X_{n+1} = 1.6(54) - 0.6(50) = 56.4$. In terms of differences: the last change was $W_n = 4$, the next is $0.6 \times 4 = 2.4$, and $54 + 2.4 = 56.4$. The
changes die away geometrically, but the level never returns to any fixed mean.
\end{solution}

\subsection{How much to difference}
Differencing too little leaves a non-stationary series, recognisable by a sample ac.f. that dies away only
very slowly. Differencing too much is also a mistake, with its own signature.

\begin{thm}[Over-differencing]\label{thm:overdifference}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item If $X_t = a + bt + Y_t$, where $Y_t$ is stationary, then $\nabla X_t = b + \nabla Y_t$ is stationary.
\item If $Y_t$ is already white noise, then $\nabla Y_t = Y_t - Y_{t-1}$ is a non-invertible MA(1) process with $\beta = -1$, and $\rho(1) = -\frac12$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
(i) $\nabla(a + bt) = b$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:differencing}), and $\nabla Y_t = Y_t - Y_{t-1}$ is a fixed linear combination of a stationary process, so its
mean is constant and $\Cov(\nabla Y_t, \nabla Y_{t+k}) = 2\gamma_Y(k) - \gamma_Y(k + 1) - \gamma_Y(k - 1)$ depends only on $k$.

(ii) $\nabla Y_t = Y_t - Y_{t-1}$ is MA(1) with $\beta = -1$, so by Section~\ref{sec:ma}, $\rho(1) = -1/(1 + 1) = -\frac12$, the boundary value of
Theorem~\ref{thm:ma1-bound}, and $\theta(B) = 1 - B$ has its root at $B = 1$, on the unit circle: not invertible.
\end{proof}

So if a differenced series has a sample $r_1$ close to $-0.5$, it has probably been differenced once too often. The
practical rule is to difference until the sample ac.f. dies away reasonably quickly, and no further. (Formal
tests for a unit root, such as the Dickey--Fuller test, are available in software.)

\begin{exa}\label{exa:arima011}
Show that the ARIMA(0,1,1) model $X_t = X_{t-1} + Z_t - 0.4Z_{t-1}$ can be written as
\[
X_t = \sum_{j=1}^\infty 0.6(0.4)^{j-1}X_{t-j} + Z_t ,
\]
so that the predictable part of $X_t$ is an exponentially weighted average of the past.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
The model is $(1 - B)X_t = (1 - 0.4B)Z_t$, invertible since $|0.4| < 1$. So
\begin{align*}
\pi(B) &= \frac{1 - B}{1 - 0.4B} = (1 - B)(1 + 0.4B + 0.16B^2 + \dots) \\
&= 1 - 0.6B - 0.24B^2 - 0.096B^3 - \dots ,
\end{align*}
and $\pi_j = 0.6(0.4)^{j-1}$: the coefficient of $B^j$ is $0.4^j - 0.4^{j-1} = -0.6(0.4)^{j-1}$. These weights are positive, decrease geometrically,
and add to $0.6/(1 - 0.4) = 1$, so $\sum\pi_jX_{t-j}$ is a weighted average of past values, with the most recent weighted most. This is
exactly the forecast of simple exponential smoothing with smoothing constant $0.6$ (Section~\ref{sec:forecasting}).
\end{solution}

Show that $X_t = 1.3X_{t-1} - 0.4X_{t-2} + Z_t - 0.5Z_{t-1}$ is really an AR(1) process.

\section{Partial autocorrelation and model identification}\label{sec:pacf}
\subsection{The partial autocorrelation function}
The autocorrelation function alone cannot identify an autoregressive model. For an AR(1) process the
correlation between $X_t$ and $X_{t+2}$ is $\rho(2) = \alpha^2$, which is not zero, but none of it is direct: it is entirely inherited through
$X_{t+1}$. The partial autocorrelation is what removes that inheritance.

\begin{defn}
The \textbf{partial autocorrelation} at lag $k$, written $\phi_{kk}$, is the correlation between $X_t$ and $X_{t+k}$ after removing the
linear effect of the intervening values $X_{t+1}, \dots, X_{t+k-1}$:
\[
\phi_{kk} = \Corr\left(X_t, \ X_{t+k} \;\middle|\; X_{t+1}, \dots, X_{t+k-1}\right) .
\]
Equivalently, $\phi_{kk}$ is the coefficient of $X_{t-k}$ in the best linear predictor of $X_t$ from the previous $k$ values,
\[
\hat X_t = \phi_{k1}X_{t-1} + \phi_{k2}X_{t-2} + \dots + \phi_{kk}X_{t-k} .
\]
\end{defn}

``Best'' means minimising the mean squared error $E(X_t - \hat X_t)^2$, for a stationary process with mean $0$. The coefficient of $X_{t-k}$
measures what $X_{t-k}$ adds to the prediction when the values $X_{t-1}, \dots, X_{t-k+1}$ are already used, which is the direct link the
definition asks for.

\begin{thm}[Yule--Walker form of the partial autocorrelations]\label{thm:pacf-yw}
The coefficients of the best linear predictor from $k$ past values solve
\[
\rho(j) = \sum_{i=1}^{k}\phi_{ki}\,\rho(j - i) , \qquad j = 1, \dots, k ,
\]
and $\phi_{kk}$ is the last coefficient of that system. In matrix form, with $\mathbf{R}_k$ the $k \times k$ matrix of $\rho(i - j)$,
\[
\mathbf{R}_k\boldsymbol\phi_k = \boldsymbol\rho_k , \qquad \boldsymbol\phi_k = (\phi_{k1}, \dots, \phi_{kk})^\top , \quad \boldsymbol\rho_k = (\rho(1), \dots, \rho(k))^\top .
\]
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
The mean squared error $Q = E\bigl(X_t - \sum_i\phi_{ki}X_{t-i}\bigr)^2$ is a quadratic in the $\phi_{ki}$. Setting $\partial Q/\partial\phi_{kj} = 0$,
\[
-2E\Bigl[\Bigl(X_t - \sum_i\phi_{ki}X_{t-i}\Bigr)X_{t-j}\Bigr] = 0 ,
\]
that is,
\[
\gamma(j) = \sum_i\phi_{ki}\gamma(j - i) , \qquad j = 1, \dots, k :
\]
the prediction error is uncorrelated with each predictor, exactly like the normal equations of least squares.
Dividing by $\gamma(0)$ gives the system. The matrix $\mathbf{R}_k$ is non-negative definite (Theorem~\ref{thm:acf-properties}), and positive definite for
any process that is not perfectly predictable, so the solution is unique and minimises $Q$.
\end{proof}

\begin{exa}\label{exa:pacf12}
Compute $\phi_{11}$ and $\phi_{22}$ for a general stationary process.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
At $k = 1$ the system is $\rho(1) = \phi_{11}$, so
\[
\phi_{11} = \rho(1) .
\]
At $k = 2$,
\[
\rho(1) = \phi_{21} + \phi_{22}\rho(1) , \qquad \rho(2) = \phi_{21}\rho(1) + \phi_{22} .
\]
Eliminating $\phi_{21}$ (multiply the first by $\rho(1)$ and subtract it from the second),
\[
\phi_{22} = \frac{\rho(2) - \rho(1)^2}{1 - \rho(1)^2} .
\]
For an AR(1) process $\rho(k) = \alpha^k$, so
\[
\phi_{22} = \frac{\alpha^2 - \alpha^2}{1 - \alpha^2} = 0 ,
\]
exactly as the definition promises: once $X_{t+1}$ is accounted for, nothing direct connects $X_t$ to $X_{t+2}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{thm}[AR and MA partial autocorrelations]\label{thm:pacf-ar}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item If the process is a stationary AR($p$), then $\phi_{pp} = \alpha_p$, and $\phi_{kk} = 0$ for every $k > p$. That is, the partial
autocorrelation function \emph{cuts off} after lag $p$.
\item For the MA(1) process $X_t = Z_t + \beta Z_{t-1}$ with $\beta \ne 0$, $\phi_{22} = -\rho(1)^2/(1 - \rho(1)^2) \ne 0$; the partial autocorrelations do not cut off.
\end{enumerate}
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
(i) Let $k \ge p$ and try $\phi_{ki} = \alpha_i$ for $i \le p$ and $\phi_{ki} = 0$ for $p < i \le k$. Then $\sum_i\phi_{ki}\rho(j - i) = \sum_{i=1}^p\alpha_i\rho(j - i)$, which equals $\rho(j)$ for
every $j \ge 1$ by the Yule--Walker equations (Theorem~\ref{thm:yule-walker}). So this choice solves the system of Theorem~\ref{thm:pacf-yw},
and since the solution is unique, it is the solution. Its last coefficient is $\alpha_p$ when $k = p$, and $0$ when $k > p$.

(ii) By Example~\ref{exa:pacf12} with $\rho(2) = 0$, $\phi_{22} = -\rho(1)^2/(1 - \rho(1)^2)$, which is non-zero because $\rho(1) = \beta/(1 + \beta^2) \ne 0$.
\end{proof}

Part (i) is intuitive: an AR($p$) process is a regression on its own last $p$ values with an error uncorrelated with
the past, so once those $p$ values are known, earlier ones add nothing. For the MA(1), more generally, the partial
autocorrelations decay geometrically in size and never vanish, because an invertible MA(1) is an AR($\infty$)
(Section~\ref{sec:invertibility}): every past value has some direct effect. With $\beta = 0.5$, for example, $\rho(1) = 0.4$ and
\[
\phi_{11} = 0.4 , \quad \phi_{22} = -0.190 , \quad \phi_{33} = 0.094 , \quad \phi_{44} = -0.047 , \dots ,
\]
alternating in sign and halving at each step.

\subsection{Identifying a model}
The two functions cut off for opposite models, and this is what makes them usable together:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
Model & ac.f. & partial ac.f. \\
\hline
AR($p$) & tails off & cuts off after lag $p$ \\
MA($q$) & cuts off after lag $q$ & tails off \\
ARMA($p, q$) & tails off & tails off \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Figure~\ref{fig:identification} shows typical patterns (``pacf'' is the partial ac.f.).

The duality is not a coincidence. An AR($p$) process is a finite regression on its own past, so beyond lag $p$ there is
no direct effect left to find and the partial ac.f. is zero. An MA($q$) process is a finite sum of $q + 1$ shocks, so beyond
lag $q$ two values share no shock at all and the ac.f. is zero. Each model is finite in one representation and
infinite in the other, which is the invertibility discussion of Section~\ref{sec:invertibility} seen from the other side.

\subhead{Sampling variation}
In practice both functions are estimated from data, as $r_k$ and $\hat\phi_{kk}$ (the latter by solving the system of
Theorem~\ref{thm:pacf-yw} with $r_k$ in place of $\rho(k)$), and neither cuts off exactly. The usual rule is to treat a sample value as
zero when it lies within $\pm2/\sqrt n$. The justification for white noise is as follows.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:rk-white-noise}
If $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are independent with mean $0$, variance $\sigma^2$ and finite fourth moment, then for each fixed $k \ge 1$, $r_k$ is
approximately $N(0, 1/n)$ when $n$ is large.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}[Derivation]
For large $n$, $\bar x \approx 0$ and $c_0 \approx \sigma^2$, so $r_k \approx c_k/\sigma^2$ with $c_k \approx \frac1n\sum_{t=1}^{n-k}X_tX_{t+k}$. Each product $X_tX_{t+k}$ has mean $0$ and variance
$E(X_t^2)E(X_{t+k}^2) = \sigma^4$, and two products $X_tX_{t+k}$ and $X_sX_{s+k}$ with $s \ne t$ are uncorrelated: the expectation of their product
contains some $X$ appearing exactly once, whose mean is $0$. So $\Var(c_k) \approx (n - k)\sigma^4/n^2 \approx \sigma^4/n$, and $\Var(r_k) \approx 1/n$. The sum is
of many terms, each dependent only on its neighbours, and a central limit theorem for such sums gives
approximate normality.
\end{proof}

For a process that is not white noise the variance is larger. If the process is MA($q$), then for $k > q$ Bartlett's
approximation gives $\Var(r_k) \approx \frac1n\bigl(1 + 2\sum_{i=1}^q\rho(i)^2\bigr)$, so the band for testing whether the ac.f. has cut off should be widened
accordingly. For an AR($p$) process, the sample partial autocorrelations beyond lag $p$ are approximately $N(0, 1/n)$, so the
plain band $\pm2/\sqrt n$ applies to them. With $n = 100$ that band is about $\pm0.2$, which is wide: identification is a
judgement, not a calculation, and the reason the Box--Jenkins approach insists on checking the fitted model
afterwards.

\begin{remark}
When both functions tail off, the process is ARMA and the orders cannot be read off directly. The practical
route is then to fit several candidate models and compare them by an information criterion, penalising
parameters:
\[
\text{AIC} = -2\log L + 2r , \qquad \text{BIC} = -2\log L + r\log n ,
\]
where $r$ is the number of estimated parameters. BIC penalises more heavily and tends to choose the smaller
model, which for forecasting is usually the safer error to make.
\end{remark}

\begin{exa}\label{exa:identify}
A stationary series of $n = 150$ observations, with mean removed, gave these sample autocorrelations and partial
autocorrelations:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{crr}
$k$ & $r_k$ & $\hat\phi_{kk}$ \\
\hline
1 & 0.394 & 0.394 \\
2 & $-0.183$ & $-0.400$ \\
3 & $-0.182$ & 0.108 \\
4 & $-0.026$ & $-0.063$ \\
5 & 0.040 & 0.026 \\
6 & $-0.013$ & $-0.066$ \\
7 & $-0.109$ & $-0.084$ \\
8 & $-0.106$ & $-0.031$ \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Identify a model and give rough estimates of its parameters.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
The band is $\pm2/\sqrt{150} = \pm0.163$. The partial autocorrelations at lags $1$ and $2$ ($0.394$ and $-0.400$) lie well outside it, and all
those from lag $3$ on lie inside: the partial ac.f. cuts off after lag $2$. The ac.f. does not cut off: $r_2$ and $r_3$ are just outside the band,
and the coefficients change sign and die away, the damped oscillation of an AR(2) with complex roots
(Figure~\ref{fig:identification}, panel~1). So an AR(2) model is indicated.

Rough estimates follow from the Yule--Walker equations with $r_1$, $r_2$ in place of $\rho(1)$, $\rho(2)$ (Problem~\ref{prob:yw-reverse}):
\begin{align*}
\hat\alpha_1 &= \frac{r_1(1 - r_2)}{1 - r_1^2} = \frac{0.394(1.183)}{1 - 0.155} = \frac{0.4661}{0.8448} = 0.552 , \\
\hat\alpha_2 &= \frac{r_2 - r_1^2}{1 - r_1^2} = \frac{-0.183 - 0.155}{0.8448} = -0.400 .
\end{align*}
As Theorem~\ref{thm:pacf-ar} predicts, $\hat\alpha_2$ equals $\hat\phi_{22}$. The point $(0.552, -0.400)$ lies inside the stationarity triangle, below the
parabola $\alpha_1^2 + 4\alpha_2 = 0$, so the fitted model has complex roots, consistent with the oscillating correlogram. (The series
was in fact simulated from $\alpha_1 = 0.6$, $\alpha_2 = -0.4$.) The next step is to estimate the parameters properly and check the fit
(Section~\ref{sec:estimation}).
\end{solution}

\subsection{Practice problems}
\begin{problem}\label{prob:pacf-calc}
A stationary process has $\rho(1) = 0.5$, $\rho(2) = 0.3$ and $\rho(3) = 0.1$. Find $\phi_{11}$, $\phi_{22}$ and $\phi_{33}$.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$\phi_{11} = 0.5$ and $\phi_{22} = (0.3 - 0.25)/(1 - 0.25) = 0.067$. For $\phi_{33}$, solve
\[
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.5 & 0.3 \\ 0.5 & 1 & 0.5 \\ 0.3 & 0.5 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} \phi_{31} \\ \phi_{32} \\ \phi_{33} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 \\ 0.3 \\ 0.1 \end{pmatrix} ,
\]
which gives $\phi_{31} = 0.473$, $\phi_{32} = 0.113$, $\phi_{33} = -0.098$. (Check the first equation: $0.473 + 0.5(0.113) + 0.3(-0.098) = 0.500$.)
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}\label{prob:pacf-ma1}
For the MA(1) process $X_t = Z_t - 0.6Z_{t-1}$, find $\phi_{11}$ and $\phi_{22}$.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$\rho(1) = -0.6/1.36 = -0.441$, so $\phi_{11} = -0.441$ and, by Theorem~\ref{thm:pacf-ar}(ii), $\phi_{22} = -0.1946/(1 - 0.1946) = -0.242$. Both are negative, and
the partial ac.f. continues to decay with the same sign, while the ac.f. is zero from lag $2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}\label{prob:pacf-ar1}
Show directly from the definition that, for an AR(1) process, $\phi_{33} = 0$.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:pacf-ar}(i) with $p = 1$, the system for $k = 3$ is solved by $(\phi_{31}, \phi_{32}, \phi_{33}) = (\alpha, 0, 0)$. Directly: with $\rho(j) = \alpha^j$,
$\sum_i\phi_{3i}\rho(j - i) = \alpha\rho(j - 1) = \alpha^j = \rho(j)$ for $j = 1, 2, 3$, so the equations hold and $\phi_{33} = 0$.
\end{solution}

\end{center}
Suggest a model for each.
\end{problem}

\end{solution}

\section{Forecasting}\label{sec:forecasting}
 Let $X_1,X_2,..............,X_n$ be an observed time series. The problem is to estimate further values $X_{t+k}$, $\hspace{0.2cm} k $is called lead time. The forecast $X_{n+k}$ at time $n$ at $k$ steps ahead can be denoted by $\widehat{x}(n,k)$. Various methods for forecasting exist. No single method is universally available.\\ Forecasting is of the form of extrapolation condition of a characteristic of a time series.\\
 
 
 
 Forecasting Methods:  Are broadly based on
 \begin{enumerate}
 \item Subjective\\-judgment\\-intuition\\-business knowledge\\or any other relevant information.
 \item Univariate\\based on a model fitted only the passed observation of $\{X_t\}$ so that $\widehat{x}(n,k)$ depends only on $X_t,X_t,........,X_n$
 \item Multivariate\\Used for non-seasonal time series showing no trends. For time series with trends or seasonal patterns these need to be removed to produce stationarity.\\
 \end{enumerate}
 
 Suppose $X_1,X_2,.......,X_n$ is a non-seasonal time series with no trend. We take $X_{n+1}$ as a waited a sum of passed observations
 $$\widehat{x}(n,1)= C_0X_n+C_1X_{n-1}+C_2X_{n-2}+\cdots\cdots$$
 then $\{C_i\}$ are the weights (Geometric weights) are used so that\\ $C_i=\alpha (1-\alpha )^i, \hspace{0.5cm}i=1,2,....$ where $\alpha$ is such that $0<\alpha <1.$\\\\
 The $\hspace{0.3cm}\widehat{x}(n,1)=C_0X_n+C_1X_{n-1}+C_2X_{n-2}+\cdots\cdots$\\\\

\section{The Frequency Domain}\label{sec:frequency}

Everything so far has described a series by how each value relates to the ones
before it. The frequency domain describes the same series by which
\emph{cycles} it is made of. Neither view adds information the other lacks ---
they are Fourier transforms of one another --- but each makes different things
obvious, and periodicity is far easier to see as a peak than as a pattern in the
autocorrelations.

\subsection{The Spectral Density}

\begin{defn}
For a stationary process with autocovariance $\gamma(\tau)$ satisfying
$\sum_{\tau}\left|\gamma(\tau)\right|<\infty$, the \emph{spectral density} is
$$f(\omega) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{\tau=-\infty}^{\infty}\gamma(\tau)\,e^{-i\omega\tau},
\qquad -\pi \leq \omega \leq \pi .$$
\end{defn}

\begin{result}
The autocovariance is recovered by inversion,
$$\gamma(\tau) = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(\omega)\,e^{i\omega\tau}\,d\omega ,$$
and in particular
$$\gamma(0) = \Var(X_t) = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(\omega)\,d\omega .$$
\end{result}

\begin{note}
The last line is the interpretation. The total variance of the series is
distributed across frequencies, and $f(\omega)\,d\omega$ is the share
contributed by cycles near frequency $\omega$. A peak in the spectrum is a
frequency band doing a disproportionate amount of the work --- which is exactly
what a seasonal effect looks like, and it is visible at a glance where the
autocorrelation function would show only a slow ripple.
\end{note}

\begin{exa}
Find the spectral density of white noise, and of the AR$(1)$ process
$X_t = \phi X_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
\subhead{White noise}
Here $\gamma(0)=\sigma^{2}$ and $\gamma(\tau)=0$ otherwise, so only one term
survives:
$$f(\omega) = \frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\pi},$$
constant in $\omega$. Every frequency contributes equally --- which is what the
name means, by analogy with white light.

\subhead{AR(1)}
For the AR$(1)$, $\gamma(\tau) = \dfrac{\sigma^{2}\phi^{|\tau|}}{1-\phi^{2}}$.
Summing two geometric series,
$$f(\omega) = \frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{1}{\left|1-\phi e^{-i\omega}\right|^{2}}
= \frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\pi\left(1 - 2\phi\cos\omega + \phi^{2}\right)} .$$
For $\phi>0$ this is largest at $\omega=0$: the series is dominated by slow
movement, which is what positive autocorrelation means. For $\phi<0$ the peak
moves to $\omega=\pi$, the fastest representable oscillation --- alternation
from one observation to the next.
\end{solution}

\subsection{The Periodogram}

\begin{defn}
Given data $x_1,\dots,x_n$, the \emph{periodogram} at the Fourier frequencies
$\omega_j = 2\pi j/n$ is
$$I(\omega_j) = \frac{1}{2\pi n}\left|\sum_{t=1}^{n} x_t e^{-i\omega_j t}\right|^{2}.$$
\end{defn}

\begin{remark}
The periodogram is the natural estimate of the spectral density and it is a bad
one. It is asymptotically unbiased, but its variance does \emph{not} fall as $n$
grows: at each frequency $I(\omega_j)$ is approximately
$f(\omega_j)\chi^{2}_{2}/2$, whose variance is $f(\omega_j)^{2}$ however long the
series. Lengthening the record adds more frequencies rather than improving any
one of them.

The cure is smoothing --- averaging the periodogram over a window of
neighbouring frequencies. Averaging $m$ approximately independent ordinates cuts
the variance by a factor $m$ at the cost of blurring detail across the window,
which is the usual bias-variance trade and is chosen by how sharp the features
of interest are expected to be.
\end{remark}

\begin{note}
This is worth stating plainly because it is counter-intuitive: an estimator can
be consistent for the whole function in an integrated sense while being
inconsistent at every single point. The periodogram is the standard example, and
it is why raw periodograms look so ragged.
\end{note}

\section{State Space Models And The Kalman Filter}

An ARMA model describes a series directly. A state space model instead supposes
an unobserved process driving it, and the observations are a noisy view of that
process. The framework is more general --- every ARMA model can be written in
state space form --- and it handles missing data and time-varying parameters,
which ARMA cannot.

\subsection{The Linear Model}

\begin{defn}
A \emph{linear state space model} consists of two equations:
$$\textbf{x}_t = F\,\textbf{x}_{t-1} + \textbf{w}_t \qquad\text{(state equation)},$$
$$\textbf{y}_t = H\,\textbf{x}_t + \textbf{v}_t \qquad\text{(observation equation)},$$
where $\textbf{x}_t$ is the unobserved state, $\textbf{y}_t$ the observation, and
$\textbf{w}_t$, $\textbf{v}_t$ independent white noise with covariance matrices
$Q$ and $R$.
\end{defn}

\begin{note}
The state equation says the system evolves by its own dynamics; the observation
equation says we see it imperfectly. Separating the two is the whole idea. A
random walk observed with error --- $x_t = x_{t-1}+w_t$, $y_t = x_t+v_t$ --- is
the simplest non-trivial case, and it already cannot be written as an ARMA model
without first working out what its observable autocorrelation structure is.
\end{note}

\subsection{The Kalman Filter}

\begin{result}
Write $\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{t\mid s}$ for the expectation of $\textbf{x}_t$
given observations up to time $s$, and $P_{t\mid s}$ for its error covariance.
The Kalman filter alternates two steps.

\subhead{Predict}
$$\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{t\mid t-1} = F\,\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{t-1\mid t-1},
\qquad P_{t\mid t-1} = F\,P_{t-1\mid t-1}F^{t} + Q .$$

\subhead{Update}
With the \emph{innovation} $e_t = \textbf{y}_t - H\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{t\mid t-1}$
and the Kalman gain
$$K_t = P_{t\mid t-1}H^{t}\left(H P_{t\mid t-1}H^{t} + R\right)^{-1},$$
$$\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{t\mid t} = \widehat{\textbf{x}}_{t\mid t-1} + K_t e_t,
\qquad P_{t\mid t} = \left(I - K_tH\right)P_{t\mid t-1} .$$
\end{result}

\begin{remark}
Read the update as a weighted compromise. The innovation $e_t$ is the part of
the new observation the model did not anticipate, and the gain $K_t$ decides how
much of it to believe. If the observation noise $R$ is large the gain is small
and the filter trusts its own prediction; if the state noise $Q$ is large the
prediction is uncertain, the gain is near one, and the filter follows the data.
Everything the filter does is that trade, applied recursively.

The recursion is what makes it usable. Each step needs only the previous
estimate and the current observation, not the whole history, so the cost per
observation is constant and the filter runs in real time. That is why it is used
in navigation and tracking as much as in statistics.
\end{remark}

\begin{note}
The filter is optimal in two distinct senses that are worth keeping apart. If the
noise is Gaussian, $\widehat{\textbf{x}}_{t\mid t}$ is the conditional
expectation and therefore the best estimator of any kind. Without normality it
is still the best \emph{linear} estimator --- the same distinction as between
conditional expectation and linear regression.
\end{note}

\section{Nonlinear Models: ARCH And Volatility}

Every model so far has assumed constant variance. Financial series violate this
plainly: quiet periods and turbulent periods cluster, so the variance is itself
serially dependent even when the series is uncorrelated. ARCH models that
directly.

\begin{defn}
A process follows an \emph{ARCH}$(p)$ model if
$$X_t = \sigma_t\,\varepsilon_t,\qquad
\sigma_t^{2} = \alpha_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_i X_{t-i}^{2},$$
where $\varepsilon_t$ is independent white noise with unit variance,
$\alpha_0>0$ and $\alpha_i\geq0$. The \emph{GARCH}$(p,q)$ extension adds lagged
variances,
$$\sigma_t^{2} = \alpha_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_i X_{t-i}^{2}
+ \sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_j \sigma_{t-j}^{2}.$$
\end{defn}

\begin{result}
For the ARCH$(1)$ model with $0\leq\alpha_1<1$:
$$E(X_t) = 0,\qquad \Var(X_t) = \frac{\alpha_0}{1-\alpha_1},
\qquad \Corr\left(X_t,\,X_{t+\tau}\right) = 0 \ \text{ for } \tau\neq0,$$
but $X_t^{2}$ is autocorrelated, following an AR$(1)$ in the squares.
\end{result}

\begin{proof}
Conditioning on the past, $E\left(X_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right)
= \sigma_t E(\varepsilon_t) = 0$, so $E(X_t)=0$ by the tower property and
$X_t$ is uncorrelated with anything earlier --- which gives the zero
autocorrelation.

For the variance, $E\left(X_t^{2}\right) = E\left(\sigma_t^{2}\right)
= \alpha_0 + \alpha_1E\left(X_{t-1}^{2}\right)$. Stationarity makes both
expectations equal, so $E\left(X_t^{2}\right)(1-\alpha_1) = \alpha_0$, giving the
stated variance and requiring $\alpha_1<1$ for it to be finite and positive.

Writing $\eta_t = X_t^{2}-\sigma_t^{2}$, which has mean zero, the definition
rearranges to
$$X_t^{2} = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 X_{t-1}^{2} + \eta_t ,$$
an AR$(1)$ for the squared series.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The last line is the whole point. The series itself is uncorrelated --- it passes
every test built on the autocorrelation function --- while its squares are
strongly correlated. A series can therefore be unpredictable in level and
entirely predictable in volatility, which is what makes returns look random
while their risk does not.

This is also the practical diagnostic: plot the ACF of the squared residuals
after fitting an ARMA model. If it is flat the constant-variance assumption
stands; if it decays slowly, an ARCH or GARCH term is needed. Nothing in the
ordinary residual ACF would have revealed it.
\end{remark}

\section{Practice Problems}

\begin{problem}
Show that the MA$(1)$ process $X_t = \varepsilon_t + \theta\varepsilon_{t-1}$ has
$\rho(1) = \theta/(1+\theta^{2})$ and $\rho(\tau)=0$ for $\tau\geq2$. Deduce the
largest value $\left|\rho(1)\right|$ can take.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
With $\Var(\varepsilon)=\sigma^{2}$,
$$\gamma(0) = \left(1+\theta^{2}\right)\sigma^{2},\qquad
\gamma(1) = \theta\sigma^{2},\qquad \gamma(\tau)=0 \ (\tau\geq2),$$
the last because $X_t$ and $X_{t+\tau}$ then share no shock. Dividing,
$$\rho(1) = \frac{\theta}{1+\theta^{2}},\qquad \rho(\tau)=0 \ (\tau\geq2).$$
Differentiating, $\rho(1)$ is extremised at $\theta=\pm1$, giving
$$\left|\rho(1)\right| \leq \tfrac12 .$$
So a sample first autocorrelation beyond $0.5$ in magnitude rules out an MA$(1)$
outright, whatever $\theta$ is chosen --- a genuine identification constraint,
not merely a guideline.

Note also that $\theta$ and $1/\theta$ give the same $\rho(1)$: the
autocorrelations do not determine the model, which is why invertibility is
imposed to pick the root inside the unit circle.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
An AR$(2)$ process is $X_t = 0.5X_{t-1} + 0.3X_{t-2} + \varepsilon_t$. Determine
whether it is stationary, and find $\rho(1)$ and $\rho(2)$.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Stationarity requires the roots of the characteristic equation
$1 - 0.5z - 0.3z^{2} = 0$ to lie outside the unit circle. Solving,
$$z = \frac{0.5 \pm \sqrt{0.25 + 1.2}}{-0.6}
= \frac{0.5 \pm 1.204}{-0.6},$$
giving $z \approx -2.84$ and $z \approx 1.17$. Both exceed one in modulus, so the
process is stationary.

The Yule--Walker equations give
$$\rho(1) = \frac{\phi_1}{1-\phi_2} = \frac{0.5}{0.7} \approx 0.714 ,$$
$$\rho(2) = \phi_1\rho(1) + \phi_2 = 0.5(0.714) + 0.3 \approx 0.657 .$$
The slow decay is characteristic of an AR process: the ACF tails off rather than
cutting, and it is the PACF that would cut off after lag two.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
A series shows a clear upward trend and yearly seasonality in monthly data.
Describe the differencing needed before an ARMA model is fitted, and say what
model class results.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
A trend is removed by first differencing, $\nabla X_t = X_t - X_{t-1}$. Yearly
seasonality in monthly data is removed by seasonal differencing at lag twelve,
$\nabla_{12}X_t = X_t - X_{t-12}$. Applying both,
$$\nabla\nabla_{12}X_t = \left(X_t - X_{t-1}\right) - \left(X_{t-12}-X_{t-13}\right).$$
Fitting an ARMA model to the result is a SARIMA$(p,1,q)\times(P,1,Q)_{12}$ model.

Two cautions. Differencing more than necessary inflates the variance and
introduces artificial negative autocorrelation, so one difference of each kind is
usually enough and the ACF should be inspected before applying a second. And a
deterministic trend is better removed by regression than by differencing --- the
two are different models, and differencing a deterministic trend leaves a
non-invertible moving-average term behind.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
After fitting an ARMA model, the residual autocorrelations look like white noise
but the autocorrelations of the \emph{squared} residuals decay slowly. What does
this indicate and what should be done?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Uncorrelated residuals mean the model has captured the conditional \emph{mean}
correctly, so the ARMA orders need no revision. Correlated squared residuals mean
the conditional \emph{variance} is not constant --- large errors follow large
errors --- which violates the homoscedasticity the ARMA model assumed.

The remedy is to add a variance model: fit an ARCH or GARCH term to the
residuals, so the mean follows the ARMA structure and the variance follows the
volatility structure. Ignoring it does not bias the point forecasts, but every
interval built from the model will be wrong --- too wide in quiet periods and far
too narrow in turbulent ones, which is precisely when the interval matters.
\end{solution}
