5.1 Radian measure

Degrees are an arbitrary choice — there is nothing special about \(360\). Radians tie the measurement of angle to the circle itself.

Definition 5.1. One radian is the angle at the centre of a circle subtended by an arc equal in length to the radius.

The circumference of a circle of radius \(r\) is \(2\pi r\), which is \(2\pi \) radii, so a full revolution is \(2\pi \) radians. Hence \[2\pi \ \text {radians}=360^\circ ,\qquad \text {that is}\qquad \pi \ \text {radians}=180^\circ .\]

That single equation is the whole conversion. To go from degrees to radians multiply by \(\frac {\pi }{180}\); to go the other way multiply by \(\frac {180}{\pi }\).

Example 5.2. Express in radians: (a) \(45^\circ \), (b) \(-30^\circ \), (c) \(270^\circ \).

Solution. Multiply each by \(\frac {\pi }{180}\) and cancel.

(a) \(45\times \frac {\pi }{180}=\frac {45\pi }{180}=\frac {\pi }{4}\).

(b) \(-30\times \frac {\pi }{180}=-\frac {30\pi }{180}=-\frac {\pi }{6}\).

(c) \(270\times \frac {\pi }{180}=\frac {270\pi }{180}=\frac {3\pi }{2}\).

Leave the answer as a multiple of \(\pi \) rather than converting to a decimal; that is the form every later formula expects.

Example 5.3. Express in degrees: (a) \(\frac {5\pi }{6}\), (b) \(\frac {3\pi }{2}\), (c) \(-\frac {7\pi }{4}\).

Solution. Multiply each by \(\frac {180}{\pi }\), so the \(\pi \) cancels.

(a) \(\frac {5\pi }{6}\times \frac {180}{\pi }=\frac {5(180)}{6}=150^\circ \).

(b) \(\frac {3\pi }{2}\times \frac {180}{\pi }=\frac {3(180)}{2}=270^\circ \).

(c) \(-\frac {7\pi }{4}\times \frac {180}{\pi }=-\frac {7(180)}{4}=-315^\circ \).
