1.4 Compact Sets

Definition 1.4.1. A family \(\zeta = \{G_{\alpha }\}\) of open subsets of \(\mathbb {R}\) is said to be an open covering of a set \(A\) if every point of \(A\) belongs to some member of \(\zeta \), that is, if \(A\subseteq \bigcup _{\alpha }G_{\alpha }\).

If a subfamily \(\zeta ' \subseteq \zeta \) is also an open covering of \(A\), then \(\zeta '\) is called a subcovering of \(A\). If \(\zeta '\) consists of finitely many sets, it is called a finite subcovering of \(A\).

Example 1.4.2. Let \(x_n \longrightarrow x\), and let \(A = \{x\} \cup \{x_n: n\in \mathbb {N}\}\) consist of the terms of the sequence together with its limit. Then every open covering of \(A\) has a finite subcovering.

Proof. Let \(\zeta \) be an open covering of \(A\). The limit \(x\) belongs to some set \(G_0\) in \(\zeta \). Since \(G_0\) is open, there is an \(\varepsilon > 0\) with \((x- \varepsilon , x + \varepsilon ) \subseteq G_0\), and since \(x_n\longrightarrow x\) there exists \(m\in \mathbb {N}\) such that \(x_n\in G_0\) for every \(n > m\). Each of the remaining terms \(x_1,\dots ,x_m\) belongs to some set \(G_i \in \zeta \). Hence \(A\) is covered by the finitely many sets \(G_0, G_1, \dots , G_m\). □

Example 1.4.3. Let \(A = (1,3)\) and let \(\zeta \) be the family of open intervals \(\left ( 1 + \frac {1}{n}, 3-\frac {1}{n}\right )\), \(n\in \mathbb {N}\). Every point of \(A\) lies in one of these intervals, so \(\zeta \) is an open covering of \(A\). The intervals increase with \(n\), so the union of any finitely many of them is the largest of them, \(\left ( 1 + \frac {1}{N}, 3-\frac {1}{N}\right )\) say, which misses the point \(1+\frac {1}{N}\) of \(A\). Thus \(\zeta \) has no finite subcovering of \(A\).

Theorem 1.4.4. If \([a,b]\) is a closed interval in \(\mathbb {R}\) and \(\zeta \) is an open covering of \([a,b]\), then \(\zeta \) has a finite subcovering of \([a,b]\).

Proof. Let \(S\) be the set of all \(x\in [a,b]\) such that the interval \([a,x]\) is covered by finitely many sets of \(\zeta \). Then \(a\in S\), because \([a,a] = \{a\}\) and \(a\) belongs to some set in \(\zeta \). So \(S\) is non-empty and bounded above by \(b\); let \(M= \sup S\). Then \(a\leq M \leq b\). We show that \(M\in S\) and \(M = b\).

Since \(M\in [a,b]\), there is a set \(G^*\) in \(\zeta \) with \(M \in G^*\), and since \(G^*\) is open there is an \(\varepsilon > 0\) such that \((M - \varepsilon , M + \varepsilon ) \subseteq G^*\). As \(M\) is the supremum of \(S\), there exists \(x \in S\) with \(M - \varepsilon < x \leq M\). Since \(x\in S\), there are finitely many sets \(G_1, G_2, \dots , G_r\) in \(\zeta \) with \([a,x] \subseteq G_1 \cup G_2 \cup \dots \cup G_r\). Moreover \([x,M+\frac {\varepsilon }{2}] \subseteq (M - \varepsilon , M + \varepsilon ) \subseteq G^*\). Hence \[\left [a, M+\tfrac {\varepsilon }{2}\right ] \subseteq G^*\cup G_1 \cup G_2 \cup \dots \cup G_r .\] In particular \([a,M]\) is covered by finitely many sets of \(\zeta \), so \(M\in S\). If \(M<b\), the same display shows that every point of \(\bigl [M, \min \{M+\frac {\varepsilon }{2}, b\}\bigr ]\) lies in \(S\), contradicting \(M=\sup S\). Hence \(M = b\in S\), and \([a,b]\) has a finite subcovering. □

Definition 1.4.5. A subset \(A\) of \(\mathbb {R}\) is said to be compact if every open covering of \(A\) has a finite subcovering.

Theorem 1.4.6 (Heine–Borel Theorem). A subset \(A\) of \(\mathbb {R}\) is compact if and only if \(A\) is bounded and closed.

Proof. Suppose \(A\) is compact. The open intervals \((-n,n)\), \(n \in \mathbb {N}\), have union \(\mathbb {R}\), so they cover \(A\). By hypothesis finitely many of them cover \(A\), and since they increase with \(n\), \(A\subseteq (-m, m)\) for some \(m\in \mathbb {N}\). Consequently \(A\) is bounded.

To show that \(A\) is closed we show that no point outside \(A\) belongs to \(\overline {A}\). Let \(x\notin A\). For each \(a\in A\) put \(r_a = \frac {1}{2}|a - x| > 0\), and let \[U_a = (a-r_a, a+r_a),\qquad V_a = (x-r_a, x+r_a).\] Then \(U_a\) and \(V_a\) are disjoint open intervals with \(a\in U_a\) and \(x\in V_a\).

As \(a\) varies over \(A\), the sets \(U_a\) form an open covering of \(A\), so by compactness \(A\subseteq U_{a_1} \cup U_{a_2} \cup \dots \cup U_{a_r}\) for some \(a_1,\dots ,a_r\in A\). Let \[V = V_{a_1} \cap V_{a_2} \cap \dots \cap V_{a_r} = (x-r, x+r),\qquad r = \min \{r_{a_1},\dots ,r_{a_r}\} > 0 .\] Then \(V\) is disjoint from each \(U_{a_j}\), hence from their union, and consequently \(V\cap A = \emptyset \). Thus \(x\notin \overline {A}\). Hence \(\overline {A}\subseteq A\), and \(A\) is closed.

Conversely, suppose \(A\) is bounded and closed, and let \(\zeta \) be an open covering of \(A\). Since \(A\) is bounded, \(A\subseteq [a,b]\) for some closed interval \([a,b]\), and since \(A\) is closed, the set \(V = \mathbb {R} \setminus A\) is open by Theorem 1.3.6. The sets of \(\zeta \) cover the points of \([a,b]\) that lie in \(A\), and \(V\) covers the rest. So \(\zeta \) together with \(V\) is an open covering of \([a,b]\), and by the previous theorem finitely many of these sets, say \(V\) and \(U_1, U_2, \dots , U_r\) from \(\zeta \), cover \([a,b]\). Since \(A\) is disjoint from \(V\), it follows that \(A \subseteq U_1 \cup U_2\cup \dots \cup U_r\). Hence \(A\) is compact. □

Corollary 1.4.7. Every non-empty compact set \(A\subseteq \mathbb {R}\) has a largest and a smallest element.

Proof. By the Heine–Borel Theorem, \(A\) is bounded and closed. Let \(M = \sup A\). For each \(n\), \(M-\frac {1}{n}\) is not an upper bound of \(A\), so there is \(x_n\in A\) with \(M-\frac {1}{n}<x_n\leq M\). Then \(x_n\longrightarrow M\), and since \(A\) is closed, \(M \in A\). So \(M\) is the largest element of \(A\). Similarly \(\inf A\) belongs to \(A\) and is its smallest element. □

Example 1.4.8. The set \([0,1]\) is closed and bounded. Hence, by the Heine–Borel Theorem, it is compact.

Example 1.4.9. The interval \((0,1)\) is not compact.

Proof. For \(n\in \mathbb {N}\) let \(G_n = \left (\frac {1}{n + 1}, 1\right )\), and let \(\zeta = \{G_n : n\in \mathbb {N}\}\). If \(x\in (0,1)\), then by the Archimedean property there exists \(n \in \mathbb {N}\) with \(n > \frac {1}{x}\), so that \(\frac {1}{n+1} < \frac {1}{n} < x\) and \(x \in G_n\). Hence \(\zeta \) is an open covering of \((0,1)\).

Now take any finitely many members \(G_{n_1},\dots ,G_{n_k}\) of \(\zeta \), and let \(N = \max \{n_1,\dots ,n_k\}\). Since the sets \(G_n\) increase with \(n\), their union is \(G_N = \left (\frac {1}{N+1}, 1\right )\), which does not contain the point \(\frac {1}{N + 2}\) of \((0,1)\). So no finite subfamily of \(\zeta \) covers \((0,1)\), and \((0,1)\) is not compact. (This agrees with the Heine–Borel Theorem, since \((0,1)\) is not closed.) □

Example 1.4.10. Decide which of the following sets are compact: (a) \([0,1]\cup [2,3]\); (b) \(\{0\}\cup \left \{\frac {1}{n}: n\in \mathbb {N}\right \}\); (c) \([0,\infty )\); (d) \(\mathbb {Q}\cap [0,1]\).

Solution. We use the Heine–Borel Theorem 1.4.6.

(a) Closed (a finite union of closed sets) and bounded, so compact.

(b) Bounded, and closed because it equals \(\overline {A}\) for \(A = \left \{\frac {1}{n}\right \}\) (see the example in the section on closed sets). So it is compact.

(c) Not bounded, so not compact. Directly: the open intervals \((-1,n)\), \(n\in \mathbb {N}\), cover \([0,\infty )\), but any finitely many of them lie in \((-1,N)\) for the largest \(N\) used, which misses the point \(N\).

(d) Bounded but not closed (its closure is \([0,1]\)), so not compact. A concrete open cover without finite subcover: since \(\frac {1}{\sqrt {2}}\) is irrational, the sets \(\left (-1,\frac {1}{\sqrt {2}}-\frac {1}{n}\right )\) and \(\left (\frac {1}{\sqrt {2}}+\frac {1}{n},2\right )\), \(n\in \mathbb {N}\), cover \(\mathbb {Q}\cap [0,1]\), but finitely many of them miss the rationals close enough to \(\frac {1}{\sqrt {2}}\).

Example 1.4.11. Show that the union of two compact sets is compact, and that the intersection of any family of compact sets is compact.

Solution. Let \(K_1\) and \(K_2\) be compact, and let \(\zeta \) be an open covering of \(K_1\cup K_2\). Then \(\zeta \) covers \(K_1\), so finitely many members of \(\zeta \) cover \(K_1\), and finitely many cover \(K_2\). Together these finitely many sets cover \(K_1\cup K_2\).

If \(\{K_i\}\) is any family of compact sets, each \(K_i\) is closed and bounded, so \(\bigcap _i K_i\) is closed (Theorem 1.2.4) and bounded (it lies in any one \(K_i\)). By the Heine–Borel Theorem it is compact.

Example 1.4.12. Let \(K\) be compact and \(F\) closed. Show that \(K\cap F\) is compact. Give an example showing that a closed set need not be compact.

Solution. \(K\cap F\) is closed, as an intersection of two closed sets, and bounded, as a subset of the bounded set \(K\). By the Heine–Borel Theorem it is compact. The closed set \(\mathbb {Z}\) is not compact, since it is unbounded; the open intervals \(\left (n-\frac {1}{2},n+\frac {1}{2}\right )\), \(n\in \mathbb {Z}\), cover it, and each contains exactly one integer, so no finite subfamily covers \(\mathbb {Z}\).
