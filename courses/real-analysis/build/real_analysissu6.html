1.6 Connected Sets

Definition 1.6.1. Two subsets \(A\) and \(B\) of \(\mathbb {R}\) are said to be separated if neither has a point in common with the closure of the other, that is, if \(A\cap \overline {B} = \emptyset \) and \(\overline {A} \cap B = \emptyset \).

Note. Separated sets are disjoint, since \(A \cap B \subseteq A \cap \overline {B} = \emptyset \). The converse is false: \((-\infty ,0)\) and \([0,\infty )\) are disjoint but not separated, because \(0\) lies in the second set and in the closure of the first.

Definition 1.6.2. Let \(A\) and \(B\) be non-empty subsets of \(\mathbb {R}\). The distance between \(A\) and \(B\) is \[d(A,B) = \inf \{| a - b| : a\in A,\ b\in B\}.\]

Theorem 1.6.3. For two sets \(A\) and \(B\) to be separated it is sufficient, but not necessary, that \(d(A,B)>0\).

Proof. Suppose \(d(A,B) = K > 0\). Then \(| a - b| \geq K\) for every \(a\in A\) and \(b\in B\). So if \(a\in A\), the neighbourhood \(\left (a - \frac {K}{2}, a + \frac {K}{2}\right )\) of \(a\) contains no point of \(B\), and by Theorem 1.2.15(iv), \(a\notin \overline {B}\). Therefore \(A \cap \overline {B} = \emptyset \). Similarly \(\overline {A} \cap B = \emptyset \), and \(A\) and \(B\) are separated.

The condition is not necessary: the sets \((-\infty ,0)\) and \((0,\infty )\) are separated, since neither closure meets the other set, but their distance is \(0\). □

Theorem 1.6.4. Let \(A\) and \(B\) be separated subsets of \(\mathbb {R}\), and let \(A_1\subseteq A\) and \(B_1\subseteq B\). Then \(A_1\) and \(B_1\) are separated.

Proof. Since \(B_1\subseteq B\), we have \(\overline {B}_1 \subseteq \overline {B}\) (every limit point of \(B_1\) is a limit point of \(B\)). Hence \[A_1 \cap \overline {B}_1 \subseteq A \cap \overline {B} = \emptyset .\] Similarly \(\overline {A}_1 \cap B_1 \subseteq \overline {A}\cap B = \emptyset \). Hence \(A_1\) and \(B_1\) are separated. □

Theorem 1.6.5. Two closed subsets of \(\mathbb {R}\) are separated if and only if they are disjoint.

Proof. Let \(F_1\) and \(F_2\) be closed, so that \(\overline {F}_1 = F_1\) and \(\overline {F}_2 = F_2\). Then \[F_1\cap \overline {F}_2 = F_1\cap F_2 \qquad \text {and}\qquad \overline {F}_1\cap F_2 = F_1\cap F_2 ,\] so the two conditions for being separated both reduce to \(F_1\cap F_2 = \emptyset \). □

Theorem 1.6.6. If an open set \(O\) is the union of two separated sets \(A\) and \(B\), then \(A\) and \(B\) are open.

Proof. Let \(x \in A\). Since \(O\) is open, there is an \(r>0\) with \((x - r, x + r)\subseteq O\). Since \(x\notin \overline {B}\), there is an \(r_1\) with \(0<r_1 \leq r\) such that \((x - r_1, x + r_1)\) contains no point of \(B\). This interval lies in \(O = A\cup B\), so it lies in \(A\). Thus every point of \(A\) is an interior point of \(A\), and \(A\) is open. Similarly \(B\) is open. □

Theorem 1.6.7. If a closed set \(F\) is the union of two separated sets \(A\) and \(B\), then \(A\) and \(B\) are closed.

Proof. Since \(A\subseteq F\) and \(F\) is closed, \(\overline {A}\subseteq F = A\cup B\) by Theorem 1.2.15(ii). But \(\overline {A}\cap B = \emptyset \), so \(\overline {A} \subseteq A\). Hence \(\overline {A} = A\) and \(A\) is closed. Similarly \(B\) is closed. □

Definition 1.6.8. A subset \(A\) of \(\mathbb {R}\) is said to be disconnected if it is the union of two non-empty separated sets.

There is nothing special about the word “two”: if \(A = B\cup C\cup D\), where \(B\), \(C\) and \(D\) are non-empty and each pair is separated, then \(A = B\cup ( C\cup D)\), where \(B\) and \(C\cup D\) are separated, so \(A\) is disconnected.

Equivalently, one can show that \(A\) is disconnected if and only if \(A\subseteq O_1 \cup O_2\) for some open sets \(O_1\) and \(O_2\) such that \(A\cap O_1\) and \(A\cap O_2\) are non-empty but \(A\cap O_1 \cap O_2\) is empty. The same holds with closed sets \(F_1\) and \(F_2\) in place of \(O_1\) and \(O_2\).

Definition 1.6.9. A subset \(A\) of \(\mathbb {R}\) is said to be connected if it is not disconnected, that is, if \(A\) is not the union of two non-empty separated sets.

Theorem 1.6.10. A subset \(A\) of \(\mathbb {R}\) is connected if and only if \(z\in A\) whenever \(x,y \in A\) and \(x < z < y\).

Proof. Suppose there are \(x, y \in A\) and \(z \in (x,y)\) with \(z\notin A\). Let \(P = A \cap (-\infty , z)\) and \(Q = A\cap (z, \infty )\). Since \(z\notin A\), \(A = P \cup Q\), and \(P\) and \(Q\) are non-empty because \(x\in P\) and \(y \in Q\). Since \(\overline {P}\subseteq (-\infty ,z]\) and \(\overline {Q}\subseteq [z,\infty )\), neither set meets the closure of the other, so \(P\) and \(Q\) are separated. Hence \(A\) is not connected. This proves that a connected set has the stated property.

Conversely, suppose \(A\) has the property but is not connected, say \(A = P\cup Q\) with \(P\) and \(Q\) non-empty and separated. Take \(x\in P\) and \(y \in Q\), and assume without loss of generality that \(x<y\). Let \(z = \sup (P\cap [x,y])\). Then \(x\leq z\leq y\), so \(z\in A\) by the property, and \(z\in \overline {P}\), so \(z\notin Q\) (as \(\overline {P}\cap Q = \emptyset \)). Hence \(z\in P\), and in particular \(z\neq y\), so \(z<y\). Since \(z \in P\) and \(P\cap \overline {Q} = \emptyset \), there is a \(\delta >0\) such that \((z-\delta , z+\delta )\) contains no point of \(Q\). Choose \(w\) with \(z<w<\min \{z+\delta , y\}\). Then \(w\in A\) by the property and \(w\notin Q\), so \(w\in P\cap [x,y]\) with \(w>z\), contradicting the choice of \(z\). Hence \(A\) is connected. □

Theorem 1.6.11. A subset \(A\) of \(\mathbb {R}\) is connected if and only if \(A\) is an interval.

Proof. A subset of \(\mathbb {R}\) is an interval (of one of the nine kinds in Section 1.1, or empty, or a single point) exactly when it contains every point lying between two of its points. By Theorem 1.6.10, this is exactly the condition for \(A\) to be connected. □

Theorem 1.6.12. If two connected sets are not separated, their union is connected.

Proof. Let \(A\) and \(B\) be connected sets which are not separated, and suppose \(A\cup B = C \cup D\), where \(C\) and \(D\) are non-empty separated sets. Then \[A = (A\cap C) \cup (A\cap D),\] and \(A\cap C\) and \(A\cap D\) are separated, being subsets of \(C\) and \(D\). Since \(A\) is connected, one of them is empty, so \(A\subseteq C\) or \(A\subseteq D\). Likewise \(B\subseteq C\) or \(B\subseteq D\).

If \(A\) and \(B\) lay in the same set, say \(C\), then \(D\) would be empty, which is not the case. So one of them lies in \(C\) and the other in \(D\), say \(A\subseteq C\) and \(B\subseteq D\). Then \[A \cap \overline {B} \subseteq C \cap \overline {D} = \emptyset \qquad \text {and}\qquad \overline {A}\cap B \subseteq \overline {C} \cap D = \emptyset ,\] so \(A\) and \(B\) are separated, contrary to hypothesis. Hence \(A\cup B\) is connected. □

Corollary 1.6.13. If two connected sets have a non-empty intersection, their union is connected.

Proof. Let \(A\) and \(B\) be connected with \(A\cap B\neq \emptyset \). Since \(A\cap B \subseteq A\cap \overline {B}\), the set \(A\cap \overline {B}\) is non-empty, so \(A\) and \(B\) are not separated. The result follows from the theorem. □

Example 1.6.14. Decide whether the sets in each pair are separated, and whether their union is connected: (a) \((0,1)\) and \((1,2)\); (b) \([0,1)\) and \([1,2]\); (c) \([0,1]\) and \([2,3]\).

Solution. (a) \(\overline {(0,1)} = [0,1]\) does not meet \((1,2)\), and \(\overline {(1,2)} = [1,2]\) does not meet \((0,1)\). So the sets are separated, and their union \((0,1)\cup (1,2)\) is disconnected. (It omits \(1\), so it is not an interval.)

(b) \(\overline {[0,1)} = [0,1]\) meets \([1,2]\) in the point \(1\), so the sets are not separated. Both are connected, so their union \([0,2]\) is connected by the theorem on unions of non-separated connected sets.

(c) The sets are closed and disjoint, hence separated, and the union is disconnected.

Example 1.6.15. Show that \(\mathbb {Q}\) is disconnected, and that the irrational numbers \(\mathbb {Q}^C\) form a disconnected set.

Solution. Let \(P = \mathbb {Q}\cap (-\infty ,\sqrt {2})\) and \(Q = \mathbb {Q}\cap (\sqrt {2},\infty )\). Since \(\sqrt {2}\notin \mathbb {Q}\), \(\mathbb {Q} = P\cup Q\). Both sets are non-empty (\(0\in P\), \(2\in Q\)), and \(\overline {P}\subseteq (-\infty ,\sqrt {2}]\) does not meet \(Q\), while \(\overline {Q}\subseteq [\sqrt {2},\infty )\) does not meet \(P\). So \(\mathbb {Q}\) is disconnected.

For the irrationals, cut at the rational number \(0\) instead: \(\mathbb {Q}^C = \bigl (\mathbb {Q}^C\cap (-\infty ,0)\bigr )\cup \bigl (\mathbb {Q}^C\cap (0,\infty )\bigr )\), and the same argument applies. Both conclusions also follow from Theorem 1.6.10, since neither set is an interval.

Example 1.6.16. Let \(A\subseteq \mathbb {R}\) be connected and \(A\subseteq B\subseteq \overline {A}\). Show that \(B\) is connected. Deduce that the closure of an interval is an interval.

Solution. Suppose \(B = C\cup D\) with \(C\) and \(D\) non-empty and separated. Then \(A = (A\cap C)\cup (A\cap D)\) with \(A\cap C\) and \(A\cap D\) separated, so, \(A\) being connected, one of them is empty; say \(A\subseteq C\). Then \(B\subseteq \overline {A}\subseteq \overline {C}\), so \(D = B\cap D\subseteq \overline {C}\cap D = \emptyset \), a contradiction. Hence \(B\) is connected.

If \(I\) is an interval, it is connected, so \(\overline {I}\) is connected and therefore an interval. For example \(\overline {(a,b)} = [a,b]\).
