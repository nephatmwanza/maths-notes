13.6 Problems

Problem 13.6.1. Two age groups have, among the exposed, \(10\) deaths in \(5000\) person-years and \(30\) in \(3000\); among the unexposed, \(20\) in \(20{,}000\) and \(15\) in \(3000\). Compute the crude rate ratio, the CMF with the combined population as standard, the SMR of the exposed against the unexposed rates, and the Mantel–Haenszel rate ratio.

Show solution

Solution. The rates per \(1000\) are \(2\) and \(10\) (exposed), \(1\) and \(5\) (unexposed): the ratio is \(2\) in both groups. Crude: \((40/8000)/(35/23{,}000) = 3.29\). Combined standard \(25{,}000\) and \(6000\), weights \(0.806\) and \(0.194\): \(\text {DSR}_1 = 3.548\), \(\text {DSR}_0 = 1.774\), \(\text {CMF} = 2.00\). SMR: \(E = 5000 \times 0.001 + 3000 \times 0.005 = 20\) and \(O = 40\), so \(\text {SMR} = 2.00\). Mantel–Haenszel: numerator \(10 \times 20{,}000/25{,}000 + 30 \times 3000/6000 = 8 + 15 = 23\), denominator \(20 \times 5000/25{,}000 + 15 \times 3000/6000 = 4 + 7.5 = 11.5\), ratio \(2.00\). With an exactly homogeneous ratio every method recovers it.

Problem 13.6.2. In a matched-pair study with a binary exposure, the unconditional likelihood has an intercept \(\alpha _i\) for each pair. Show that maximising over the \(\alpha _i\) and then over \(\psi = e^\beta \) gives \(\hat \psi = (n_{10}/n_{01})^2\).

Show solution

Solution. Concordant pairs can be fitted perfectly by their intercepts and carry no information about \(\beta \). For a discordant pair with the case exposed, the likelihood is \(\psi u/\{(1 + \psi u)(1 + u)\}\) with \(u = e^{\alpha _i}\); its derivative in \(u\) vanishes when \(1 - \psi u^2 = 0\), so \(u = \psi ^{-1/2}\), and the maximum is \(\psi /(1 + \sqrt \psi )^2\). For a pair with the control exposed, the likelihood is \(u/\{(1 + u)(1 + \psi u)\}\), maximised at the same \(u\), with value \(1/(1 + \sqrt \psi )^2\). The profile log-likelihood is therefore \(n_{10}\log \psi - 2(n_{10} + n_{01})\log (1 + \sqrt \psi )\). With \(\phi = \sqrt \psi \), its derivative is \(2n_{10}/\phi - 2(n_{10} + n_{01})/(1 + \phi )\), zero when \(n_{10}(1 + \phi ) = (n_{10} + n_{01})\phi \), that is \(\phi = n_{10}/n_{01}\). So \(\hat \psi = (n_{10}/n_{01})^2\), the square of the consistent conditional estimate.

Problem 13.6.3. Verify the weights in Example 13.2.1 for the SMR. Which stratum carries the most weight, and why?

Show solution

Solution. The expected deaths among the exposed at unexposed rates are \(T_{1k}r_{0k}\): \(12{,}000 \times 0.000267 = 3.2\), \(15{,}000 \times 0.0009 = 13.5\), \(10{,}000 \times 0.00325 = 32.5\) and \(3000 \times 0.0075 = 22.5\), total \(71.7\). The weights are \(0.045\), \(0.188\), \(0.453\) and \(0.314\). The \(50\)–\(64\) stratum carries the most: it has a large share of the exposed person-time and a fairly high death rate, so it produces the most expected deaths. The youngest stratum, with the most person-time in all but a very low rate, counts for little.

Problem 13.6.4. In Example 13.3.1, explain why the SMR interval \((1.31,\ 1.53)\) is much narrower than the Mantel–Haenszel interval \((1.15,\ 1.76)\), and suggest how the SMR interval could be corrected.

Show solution

Solution. The SMR interval treats the non-smokers’ rates as known exactly. Its width reflects only the Poisson variation in the \(630\) deaths among smokers, while the non-smokers contribute just \(101\) deaths and their rates are much less precise. The Mantel–Haenszel variance includes both sources. A corrected SMR variance adds the variance of the expected count: \(\Var (\log \text {SMR}) \approx 1/O + \Var (E)/E^2\), with \(\Var (E) = \sum _k T_{1k}^2 D_{0k}/T_{0k}^2\) from the Poisson variances of the reference rates.

Problem 13.6.5. A colleague reports that “the four adjustment methods gave rate ratios from \(1.36\) to \(1.43\), so the result is robust”. Comment, with reference to Example 13.3.1.

Show solution

Solution. Agreement between methods shows that the result does not depend on the choice of weights, which is reassuring only if the stratum ratios are similar. In Example 13.3.1 they range from \(5.7\) to \(0.9\), and the methods agree because they all weight the older strata heavily. A different population with more young people would give different summaries from every method. The appropriate check of robustness is a test of homogeneity, which fails here, and the appropriate report is the age-specific ratios.
