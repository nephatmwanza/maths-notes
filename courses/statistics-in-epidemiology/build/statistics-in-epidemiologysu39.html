7.1 Random error and bias

An estimate of a measure of association can be wrong for two kinds of reason. Random error comes from sampling variation; it shrinks as the study grows and is described by a confidence interval. Systematic error, or bias, is a difference between what the study estimates on average and the true value in the source population; it does not shrink with size, and no confidence interval reflects it. A study is internally valid if it is free of bias for the population it sampled. Whether its result applies to other populations, its external validity or generalisability, is a separate and usually more judgemental question.

Biases are traditionally grouped into three kinds:

1.
selection bias, from the way subjects come to be in the study or in the analysis;
2.
information bias, from errors in measuring exposure, disease or other variables;
3.
confounding, from the mixing of the effect of exposure with the effects of other factors.

The first two are flaws in how the data were obtained. The third is a property of the source population, present even in perfectly collected data, and it is the one the methods of the following sections are designed to remove.
