14.1 Problems

Problem 14.1.1. After a wedding, \(40\) of \(120\) guests develop gastroenteritis. Of \(70\) guests who ate the chicken, \(35\) became ill; of the \(50\) who did not, \(5\) did. Of \(60\) who ate the potato salad, \(22\) became ill, against \(18\) of the \(60\) who did not.

(a)
Compute the attack rates and risk ratios for each food. Which is the likely vehicle?
(b)
Give a \(95\%\) confidence interval for the risk ratio for chicken and test for association.
(c)
What proportion of the cases is attributable to the chicken? Check the answer with Miettinen’s formula.

Show solution

Solution. (a) Chicken: \(35/70 = 0.50\) against \(5/50 = 0.10\), \(\RR = 5.0\). Potato salad: \(22/60 = 0.37\) against \(18/60 = 0.30\), \(\RR = 1.22\). The chicken is the likely vehicle; the weak association with the salad may come from guests who ate both.

(b) \(\text {SE}(\log \widehat {\RR }) = \sqrt {1/35 - 1/70 + 1/5 - 1/50} = 0.441\), so the interval is \(5.0\,e^{\pm 0.864} = (2.1,\ 11.9)\). By Proposition 4.4.1, \(X^2 = 120(35 \times 45 - 5 \times 35)^2/(70 \times 50 \times 40 \times 80) = 21.0\), \(P < 0.0001\).

(c) The overall attack rate is \(40/120 = 0.333\), so \(\text {PAF} = (0.333 - 0.10)/0.333 = 0.70\). By Miettinen, \(P_c = 35/40 = 0.875\) and \(\text {AF}_E = 4/5 = 0.8\), giving \(0.875 \times 0.8 = 0.70\). Seven in ten cases would not have occurred without the chicken; the other cases arose from the background or from another source.

Problem 14.1.2. A case-control study of \(200\) cases and \(200\) controls finds \(90\) exposed cases and \(60\) exposed controls. A validation study shows that the exposure questionnaire has sensitivity \(0.85\) and specificity \(0.95\) in both groups.

(a)
Compute the observed odds ratio and the odds ratio corrected for misclassification.
(b)
Explain the direction of the difference using a theorem of the course.
(c)
Why might the misclassification in fact be differential, and what then?

Show solution

Solution. (a) Observed: \((90 \times 140)/(110 \times 60) = 1.91\). Corrected exposed counts: \((90 - 0.05 \times 200)/0.80 = 100\) among cases and \((60 - 10)/0.80 = 62.5\) among controls, so the corrected odds ratio is \((100 \times 137.5)/(100 \times 62.5) = 2.20\).

(b) Nondifferential misclassification of a binary exposure biases the odds ratio towards \(1\) (Theorem 2.4.3), so the corrected value lies further from \(1\).

(c) If exposure is recalled at interview after diagnosis, cases may report it more completely than controls (recall bias), giving a higher sensitivity among cases. The bias could then be away from the null, and the correction must use separate sensitivities and specificities for cases and controls, which needs a validation study within each group.

Problem 14.1.3. A cohort study gives, for workers under \(50\), \(12\) cancer deaths in \(8000\) person-years among the exposed and \(10\) in \(16{,}000\) among the unexposed; for workers aged \(50\) and over, \(40\) in \(4000\) and \(24\) in \(4800\).

(a)
Compute the crude and the stratum-specific rate ratios. Is there confounding by age?
(b)
Compute the Mantel–Haenszel rate ratio with a \(95\%\) interval, and the Mantel–Haenszel test.
(c)
Compute the SMR of the exposed using the unexposed rates as standard.
(d)
Is a single summary appropriate?

Show solution

Solution. (a) Rates per \(1000\) person-years: under \(50\), \(1.5\) and \(0.625\) (ratio \(2.4\)); \(50\) and over, \(10\) and \(5\) (ratio \(2.0\)). Crude: \((52/12{,}000)/(34/20{,}800) = 2.65\). The exposed are older (\(33\%\) of their person-time over \(50\), against \(23\%\) of the unexposed), and death rates rise with age, so age inflates the crude ratio.

(b) The numerator is \(12 \times 16{,}000/24{,}000 + 40 \times 4800/8800 = 8.0 + 21.82 = 29.82\), and the denominator \(10 \times 8000/24{,}000 + 24 \times 4000/8800 = 3.33 + 10.91 = 14.24\), so \(\widehat {\text {IRR}}_{\text {MH}} = 2.09\). The variance of its log is \(\sum _k D_k T_{1k}T_{0k}/T_k^2\) divided by the product of numerator and denominator: \((22 \times 8000 \times 16{,}000/24{,}000^2 + 64 \times 4000 \times 4800/8800^2)/(29.82 \times 14.24) = 0.0489\), \(\text {SE} = 0.221\), interval \((1.36,\ 3.23)\). The test: observed \(52\) exposed deaths, expected \(36.42\), variance \(20.76\), so \(X^2 = (52 - 36.42)^2/20.76 = 11.7\).

(c) Expected deaths among the exposed at the unexposed rates: \(8000 \times 0.000625 + 4000 \times 0.005 = 5 + 20 = 25\), so \(\text {SMR} = 52/25 = 2.08\).

(d) The stratum ratios, \(2.4\) and \(2.0\), are close; Woolf’s homogeneity statistic is \(0.13\) on \(1\) degree of freedom. A summary of about \(2.1\) is appropriate, and all the methods give it.

Problem 14.1.4. A matched case-control study has \(150\) pairs. In \(36\) pairs only the case was exposed, in \(18\) only the control.

(a)
Estimate the odds ratio, test for association, and give an exact \(95\%\) interval.
(b)
A new study is to detect an odds ratio of \(2\) with power \(0.90\) at \(\alpha = 0.05\). If \(35\%\) of pairs are discordant, as here, how many pairs are needed?

Show solution

Solution. (a) \(\hat \psi = 36/18 = 2.0\). McNemar: \((36 - 18)^2/54 = 6.0\), \(P = 0.014\). The exact Clopper–Pearson interval for \(\pi = \psi /(1 + \psi )\) from \(36\) of \(54\) is \((0.525,\ 0.789)\), which transforms to \((1.11,\ 3.74)\).

(b) By Theorem 6.3.1 the discordant pairs are binomial with \(\pi = 2/3\) under the alternative and \(\tfrac 12\) under the null. The one-sample version of Theorem 6.4.1 needs \[ n_d = \frac {\bigl (1.960 \times 0.5 + 1.282\sqrt {(2/3)(1/3)}\bigr )^2}{(2/3 - 1/2)^2} = 90.3 , \] so \(91\) discordant pairs, and about \(91/0.35 = 260\) pairs in all.

Problem 14.1.5. An observational study reports a risk ratio of \(1.8\) for an exposure. A critic suggests an unmeasured confounder.

(a)
If the confounder triples the risk and is present in \(20\%\) of the unexposed, how common must it be among the exposed to explain the whole association?
(b)
Could a confounder with risk ratio \(1.5\) explain it?

Show solution

Solution. (a) By Theorem 7.4.2 with \(\theta = 1\), \(1.8 = (1 + 2\pi _1)/(1 + 2 \times 0.2)\), so \(1 + 2\pi _1 = 2.52\) and \(\pi _1 = 0.76\): the confounder would have to be nearly four times as common among the exposed. Whether that is plausible depends on what the confounder is.

(b) No. By Theorem 7.4.4, a confounder that explains a risk ratio of \(1.8\) must have \(R_C \geq 1.8\). Directly, \(1.8 = (1 + 0.5\pi _1)/(1 + 0.5\pi _0)\) would need \(\pi _1 \geq 1.6\) even with \(\pi _0 = 0\), which is impossible.

Problem 14.1.6. A serological survey of \(2000\) adults finds \(64\) positive on an assay with sensitivity \(0.90\) and specificity \(0.99\). The condition lasts on average four years. Assuming a steady state, estimate the prevalence and the incidence rate.

Show solution

Solution. The apparent prevalence is \(0.032\). By Theorem 2.2.4, \(\hat p = (0.032 + 0.99 - 1)/0.89 = 0.0247\). By Theorem 3.4.1, \(\hat I = \hat p/\{(1 - \hat p)\bar D\} = 0.0247/(0.9753 \times 4) = 0.0063\) per person-year, about \(6.3\) per \(1000\). Ignoring the misclassification would give \(0.032/(0.968 \times 4) = 8.3\) per \(1000\), a third too high.

Problem 14.1.7. A disease has annual incidence \(2\) per \(100{,}000\). An exposure is carried by \(30\%\) of the population, and an odds ratio of \(1.5\) is to be detected with power \(0.80\) at \(\alpha = 0.05\).

(a)
How many cases and controls are needed in a case-control study with two controls per case?
(b)
Roughly how many person-years of follow-up would a cohort study need to accumulate the same number of cases? Comment.

Show solution

Solution. (a) By (6.1), \(q_1 = 1.5 \times 0.3/(1 + 0.3 \times 0.5) = 0.391\). Theorem 6.4.1 with \(k = 2\) gives \(316\) cases and \(632\) controls.

(b) \(316\) cases at \(2\) per \(100{,}000\) person-years need about \(16\) million person-years: a million people for sixteen years. The case-control study reaches the same cases by finding them where they already are, which is why it is the design of choice for rare diseases.

Problem 14.1.8. A nested case-control study is drawn from a cohort by density sampling: for each case, two controls are chosen from the members of the cohort at risk at the time of the case and matched on age. Explain why the Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio over the matched sets estimates the age-adjusted incidence rate ratio, and why no rare disease assumption is needed.

Show solution

Solution. Each matched set is drawn from a risk set at one moment and one age. Within such a set, the chance that the case is exposed rather than one of the controls depends on the exposed and unexposed hazards at that moment, in the ratio \(\lambda _1/\lambda _0\), exactly as in the proof of Theorem 4.6.1, applied to an instant instead of a period. If the rate ratio is constant across ages and times, every set is a stratum with the same odds ratio \(\lambda _1/\lambda _0\), and by Theorem 10.1.3 the Mantel–Haenszel estimator over many small strata is consistent for it (Problem 10.8.3 gives its form for \(1{:}2\) sets). The controls are sampled from people at risk, not from people who remained disease-free to the end, so the rarity of the disease plays no part. Matching on age adjusts for it, provided the analysis keeps the sets.
