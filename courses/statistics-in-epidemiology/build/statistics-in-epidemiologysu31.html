5.6 Problems

Problem 5.6.1. Show that Levin’s and Miettinen’s formulas agree, by expressing \(P_c\) in terms of \(P_e\) and \(\RR \).

Show solution

Solution. \(P_c = P_e p_1/p = P_e\RR \,p_0/[p_0\{1 + P_e(\RR - 1)\}] = P_e\RR /\{1 + P_e(\RR - 1)\}\). Then \[ P_c\,\frac {\RR - 1}{\RR } = \frac {P_e\RR }{1 + P_e(\RR - 1)} \cdot \frac {\RR - 1}{\RR } = \frac {P_e(\RR - 1)}{1 + P_e(\RR - 1)} , \] which is Levin’s formula.

Problem 5.6.2. A case-control study gives \(a = 40\) exposed and \(b = 60\) unexposed cases, and \(c = 30\) exposed and \(d = 170\) unexposed controls. Estimate the odds ratio, the attributable fraction among the exposed and the PAF with a \(95\%\) confidence interval.

Show solution

Solution. \(\widehat {\OR } = (40 \times 170)/(60 \times 30) = 3.78\) and \(\widehat {\text {AF}}_E = 2.78/3.78 = 0.735\). From Theorem 5.3.1, \(1 - \widehat {\text {PAF}} = (60/100)/(170/200) = 0.706\), so \(\widehat {\text {PAF}} = 0.294\) \((= 0.4 \times 0.735)\). The standard error of \(\log (1 - \widehat {\text {PAF}})\) is \(\sqrt {40/(100 \times 60) + 30/(200 \times 170)} = 0.0869\), so \(1 - \text {PAF}\) lies in \(0.706\,e^{\pm 0.170} = (0.595,\ 0.837)\) and the PAF in \((0.163,\ 0.405)\).

Problem 5.6.3. An exposure has three levels, called none, light and heavy, with population prevalences \(0.5\), \(0.3\), \(0.2\) and risk ratios \(1\), \(2\), \(4\).

(a)
Find the PAF directly and by the formula for several levels.
(b)
Collapse light and heavy into “exposed”. Find the risk ratio for exposed against unexposed and the PAF by Levin’s formula. Comment.

Show solution

Solution. (a) Relative to the risk \(p_0\) at level none, the population risk is \(p_0(0.5 + 0.3 \times 2 + 0.2 \times 4) = 1.9p_0\), so \(\text {PAF} = 0.9/1.9 = 0.474\). The proportions of cases at the three levels are \(0.5/1.9\), \(0.6/1.9\), \(0.8/1.9\), that is \(0.263\), \(0.316\), \(0.421\). Then \(0.316 \times \tfrac 12 + 0.421 \times \tfrac 34 = 0.158 + 0.316 = 0.474\).

(b) The exposed have risk \(p_0(0.3 \times 2 + 0.2 \times 4)/0.5 = 2.8p_0\), so \(\RR = 2.8\), and with \(P_e = 0.5\) Levin gives \(0.5 \times 1.8/(1 + 0.5 \times 1.8) = 0.9/1.9 = 0.474\). Collapsing does no harm here, because the reference group is unchanged and the collapsed risk ratio is the correct average. It goes wrong if the collapsed ratio is taken from elsewhere (another population with a different mix of light and heavy exposure), or if the question is the effect of reducing heavy exposure to light, which only the separate ratios can answer.

Problem 5.6.4. In Example 5.4.1, find the fraction of cases among people with both exposures that is attributable to the combination, and explain why the separate PAFs sum to more than \(1\).

Show solution

Solution. People with both exposures have risk \(0.05\) against \(0.001\) with neither, so \((0.05 - 0.001)/0.05 = 98\%\) of their cases are attributable to the combination. They make up \(5\%\) of the population and contribute \(0.05 \times 0.05 = 0.0025\) of the population risk of \(0.0077\), a third of all cases. Each of these cases is prevented by removing smoking or by removing the occupational exposure, so it is counted in both PAFs. The sum exceeds \(1\) by the share of cases that either removal would prevent.

Problem 5.6.5. In a five-year trial with \(1000\) patients per arm, the risk of a stroke is \(0.12\) on placebo and \(0.08\) on treatment. Find the number needed to treat and a \(95\%\) interval for it.

Show solution

Solution. \(\RD = 0.04\), \(\text {NNT} = 25\). \(\text {SE}(\RD ) = \sqrt {0.08 \times 0.92/1000 + 0.12 \times 0.88/1000} = 0.0134\), so \(\RD \in (0.0138,\ 0.0662)\). Inverting, the NNT lies between \(1/0.0662 = 15\) and \(1/0.0138 = 73\). The inversion is valid only because the interval for \(\RD \) excludes \(0\); if it did not, the interval for the NNT would run from a number needed to treat, through infinity, to a number needed to harm.

Problem 5.6.6. Show that \(\text {PAF} \leq \text {AF}_E\) and \(\text {PAF} \leq P_c\), with equality in the first if and only if everyone with the disease is exposed. When is the PAF close to \(\text {AF}_E\)?

Show solution

Solution. By Miettinen’s formula \(\text {PAF} = P_c\,\text {AF}_E\) with \(0 \leq P_c \leq 1\) and \(0 \leq \text {AF}_E \leq 1\) (for \(\RR \geq 1\)), so \(\text {PAF}\) is at most each factor. Equality \(\text {PAF} = \text {AF}_E\) (with \(\text {AF}_E > 0\)) requires \(P_c = 1\). The PAF is close to \(\text {AF}_E\) when almost all cases are exposed, as for lung cancer and smoking, where \(\text {AF}_E\) is about \(0.9\) and the PAF is about \(0.8\) to \(0.9\).
