\section{Introduction to epidemiological studies}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{What epidemiology studies}

\begin{defn}
\textbf{Epidemiology} is the study of the distribution and the determinants of health-related states and events in
specified populations, and the application of that study to the control of health problems.
\end{defn}

Three words in the definition carry the subject. \emph{Distribution} asks who becomes ill, where and when; answering it is \textbf{descriptive
epidemiology}. \emph{Determinants} asks why: which exposures, behaviours, genes or circumstances change the chance of becoming ill. Answering
it is \textbf{analytic epidemiology}, and almost all of this course is about it. \emph{Populations} says the unit of interest is a group, not a
patient. A clinician asks whether this patient has the disease; an epidemiologist asks how often the disease occurs in a group and whether
it occurs more often in one group than another.

The word \textbf{exposure} is used for any characteristic whose effect on disease is being studied: a drug, an occupation, smoking, a gene,
a water supply. The \textbf{outcome} is usually the occurrence of a disease or death, coded $D$ (disease) and $\bar D$ (no disease); exposure
is coded $E$ and $\bar E$. Both may be more than two-valued, but the binary case carries every idea and is used throughout.

The statistical problems of the subject come from the fact that in most epidemiological work the investigator cannot assign the exposure.
Nobody can be told to smoke for twenty years or to drink from a contaminated well. The data are \emph{observational}: people chose, or
were given by circumstance, their exposures, and whatever led them to it may also affect their disease. Separating the effect of the
exposure from the effects of everything that travels with it is the central task, and the methods of the later sections (standardisation,
stratification, the Mantel--Haenszel procedure, regression) are all tools for it.

\begin{exa}[Snow and the London water companies]
\label{exa:snow}
In 1854 two companies supplied water to the same districts of south London, their pipes running down the same streets. The Southwark and
Vauxhall Company drew its water from the Thames below the sewage outfalls; the Lambeth Company had moved its intake upstream. John Snow
counted cholera deaths in the first seven weeks of the epidemic by the company supplying each house, and the deaths per
$10{,}000$ houses:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\hline
Supply & Houses & Deaths & Per $10{,}000$ houses \\
\hline
Southwark and Vauxhall & $40{,}046$ & $1{,}263$ & $315$ \\
Lambeth & $26{,}107$ & $98$ & $38$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The death rate in houses supplied by Southwark and Vauxhall was
\[
\frac{1263/40046}{98/26107} = \frac{0.03154}{0.00375} = 8.4
\]
times that in houses supplied by Lambeth.

Snow's argument for causation did not rest on the size of the ratio alone. He pointed out that the two supplies were intermixed house by
house, so that the customers of the two companies were alike in wealth, occupation, crowding and every other respect he could think of,
and differed only in their water. In modern language the comparison was \emph{exchangeable} (Section~\ref{sec:causation}): a natural
experiment in which circumstance had assigned the exposure almost at random. The table also shows the first data problem of the
subject. The denominator is houses, not people, because houses were what Snow could count; a rate per person, or per person-week at
risk, would need the number of residents, which he did not have.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Disease occurrence in populations}
\label{sec:occurrence}

\subhead{Populations and time}

Disease occurs in people over time, and every measure in the course has a population and a period attached. A \textbf{closed} population
(a \textbf{cohort}) gains no members after it is defined: the people enrolled in a trial, the children born in one hospital in 2010, the
workers employed at a plant on a given date. It loses members only by death or by the event studied. An \textbf{open} population, such as
the residents of a city, gains members by birth and immigration and loses them by death and emigration; individuals enter and leave
throughout the period of observation.

A person is \textbf{at risk} of a disease while they are free of it and capable of getting it. Women who have had a hysterectomy are not at
risk of uterine cancer; a child immune after measles is not at risk of measles. The population at risk is the right denominator for every
measure of how often a disease begins.

\subhead{The natural history of disease}

For a chronic disease the sequence is: exposure begins; after an \textbf{induction period} the disease process starts; after a
\textbf{latent period} it becomes detectable, then symptomatic, and is diagnosed; it ends in recovery, chronic illness or death. The
epidemiologist usually sees only the date of diagnosis. Two consequences recur. Exposure measured shortly before diagnosis may be the wrong
exposure, because the relevant one acted years earlier; and a disease that is present but not yet diagnosed makes a person look disease-free
when they are not.

\subhead{Three measures of occurrence}

There are three basic ways of saying how often a disease occurs, studied fully in Section~\ref{sec:frequency}. Here they are introduced
informally, because the study designs differ in which of them they can estimate.

\begin{enumerate}
\item The \textbf{risk} (cumulative incidence) of a disease over a period is the probability that a person at risk at the start develops it
during the period. It is a proportion, between $0$ and $1$, and means nothing without the length of the period: the five-year risk is larger
than the one-year risk.
\item The \textbf{rate} (incidence rate, incidence density) is the number of new cases divided by the total \emph{person-time} at risk:
cases per person-year. It has units of $\text{time}^{-1}$ and can exceed one.
\item The \textbf{odds} of disease is $p/(1-p)$, where $p$ is a risk. It carries the same information as the risk but behaves better
mathematically, and it is what a case-control study can compare.
\end{enumerate}

The \textbf{prevalence} of a disease is different from all three: it is the proportion of a population that \emph{has} the disease at a
given time, counting old cases as well as new ones. Prevalence mixes how often a disease begins with how long it lasts.

\subhead{The fourfold table}

When both exposure and disease are binary, the data of almost any study can be laid out as a $2 \times 2$ table. The notation below is
used throughout the course.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|cc|c}
 & Exposed $E$ & Unexposed $\bar E$ & Total \\
\hline
Diseased $D$ & $a$ & $b$ & $m_1$ \\
Not diseased $\bar D$ & $c$ & $d$ & $m_0$ \\
\hline
Total & $n_1$ & $n_0$ & $n$ \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The columns are exposure groups and the rows are disease groups. Association is measured by:
\begin{itemize}
\item the \textbf{risk ratio} (relative risk) $\RR = \dfrac{a/n_1}{b/n_0}$;
\item the \textbf{risk difference} $\RD = \dfrac{a}{n_1} - \dfrac{b}{n_0}$;
\item the \textbf{odds ratio} $\OR = \dfrac{a/c}{b/d} = \dfrac{ad}{bc}$.
\end{itemize}
Whether these sample quantities estimate anything depends on how the table was obtained, which is the subject of the next two subsections.

\subhead{Experimental studies}

In a \textbf{randomised controlled trial} the investigator assigns the exposure (usually a treatment) by a chance mechanism and follows the
groups to compare outcomes. Randomisation makes the groups comparable, in distribution, in every characteristic measured or not, which is
why the trial is the standard against which observational designs are judged. A \textbf{field trial} randomises healthy people to a
preventive measure (a vaccine, a dietary supplement); a \textbf{community trial} randomises whole communities (fluoridation of water,
a health-education campaign). Trials are possible only for exposures that are expected to do good, or at least no harm; nobody may be
randomised to asbestos.

\subhead{Cohort studies}

A \textbf{cohort study} identifies exposed and unexposed people who are free of the disease and follows them over time to record who
develops it. It may be \textbf{prospective} (follow-up runs from now into the future) or \textbf{historical} (retrospective): exposure is
reconstructed from past records, such as employment files, and follow-up from then to now is done through death registers. In either case
the logical direction is from exposure to disease. Cohort studies measure risks and rates directly, can study many diseases after one
exposure, and establish that exposure came before disease. They are expensive and slow for rare diseases: to see $50$ cases of a disease with
annual rate $10^{-4}$ needs half a million person-years.

\subhead{Case-control studies}

A \textbf{case-control study} starts at the other end. It collects people with the disease (\textbf{cases}) and a sample of people without
it (\textbf{controls}) from the population that produced the cases, then compares their histories of exposure. It is fast and cheap for rare
diseases, which is why most of what is known about the causes of cancer first came from case-control studies. Its weaknesses are that
exposure is often recalled after diagnosis, when cases may remember differently from controls, and that choosing controls who truly
represent the source population of the cases is difficult. As Section~\ref{sec:estimable} shows, it cannot estimate risks, only odds ratios.

\subhead{Cross-sectional and ecological studies}

A \textbf{cross-sectional} study, also called a prevalence study, records exposure and disease in a sample of a population at one time. It
estimates prevalence and is the natural design for planning health services. Because it counts existing cases, it confuses the causes
of getting a disease with the causes of having it for a long time, and it often cannot tell whether exposure came first.

An \textbf{ecological} study uses groups, not individuals, as the unit of analysis: countries, districts, years. It relates the disease
rate of each group to the average exposure of the group, for instance the lung cancer rate of each country to its cigarette consumption per
head. The data are cheap, often already published, but associations between groups need not hold for the individuals in them
(Example~\ref{exa:ecological}).

\subsection{What each design can estimate}
\label{sec:estimable}

The designs differ statistically in which margins of the $2 \times 2$ table are fixed by the investigator. Let the population
probabilities be
\[
p_1 = P(D \mid E), \qquad p_0 = P(D \mid \bar E), \qquad q_1 = P(E \mid D), \qquad q_0 = P(E \mid \bar D),
\]
so that $p_1, p_0$ are the risks in the exposed and unexposed, and $q_1, q_0$ are the probabilities of exposure among the diseased and
non-diseased.

\begin{prop}[Sampling models]
\label{prop:sampling}
Under random sampling within the groups fixed by the design:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Cross-sectional}: only $n$ is fixed, and $(a, b, c, d)$ is multinomial with cell probabilities $P(D \cap E)$, \dots.
Every probability in the table can be estimated.
\item \emph{Cohort}: $n_1$ and $n_0$ are fixed and $a \sim \text{Bin}(n_1, p_1)$, $b \sim \text{Bin}(n_0, p_0)$ independently. The risks $p_1, p_0$ and every
function of them are estimable; $q_1, q_0$ are not, since they depend on the proportion exposed, which the investigator chose.
\item \emph{Case-control}: $m_1$ and $m_0$ are fixed and $a \sim \text{Bin}(m_1, q_1)$, $c \sim \text{Bin}(m_0, q_0)$ independently. The exposure
probabilities $q_1, q_0$ are estimable; $p_1, p_0$ are not, since they depend on the proportion diseased, which the investigator chose.
\end{enumerate}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
In the cohort design the $n_1$ exposed subjects are a random sample from the exposed population, so each one independently has the
disease with probability $p_1$; likewise for the unexposed. In the case-control design each sampled case is exposed with probability
$q_1$ and each control with probability $q_0$. To see that the risks cannot be estimated in a case-control study, use Bayes' theorem
with $\pi = P(D)$:
\[
p_1 = \frac{q_1 \pi}{q_1 \pi + q_0 (1 - \pi)},
\]
which takes every value in $(0,1)$ as $\pi$ ranges over $(0,1)$ with $q_1, q_0$ fixed. The distribution of the data depends only on $(q_1, q_0)$,
so two populations with the same $(q_1, q_0)$ but different $\pi$ give the same distribution of data and different $p_1$. No function of the data
can be consistent for $p_1$. The cohort argument is the same with the roles of $E$ and $D$ exchanged.
\end{proof}

So the case-control study, which samples on disease, cannot estimate the risk ratio, which is the quantity of interest. What rescues it is
the following identity, which is why the odds ratio is everywhere in epidemiology.

\begin{thm}[Invariance of the odds ratio]
\label{thm:orinv}
The \emph{exposure odds ratio} among the diseased and non-diseased equals the \emph{disease odds ratio} among the exposed and unexposed:
\[
\frac{q_1/(1 - q_1)}{q_0/(1 - q_0)} = \frac{p_1/(1 - p_1)}{p_0/(1 - p_0)} .
\]
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
Both sides equal the cross-product ratio of the joint probabilities. Write $\pi_{DE} = P(D \cap E)$ and similarly for the other three cells.
Then $q_1 = \pi_{DE}/P(D)$ and $1 - q_1 = \pi_{D\bar E}/P(D)$, so $q_1/(1 - q_1) = \pi_{DE}/\pi_{D\bar E}$; similarly $q_0/(1 - q_0) = \pi_{\bar D E}/\pi_{\bar D \bar E}$. The
left side is therefore
\[
\frac{\pi_{DE}\,\pi_{\bar D \bar E}}{\pi_{D\bar E}\,\pi_{\bar D E}} .
\]
On the right, $p_1/(1 - p_1) = \pi_{DE}/\pi_{\bar D E}$ and $p_0/(1 - p_0) = \pi_{D\bar E}/\pi_{\bar D \bar E}$, whose ratio is the same cross-product.
\end{proof}

\begin{coro}
In a case-control study the sample odds ratio $ad/(bc)$ estimates the disease odds ratio $\OR = p_1(1 - p_0)/\{p_0(1 - p_1)\}$ of the source
population, consistently as $m_1, m_0 \to \infty$.
\end{coro}

\begin{proof}
$a/m_1 \to q_1$ and $c/m_0 \to q_0$ in probability by the law of large numbers, and $b/m_1 \to 1 - q_1$, $d/m_0 \to 1 - q_0$. The sample odds ratio
$ad/(bc) = \{(a/m_1)(d/m_0)\}/\{(b/m_1)(c/m_0)\}$ is a continuous function of these four, so it converges to the exposure odds ratio, which equals the
disease odds ratio by Theorem~\ref{thm:orinv}.
\end{proof}

When the disease is rare in both exposure groups the odds ratio is close to the risk ratio, since
\[
\OR = \RR \cdot \frac{1 - p_0}{1 - p_1}
\]
and the second factor is near $1$ when $p_0$ and $p_1$ are both small. This \textbf{rare disease assumption} is what lets a case-control
study of cancer be read as estimating a relative risk. Section~\ref{sec:ccsampling} shows that with a suitable choice of controls
(\emph{density sampling}) the odds ratio estimates the \emph{rate} ratio exactly, without the assumption.

\begin{exa}[Same exposure probabilities, different risk ratios]
Suppose in two populations $q_1 = P(E \mid D) = 0.6$ and $q_0 = P(E \mid \bar D) = 0.3$, so a case-control study in either gives the same
data in distribution. The odds ratio is $(0.6/0.4)/(0.3/0.7) = 3.5$ in both. If the disease has prevalence $\pi = 0.01$ in the first
population, Bayes' theorem gives $p_1 = 0.0198$ and $p_0 = 0.0057$, and $\RR = 3.45$, close to the odds ratio. If $\pi = 0.2$ in the
second, then $p_1 = 0.333$, $p_0 = 0.125$, and $\RR = 2.67$. The case-control data cannot tell these populations apart; the odds ratio is the
part of the association they can recover.
\end{exa}

\begin{prop}[Case-control study with known sampling fractions]
\label{prop:fractions}
If cases are sampled from the source population with known probability $f_1$ and non-cases with known probability $f_0$, then the
number of exposed cases in the source population is estimated by $a/f_1$ and of exposed non-cases by $c/f_0$, and
\[
\hat p_1 = \frac{a/f_1}{a/f_1 + c/f_0}, \qquad \hat p_0 = \frac{b/f_1}{b/f_1 + d/f_0}
\]
estimate the risks. Their ratio $\hat p_1/\hat p_0$ estimates the risk ratio, and their odds ratio is $ad/(bc)$ whatever $f_1, f_0$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Each exposed case in the population is included with probability $f_1$, so $E(a) = f_1 N_{DE}$, where $N_{DE}$ is the number of exposed cases;
$a/f_1$ is unbiased for $N_{DE}$ and $c/f_0$ for $N_{\bar D E}$. The risk among the exposed is $N_{DE}/(N_{DE} + N_{\bar D E})$, estimated by substituting.
For the odds ratio, $\hat p_1/(1 - \hat p_1) = (a/f_1)/(c/f_0)$ and $\hat p_0/(1 - \hat p_0) = (b/f_1)/(d/f_0)$; the sampling fractions cancel in the ratio.
\end{proof}

\begin{exa}
A registry records all $200$ cases of a disease in a town over a year, so $f_1 = 1$. A random $1\%$ of the town's non-cases is sampled as
controls, $f_0 = 0.01$. Of the cases $120$ were exposed and $80$ not; of $300$ controls $90$ were exposed and $210$ not. The estimated numbers
of exposed and unexposed non-cases in the town are $9000$ and $21{,}000$, so
\[
\hat p_1 = \frac{120}{120 + 9000} = 0.0132, \qquad \hat p_0 = \frac{80}{80 + 21000} = 0.0038,
\]
so $\widehat{\RR} = 0.0132/0.0038 = 3.47$,
while the odds ratio is $(120 \times 210)/(80 \times 90) = 3.50$. A population-based case-control study with known sampling fractions is
a cohort study in which only a sample of the non-cases has been measured.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Association and causation}
\label{sec:causation}

An association between exposure and disease, however strong, is not by itself evidence that the exposure causes the disease. To say
precisely what causation would mean, imagine each person's outcome under each exposure.

\begin{defn}[Potential outcomes]
For each person let $Y^1$ be the disease indicator that would be observed if the person were exposed and $Y^0$ the indicator if unexposed.
The exposure has a \textbf{causal effect} on a person if $Y^1 \neq Y^0$. In a population the \textbf{causal risk ratio} and \textbf{causal risk difference} are
\[
\frac{P(Y^1 = 1)}{P(Y^0 = 1)} \qquad\text{and}\qquad P(Y^1 = 1) - P(Y^0 = 1) ,
\]
comparing the risk if everyone were exposed with the risk if no one were.
\end{defn}

Only one of $Y^1, Y^0$ is ever seen for a given person: under \textbf{consistency}, the observed outcome is $Y = Y^1$ for the exposed and $Y = Y^0$
for the unexposed. The observed (associational) risk ratio $P(Y = 1 \mid E)/P(Y = 1 \mid \bar E)$ compares different people, and equals the causal risk
ratio only under a condition on how exposure was assigned.

\begin{defn}
Exposure is \textbf{exchangeable} with respect to the outcome if $(Y^1, Y^0)$ is independent of $E$: the exposed and unexposed would have
had the same distribution of outcomes under either exposure.
\end{defn}

\begin{thm}
\label{thm:exchange}
Under consistency and exchangeability the associational risk ratio and risk difference equal the causal ones.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
By consistency $P(Y = 1 \mid E) = P(Y^1 = 1 \mid E)$, and by exchangeability $P(Y^1 = 1 \mid E) = P(Y^1 = 1)$. Likewise $P(Y = 1 \mid \bar E) = P(Y^0 = 1 \mid \bar E) = P(Y^0 = 1)$.
The associational measures are therefore built from the same two numbers as the causal ones.
\end{proof}

Randomisation makes the exposure independent of everything that exists before it, including $(Y^1, Y^0)$, so a randomised trial delivers
exchangeability by design. An observational study does not, and when exchangeability fails the associational measure is said to be
\textbf{confounded}. Confounding is treated formally in a later section. The practical response, used throughout the course, is to
find variables $C$ (age, sex, social class) such that exchangeability holds \emph{within} the strata of $C$, and to compare like with
like there.

\subhead{Judging causation from observational evidence}

Because exchangeability can never be verified from observational data, causal conclusions there rest on judgement. Hill (1965) listed
aspects of an association that make a causal reading more or less plausible: its \emph{strength} (a large risk ratio is harder to explain
away by confounding), \emph{consistency} across studies and populations, \emph{specificity}, \emph{temporality} (exposure must precede
disease, the only one that is necessary), a \emph{biological gradient} (dose--response), \emph{plausibility}, \emph{coherence} with
other knowledge, \emph{experimental} evidence and \emph{analogy}. They are not criteria to be scored. Each has exceptions, and a modern
reading treats them as questions about bias: could confounding, selection or measurement error produce an association this strong, this
consistent, with this gradient?

\begin{exa}[The ecological fallacy]
\label{exa:ecological}
Three regions have the following proportions $x$ of the population exposed and annual disease risks $r$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\hline
Region & A & B & C \\
\hline
Proportion exposed $x$ & $0.2$ & $0.5$ & $0.8$ \\
Disease risk $r$ & $0.01$ & $0.02$ & $0.03$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The least squares line through the three points is $r = 0.00333 + 0.0333\,x$. Read naively, a wholly unexposed region ($x = 0$) has risk
$0.00333$ and a wholly exposed one ($x = 1$) has risk $0.0367$, an \emph{ecological} risk ratio of $0.0367/0.00333 = 11$.

Now suppose that within each region the risk is the same in exposed and unexposed people: $0.01$ in A, $0.02$ in B, $0.03$ in C. The data
above are exactly what this produces, yet the exposure has no effect on anyone. The regional risks differ for some other reason that
happens to rise with the proportion exposed, such as the age structure of the regions. Group-level data cannot separate the two
explanations, because they do not record who among the exposed became ill.
\end{exa}

\begin{prop}
Let region $j$ have exposure proportion $x_j$ and risks $p_{1j}$, $p_{0j}$ among its exposed and unexposed. If $p_{1j} = p_1$ and $p_{0j} = p_0$ in every
region, then the regional risks lie exactly on the line $r = p_0 + (p_1 - p_0)x$, and the ecological risk ratio equals $p_1/p_0$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
The risk in region $j$ is the average of the two group risks weighted by group size, $r_j = p_{0j}(1 - x_j) + p_{1j}\,x_j$. With common $p_1, p_0$ this is
$p_0 + (p_1 - p_0)x_j$, linear in $x_j$ with intercept $p_0$ and value $p_1$ at $x = 1$.
\end{proof}

The ecological analysis is sound when the baseline risk $p_{0j}$ does not vary with the exposure proportion, and the effect does not either. Both
are assumptions about individuals that group data cannot check. In Example~\ref{exa:ecological} $p_{0j}$ rises with $x_j$, so the
condition fails.

\subsection{Problems}

\begin{problem}
\label{prob:snow}
In Example~\ref{exa:snow}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Give the risk difference in cholera deaths per $10{,}000$ houses between the two supplies, and say what it measures that the
ratio $8.4$ does not.
\item Snow's denominator is houses. State an assumption under which the ratio of deaths per house equals the ratio of deaths per
person, and give a way in which it could fail.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
(a) $315.4 - 37.5 = 277.9$ deaths per $10{,}000$ houses in seven weeks. If the Southwark and Vauxhall houses had instead received
Lambeth water, and the ratio reflects the effect of the water, about $278$ deaths per $10{,}000$ houses would have been avoided. The
difference measures the burden of the exposure on an absolute scale; the ratio measures the strength of the association, which is what
bears on causation. A ratio of $8.4$ on a tiny baseline could be of little public-health importance; here both are large.

(b) Let $h_1, h_0$ be the average number of residents per house in the two groups. Deaths per person are deaths per house divided by $h_1$ or
$h_0$, so the ratio per person is $8.4 \times h_0/h_1$. The two ratios agree when $h_1 = h_0$. They would differ if, say, the cheaper company
supplied more crowded houses. Snow's argument that the supplies were intermixed street by street makes a large difference in occupancy
unlikely.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
Classify each study and say whether it can estimate the risk of disease in the exposed.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item All $412$ children diagnosed with leukaemia in a country over five years are compared with $1200$ children drawn at random
from the birth register, on whether their mothers had a pelvic X-ray in pregnancy.
\item Workers employed at a smelter in 1950--1960 are identified from company records, and their deaths up to 2020 are found through the
national death index.
\item A household survey records current smoking and current chronic bronchitis in a random sample of adults.
\item Villages are randomly allocated to receive insecticide-treated bed nets or not, and malaria episodes in children are counted over
two years.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
(a) A case-control study (sampling on disease). Risk is not estimable from the data alone; the odds ratio is. If the number of births in
the source population is known, the controls are a known fraction of the non-cases and Proposition~\ref{prop:fractions} recovers risks.
(b) A historical cohort study: exposure (employment) is defined at the start and deaths are followed forward from records. Risks and
rates are estimable.
(c) A cross-sectional study. It estimates the prevalence of bronchitis among smokers and non-smokers, not the risk of developing it.
(d) A community (cluster-randomised) trial. Risks and rates are estimable, with the village as the unit of randomisation, which
the analysis must take into account.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
\label{prob:orrr}
Show that $\OR \geq \RR \geq 1$ when $p_1 \geq p_0$, and $\OR \leq \RR \leq 1$ when $p_1 \leq p_0$. So the odds ratio always lies further from $1$ than the
risk ratio.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$\OR = \RR \cdot (1 - p_0)/(1 - p_1)$. If $p_1 \geq p_0$ then $\RR = p_1/p_0 \geq 1$ and $1 - p_0 \geq 1 - p_1$, so the second factor is at least $1$ and
$\OR \geq \RR$. If $p_1 \leq p_0$ both inequalities reverse: $\RR \leq 1$ and the factor $(1 - p_0)/(1 - p_1) \leq 1$, so $\OR \leq \RR$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
\label{prob:po}
A population of eight people has the potential outcomes below.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccccccc}
\hline
Person & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 & 7 & 8 \\
\hline
$Y^1$ & 1 & 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
$Y^0$ & 1 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
Exposed? & yes & yes & yes & yes & no & no & no & no \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Find the causal risk ratio and the observed risk ratio.
\item Which people does the exposure affect? Explain the difference between the two ratios.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
(a) $P(Y^1 = 1) = 4/8$ and $P(Y^0 = 1) = 3/8$, so the causal risk ratio is $4/3$. Observed: the exposed (persons 1--4) show $Y^1 = 1, 1, 1, 0$, a
risk of $3/4$; the unexposed (5--8) show $Y^0 = 1, 0, 0, 0$, a risk of $1/4$. The observed risk ratio is $3$.

(b) Only person 3 has $Y^1 \neq Y^0$; the exposure causes that person's disease. Persons 1, 2 and 5 would be ill either way. Three of the four
\emph{doomed} people (1, 2, 5) are in the exposed group, so the exposed would have had risk $2/4$ even without exposure, against $1/4$ for
the unexposed. Exchangeability fails, and the observed ratio mixes the effect of the exposure with the higher baseline risk of the
exposed: the association is confounded.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
Three regions have exposure proportions $0.1$, $0.4$ and $0.7$. In each region the risk is $0.02$ in the unexposed and $0.04$ in the exposed.
Find the regional risks and the ecological risk ratio, and explain why the answer is correct here but not in Example~\ref{exa:ecological}.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$r_j = 0.02(1 - x_j) + 0.04x_j = 0.02 + 0.02x_j$, giving $0.022$, $0.028$ and $0.034$. These lie exactly on $r = 0.02 + 0.02x$, so the
ecological risk ratio is $(0.02 + 0.02)/0.02 = 2$, the individual risk ratio. The baseline risk is the same in every region and so is the effect,
which is the condition of the last proposition. In Example~\ref{exa:ecological} the baseline risk rose with the proportion exposed, and the
regression attributed that rise to the exposure.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
In a case-control study the controls are sampled from people still free of disease at the \emph{end} of the study period. Use
Theorem~\ref{thm:orinv} to say what the sample odds ratio estimates, and show that when the risks are small it approximates the risk ratio.
Find the risk ratio and the odds ratio if $p_1 = 0.02$ and $p_0 = 0.01$, and if $p_1 = 0.4$ and $p_0 = 0.2$.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
The controls are a sample of $\bar D$, the cases of $D$, so $a/m_1$ and $c/m_0$ estimate $q_1$ and $q_0$, and by Theorem~\ref{thm:orinv} the sample
odds ratio estimates the disease odds ratio $p_1(1 - p_0)/\{p_0(1 - p_1)\} = \RR\,(1 - p_0)/(1 - p_1)$, which tends to $\RR$ as $p_1, p_0 \to 0$.
With $p_1 = 0.02$, $p_0 = 0.01$: $\RR = 2$ and $\OR = 2 \times 0.99/0.98 = 2.02$. With $p_1 = 0.4$, $p_0 = 0.2$: $\RR = 2$ and $\OR = 2 \times 0.8/0.6 = 2.67$.
The approximation is good only for a rare disease.
\end{solution}

\section{The primacy of data collection}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Why data collection comes first}

Every later section of the course takes a table of counts and extracts from it a measure of frequency or association, with a confidence
interval and perhaps an adjustment for confounding. All of that machinery assumes that the counts mean what they say: that a person
recorded as a case had the disease, that a person recorded as exposed was exposed, and that the people counted are the people the study
meant to count. No analysis can repair data that fail these assumptions. A confidence interval measures the random error of a
well-collected sample; it says nothing about systematic error, and a large study with biased measurement gives a narrow interval around
the wrong value.

This is the sense in which data collection has \emph{primacy}. The decisions that most affect the validity of an epidemiological study are
made before any analysis: who is eligible, how cases are defined and found, how exposure is measured, how many people are lost or refuse.
The statistician's part in them is to say, in numbers, what each choice will cost.

\subhead{The protocol}

A study begins with a written protocol, fixed before data collection, which states:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the \textbf{question}, as a comparison of specified measures in a specified population (for instance, the rate ratio of hip fracture
for current users of a drug against non-users, among women aged 65 and over);
\item the \textbf{design} and the source population;
\item the \textbf{case definition} and how cases will be ascertained;
\item the \textbf{exposure} definition, its time window and how it will be measured;
\item the variables to be recorded as possible confounders;
\item the \textbf{sample size}, with the calculation behind it;
\item the planned analysis, including what will be done about missing data.
\end{enumerate}
Writing the analysis down in advance matters. With many possible definitions of exposure, cut-points and adjustment sets, an analysis
chosen after looking at the data can find an association that is an artefact of the choice.

\subhead{Sources of data}

Epidemiological data come either from \textbf{routine sources}, collected for other purposes (death certificates and vital registration, disease
registries such as cancer registries, notifications of infectious disease, hospital discharge records, insurance and prescription
databases, censuses) or from \textbf{data collected for the study} (interviews, self-completed questionnaires, physical examination,
laboratory measurement of biological samples). Routine data are cheap and cover whole populations, but their definitions were set by
someone else and their quality varies with place and time: a rise in the recorded rate of a disease may reflect a new diagnostic test or a
change in coding rules. Study data can be made to fit the question, at a cost that limits the number of people measured.

A \textbf{pilot study} on a small sample tests the questionnaire, the field procedures and the timing before the main study starts, and
gives the first estimates of the quantities (prevalence of exposure, response rate, variance) that the sample size calculation needs.

\subsection{Measuring disease: sensitivity and specificity}
\label{sec:sensspec}

A case definition is applied through some procedure (a questionnaire, a clinical examination, a laboratory test) that will classify some
people wrongly. Let $D$ denote true disease status and $T^+$, $T^-$ a positive or negative result of the procedure.

\begin{defn}
The \textbf{sensitivity} of a classification procedure is $\text{Se} = P(T^+ \mid D)$, the probability that a true case is classified as a case. Its
\textbf{specificity} is $\text{Sp} = P(T^- \mid \bar D)$, the probability that a non-case is classified as a non-case. The \textbf{positive predictive value}
is $\text{PPV} = P(D \mid T^+)$ and the \textbf{negative predictive value} is $\text{NPV} = P(\bar D \mid T^-)$.
\end{defn}

Sensitivity and specificity describe the procedure and, to a first approximation, do not depend on the population it is applied to. The
predictive values describe what a result means for a person, and depend heavily on how common the disease is.

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:ppv}
If the prevalence of disease is $p$, then
\[
\text{PPV} = \frac{\text{Se}\,p}{\text{Se}\,p + (1 - \text{Sp})(1 - p)}, \qquad \text{NPV} = \frac{\text{Sp}\,(1 - p)}{\text{Sp}\,(1 - p) + (1 - \text{Se})\,p} .
\]
PPV increases and NPV decreases with $p$; as $p \to 0$, $\text{PPV} \to 0$ whenever $\text{Sp} < 1$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
The formulas are Bayes' theorem. Dividing numerator and denominator of PPV by $\text{Se}\,p$ gives
$\text{PPV} = \{1 + (1 - \text{Sp})(1 - p)/(\text{Se}\,p)\}^{-1}$, and $(1 - p)/p$ decreases in $p$, so PPV increases; it tends to $0$ as $p \to 0$ because
$(1 - p)/p \to \infty$. The argument for NPV is the same with $(1 - p)$ and $p$ exchanged.
\end{proof}

\begin{exa}
A test has sensitivity $0.90$ and specificity $0.95$. Where the prevalence is $1\%$,
\[
\text{PPV} = \frac{0.90 \times 0.01}{0.90 \times 0.01 + 0.05 \times 0.99} = \frac{0.009}{0.0585} = 0.154 ,
\]
so only about one positive in six is a true case; the false positives from the $99\%$ who are healthy outnumber the true positives from the
$1\%$ who are ill. Where the prevalence is $20\%$, as in a clinic of people with symptoms, the same test has $\text{PPV} = 0.818$. The NPV
is $0.999$ and $0.974$ in the two settings.
\end{exa}

\subhead{Estimating prevalence from an imperfect test}

If a survey classifies $n$ people with a test of known sensitivity and specificity, the proportion testing positive estimates the
\textbf{apparent prevalence}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:apparent}
p^* = P(T^+) = \text{Se}\,p + (1 - \text{Sp})(1 - p) ,
\end{equation}
not the true prevalence $p$. Whenever $\text{Se} + \text{Sp} > 1$ (that is, whenever the test is better than tossing a coin) the relation can be inverted.

\begin{thm}[Rogan--Gladen estimator]
\label{thm:rogan}
Let $\hat p^*$ be the proportion testing positive in a random sample of size $n$, and $J = \text{Se} + \text{Sp} - 1 > 0$. Then
\[
\hat p = \frac{\hat p^* + \text{Sp} - 1}{J}
\]
is unbiased for $p$, with variance $p^*(1 - p^*)/(nJ^2)$.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
From \eqref{eq:apparent}, $p^* = (1 - \text{Sp}) + Jp$, so $p = (p^* + \text{Sp} - 1)/J$. The number testing positive is $\text{Bin}(n, p^*)$, so
$E(\hat p^*) = p^*$ and $\Var(\hat p^*) = p^*(1 - p^*)/n$. The estimator is a linear function of $\hat p^*$ with slope $1/J$, which gives both results.
\end{proof}

The quantity $J$ is \textbf{Youden's index}. The variance is inflated by $1/J^2$ compared with a perfect test, the price of the
misclassification. The estimate can fall outside $[0, 1]$ when $\hat p^*$ is below $1 - \text{Sp}$ or above Se; it is then truncated to the nearer
end.

\begin{exa}
In a survey of $1000$ people, $130$ test positive on the test of the last example. The apparent prevalence is $0.130$ and
\begin{gather*}
\hat p = \frac{0.130 + 0.95 - 1}{0.85} = 0.094, \\
\text{SE}(\hat p) = \frac{\sqrt{0.130 \times 0.870/1000}}{0.85} = 0.0125 ,
\end{gather*}
with approximate $95\%$ interval $0.094 \pm 1.96 \times 0.0125 = (0.070, 0.119)$. Taking $13\%$ at face value would overstate the
prevalence by a third.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Measuring exposure: validity and reliability}

A measurement is \textbf{valid} if it measures what it is meant to, on average, and \textbf{reliable} (repeatable) if it gives the same answer
when repeated. Validity is judged against a better reference (a \emph{gold standard}): self-reported smoking against cotinine in saliva,
recalled diet against weighed food records. Reliability can be judged without one, by repeating the measurement or having two observers
make it independently. A measurement can be reliable and invalid (a scale that always reads two kilograms light); it cannot be valid
for individuals unless it is reasonably reliable.

\subhead{Agreement between two classifications: kappa}

When two observers classify the same $n$ subjects into two categories, the proportion of subjects on whom they agree, $p_o$, is a poor
measure of reliability by itself, because two observers who classify at random agree on many subjects by chance. If the observers'
marginal proportions of ``positive'' are $r_1$ and $c_1$, independent random classification would agree with probability
$p_e = r_1 c_1 + (1 - r_1)(1 - c_1)$.

\begin{defn}
\textbf{Cohen's kappa} is
\[
\kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e} ,
\]
the agreement beyond chance as a fraction of the largest possible agreement beyond chance.
\end{defn}

So $\kappa = 1$ for perfect agreement, $\kappa = 0$ for agreement no better than chance, and $\kappa < 0$ for less. Values above about $0.8$ are
usually read as very good and below $0.4$ as poor, although the scale is a convention.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:kappa}
Two radiologists read the same $200$ chest X-rays for a lesion.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|cc|c}
 & B positive & B negative & Total \\
\hline
A positive & $46$ & $10$ & $56$ \\
A negative & $14$ & $130$ & $144$ \\
\hline
Total & $60$ & $140$ & $200$ \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
They agree on $p_o = (46 + 130)/200 = 0.880$ of the films. Chance agreement is
\[
p_e = \frac{56}{200} \cdot \frac{60}{200} + \frac{144}{200} \cdot \frac{140}{200} = 0.084 + 0.504 = 0.588 ,
\]
so $\kappa = (0.880 - 0.588)/(1 - 0.588) = 0.709$. Most of the $88\%$ raw agreement comes from the many films both read as clear.
\end{exa}

\subhead{Continuous exposures measured with error}

If a continuous exposure $X$ is measured as $W = X + U$, with $U$ independent of $X$, of mean zero and variance $\sigma^2_U$, then a regression of
an outcome on $W$ instead of $X$ estimates the true slope multiplied by the \textbf{reliability ratio}
\[
\lambda = \frac{\sigma^2_X}{\sigma^2_X + \sigma^2_U} ,
\]
the proportion of the variance of $W$ that is true variation between people. For if $Y = \alpha + \beta X + \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon$ independent of
$X$ and $U$, the slope of $Y$ on $W$ is $\Cov(Y, W)/\Var(W) = \beta\sigma^2_X/(\sigma^2_X + \sigma^2_U) = \lambda\beta$. This attenuation is called
\textbf{regression dilution}. Its epidemiological reading is that an exposure measured on a single occasion, such as one
reading of blood pressure, understates the strength of its relation with disease. If repeated measurements on a sample of subjects give
$\lambda = 0.8$, the observed slope is $80\%$ of the true one, and dividing by $\lambda$ corrects it.

\subsection{Misclassification and its effects}
\label{sec:misclass}

When a binary exposure or disease is measured with error, the $2 \times 2$ table is \textbf{misclassified}, and the measures of association
computed from it are biased. Whether the error depends on the other variable is the key distinction.

\begin{defn}
Misclassification of exposure is \textbf{nondifferential} if its sensitivity and its specificity are the same for cases and
non-cases;
misclassification of disease is nondifferential if they are the same for exposed and unexposed. Otherwise it is \textbf{differential}.
\end{defn}

Exposure taken from records made before the disease appeared, such as employment files or prescriptions, is usually
nondifferentially misclassified. Exposure recalled at interview after diagnosis may not be: a mother whose child has a birth defect searches her memory of
the pregnancy more thoroughly than the mother of a healthy child. This is \textbf{recall bias}, the commonest form of differential
misclassification.

\begin{thm}[Nondifferential misclassification of disease]
\label{thm:ndd}
In a cohort study, let disease be classified with sensitivity Se and specificity Sp in both exposure groups, with $J = \text{Se} + \text{Sp} - 1 > 0$. The
observed risks are $p_i^* = (1 - \text{Sp}) + J p_i$ for $i = 0, 1$, and
\begin{enumerate}
\item the observed risk difference is $J$ times the true one;
\item the observed risk ratio lies between $1$ and the true risk ratio, and equals it if $\text{Sp} = 1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
The expression for $p_i^*$ is \eqref{eq:apparent} applied within exposure group $i$. Then $p_1^* - p_0^* = J(p_1 - p_0)$, which is (1). For (2), write
$s = 1 - \text{Sp} \geq 0$ and suppose $p_1 > p_0$. Then $p_1^*/p_0^* = (s + Jp_1)/(s + Jp_0) > 1$, and
\[
\frac{s + Jp_1}{s + Jp_0} \leq \frac{p_1}{p_0} \iff p_0 (s + Jp_1) \leq p_1 (s + Jp_0) \iff s\,p_0 \leq s\,p_1 ,
\]
which holds, with equality if and only if $s = 0$. The case $p_1 < p_0$ is the same with the inequalities reversed.
\end{proof}

The second part has a practical consequence. When the effect is measured as a ratio, \emph{specificity} matters far more than sensitivity:
a case definition that misses some cases but admits no false ones leaves the risk ratio unbiased. Studies therefore often use strict case
definitions, accepting that some true cases are lost.

\begin{thm}[Nondifferential misclassification of exposure]
\label{thm:nde}
In a case-control study, let exposure be classified with sensitivity Se and specificity Sp among both cases and controls, with
$J = \text{Se} + \text{Sp} - 1 > 0$, and let $q_1, q_0 \in (0, 1)$ be the true probabilities of exposure among cases and controls. Then the observed odds ratio lies
between $1$ and the true odds ratio.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
The observed exposure probabilities are $q_i^* = f(q_i)$, where $f(q) = a + Jq$ with $a = 1 - \text{Sp}$; note $1 - f(q) = b + J(1 - q)$ with $b = 1 - \text{Se}$, and
$a + b = 1 - J$. Let $g(\theta) = \operatorname{logit} f(q)$ where $\theta = \operatorname{logit} q$. The log odds ratios are $\theta_1 - \theta_0$ (true) and $g(\theta_1) - g(\theta_0)$ (observed), so it is
enough to show $0 < g'(\theta) \leq 1$; the mean value theorem then gives $g(\theta_1) - g(\theta_0) = g'(\xi)(\theta_1 - \theta_0)$, of the same sign and no larger in size.

Since $d\theta/dq = 1/\{q(1 - q)\}$ and $d \operatorname{logit} f/dq = J/\{f(1 - f)\}$,
\[
g'(\theta) = \frac{J\,q(1 - q)}{f(q)\{1 - f(q)\}} .
\]
This is positive. Expanding the denominator,
\[
f(1 - f) = (a + Jq)\{b + J(1 - q)\} = ab + aJ(1 - q) + bJq + J^2 q(1 - q) .
\]
Now $aJ(1 - q) \geq aJ\,q(1 - q)$ and $bJq \geq bJ\,q(1 - q)$, so
\begin{align*}
f(1 - f) &\geq (a + b)J\,q(1 - q) + J^2 q(1 - q) \\
&= (1 - J + J)\,J\,q(1 - q) = J\,q(1 - q) ,
\end{align*}
and $g'(\theta) \leq 1$.
\end{proof}

So nondifferential misclassification of a binary exposure biases the odds ratio towards the null, and a study that finds an association
in spite of it has, if anything, understated it. Three cautions keep this result in its place. It is a statement about the expected
value; in a particular small study random error can carry the estimate past the true value. It concerns a binary exposure; with three or
more categories misclassification between adjacent levels can bias the middle category away from the null. And it assumes the errors in
exposure are independent of any errors in disease.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:misclass}
In a cohort study the true risks are $p_1 = 0.10$ and $p_0 = 0.05$ ($\RR = 2$, $\RD = 0.05$). Disease is ascertained with $\text{Se} = 0.80$ and
$\text{Sp} = 0.95$, so $J = 0.75$. The observed risks are
\begin{gather*}
p_1^* = 0.05 + 0.75 \times 0.10 = 0.125, \\
p_0^* = 0.05 + 0.75 \times 0.05 = 0.0875 ,
\end{gather*}
giving an observed risk ratio of $1.43$ and a risk difference of $0.0375 = 0.75 \times 0.05$.

In a case-control study with true $q_1 = 0.4$ and $q_0 = 0.2$ ($\OR = 2.67$), exposure is classified with $\text{Se} = 0.8$ and $\text{Sp} = 0.9$ in both
groups. Then $q_1^* = 0.1 + 0.7 \times 0.4 = 0.38$, $q_0^* = 0.1 + 0.7 \times 0.2 = 0.24$, and the observed odds ratio is
$(0.38/0.62)/(0.24/0.76) = 1.94$.

If instead the misclassification is differential (cases recall exposure with sensitivity $0.95$, controls with $0.75$, specificity $0.9$ in both)
and the exposure has no effect, $q_1 = q_0 = 0.3$, then $q_1^* = 0.355$ and $q_0^* = 0.295$, and the observed odds ratio is $1.32$: an association
created entirely by the way exposure was measured.
\end{exa}

\subhead{Correcting for misclassification}

When Se and Sp are known, from a validation study in which a subsample is measured both ways, the expected observed counts can be
inverted. Among $m$ subjects of whom $x^*$ are classified exposed, the expected number truly exposed is
\[
x = \frac{x^* - (1 - \text{Sp})\,m}{J} ,
\]
which is Theorem~\ref{thm:rogan} written in counts. Applying it separately to cases and controls (with their own Se and Sp if the
misclassification is differential) gives a corrected table.

\begin{exa}
A case-control study with $200$ cases and $200$ controls observes $76$ exposed cases and $48$ exposed controls, an odds ratio of
$(76 \times 152)/(124 \times 48) = 1.94$. If exposure is known to be classified with $\text{Se} = 0.8$, $\text{Sp} = 0.9$, the corrected numbers exposed are
\[
\frac{76 - 0.1 \times 200}{0.7} = 80 \quad\text{(cases)}, \qquad \frac{48 - 0.1 \times 200}{0.7} = 40 \quad\text{(controls)},
\]
and the corrected odds ratio is $(80 \times 160)/(120 \times 40) = 2.67$, recovering the second part of Example~\ref{exa:misclass}. The correction
is only as good as the values of Se and Sp; small errors in them, especially in Sp when exposure is rare, can produce impossible
(negative) corrected counts.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Completeness of the data}

Measurement error concerns the people who are measured; the other threat at the collection stage is the people who are not.
\textbf{Non-response} in a survey, \textbf{refusal} in a case-control study and \textbf{loss to follow-up} in a cohort remove people from the
data, and if the reasons are related to exposure or disease, the people left are no longer a random sample.

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:nonresp}
In a population in which a fraction $r$ responds, let $\mu_R$ and $\mu_N$ be the means (or prevalences) of a variable among responders and
non-responders. The population mean is $\mu = r\mu_R + (1 - r)\mu_N$, and the responders' mean has bias
\[
\mu_R - \mu = (1 - r)(\mu_R - \mu_N) .
\]
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
$\mu_R - \mu = \mu_R - r\mu_R - (1 - r)\mu_N = (1 - r)(\mu_R - \mu_N)$.
\end{proof}

The bias is the product of the non-response rate and the difference between responders and non-responders. A high response rate limits
it, but cannot remove it if the two groups are very different, and the second factor cannot be estimated from the responders alone.

\begin{exa}
In a survey with $70\%$ response, the prevalence of a condition among responders is $0.12$. If a follow-up of a sample of
non-responders finds a prevalence of $0.25$ among them, the population prevalence is $0.7 \times 0.12 + 0.3 \times 0.25 = 0.159$, and the
responders understated it by $0.3 \times (0.12 - 0.25) = -0.039$, about a quarter of the true value. People who are ill are often less likely
to take part in a health survey, which makes this direction of bias common.
\end{exa}

Missing values within the records of those who do take part raise the same issue. Data are \textbf{missing completely at random} (MCAR)
if whether a value is missing is unrelated to any variable, observed or not; \textbf{missing at random} (MAR) if it depends only on observed
variables; and \textbf{missing not at random} otherwise. An analysis of complete records only is unbiased under MCAR. Under MAR the
missingness can be corrected for by using the observed variables it depends on (weighting, or multiple imputation); when values are
missing not at random no analysis of the data alone can correct for it, which is why the protocol should aim to prevent missing data
rather than plan to analyse it.

\subsection{Problems}

\begin{problem}
A screening test for a disease has sensitivity $0.95$ and specificity $0.98$. Find the positive predictive value when the prevalence
is $0.001$, $0.01$ and $0.1$. Explain why mass screening of a general population for a rare disease generates mostly false
positives, and what can be done about it.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$\text{PPV} = 0.95p/\{0.95p + 0.02(1 - p)\}$, which is $0.045$ at $p = 0.001$, $0.324$ at $p = 0.01$ and $0.841$ at $p = 0.1$. At low prevalence the $2\%$
false positive rate applies to almost the whole population, while the $95\%$ true positive rate applies to very few, so false positives
dominate (Proposition~\ref{prop:ppv}). Remedies are to screen only a higher-prevalence group (by age or risk factors), or to follow a positive
result with a second, more specific test; testing in series raises the effective specificity to about $1 - (1 - \text{Sp}_1)(1 - \text{Sp}_2)$ if
the two tests err independently.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
In a serological survey $84$ of $600$ people test positive on an assay with sensitivity $0.92$ and specificity $0.97$. Estimate the true
prevalence with a $95\%$ confidence interval. What would the estimate be if only $15$ had tested positive?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$\hat p^* = 84/600 = 0.140$ and $J = 0.89$, so $\hat p = (0.140 - 0.03)/0.89 = 0.124$ and $\text{SE} = \sqrt{0.14 \times 0.86/600}/0.89 = 0.0159$. The interval is
$0.124 \pm 0.031 = (0.093, 0.155)$. With $15$ positives, $\hat p^* = 0.025 < 1 - \text{Sp} = 0.03$, so $\hat p = (0.025 - 0.03)/0.89 < 0$: fewer people tested positive than
the false positive rate alone would produce. The estimate is truncated to $0$; the data are consistent with no infection at all.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
Show that $\kappa$ can be low even when the observed agreement is high. Two observers classify $100$ subjects; they agree that $90$ are
negative, both call $2$ positive, and they disagree on the remaining $8$, split equally. Compute $p_o$ and $\kappa$, and explain.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Each observer calls $2 + 4 = 6$ positive, so $p_e = 0.06^2 + 0.94^2 = 0.8872$ and $p_o = 0.92$. Then $\kappa = (0.92 - 0.8872)/(1 - 0.8872) = 0.29$. When one category
is rare, two observers agree on most subjects by chance, so $p_e$ is close to $1$ and the agreement beyond chance is small: the observers
agree on only $2$ of the $10$ subjects that either of them called positive. Kappa depends on the prevalence of the trait, and should be
reported with the marginal proportions.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
In a cohort study the true risks are $p_1 = 0.02$ and $p_0 = 0.01$. Find the observed risk ratio if disease is ascertained (a) with
sensitivity $0.7$ and specificity $1$, (b) with sensitivity $1$ and specificity $0.99$, in both groups. Comment.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
(a) $p_i^* = 0.7p_i$, so $\RR^* = 0.014/0.007 = 2$, unbiased (Theorem~\ref{thm:ndd}, $\text{Sp} = 1$). (b) $p_i^* = 0.01 + 0.99p_i$, so
$\RR^* = (0.01 + 0.0198)/(0.01 + 0.0099) = 0.0298/0.0199 = 1.50$. Losing $30\%$ of the cases costs only precision; a $1\%$ false positive rate, applied to a
population in which the disease itself has a risk of $1$--$2\%$, adds as many false cases as there are true ones in the unexposed and
pulls the ratio a long way towards $1$. For a rare disease, specificity is what matters.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
Give an example, with numbers, in which differential misclassification of exposure biases the odds ratio \emph{away} from the null
when there is a true association, and one in which it reverses the direction of the association.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Take $q_1 = 0.4$, $q_0 = 0.2$ (true $\OR = 2.67$) and $\text{Sp} = 1$ in both groups. If cases report exposure with $\text{Se} = 1$ and controls with
$\text{Se} = 0.5$, then $q_1^* = 0.4$, $q_0^* = 0.1$ and $\OR^* = (0.4/0.6)/(0.1/0.9) = 6.0$, biased away from the null. If instead cases under-report with
$\text{Se} = 0.4$ and controls report fully, $q_1^* = 0.16$, $q_0^* = 0.2$ and $\OR^* = (0.16/0.84)/(0.2/0.8) = 0.76$: a harmful exposure appears protective. Differential
error has no predictable direction, which is why it is worse than nondifferential error of the same size.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
In a cohort of $5000$ people, $20\%$ are lost to follow-up. Among those followed, the five-year risk is $0.08$. Using
Proposition~\ref{prop:nonresp}, find the range of the true five-year risk if the risk among those lost could be anything from $0$ to $0.30$.
What assumption makes the complete-case risk unbiased?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
The true risk is $0.8 \times 0.08 + 0.2\,\mu_N = 0.064 + 0.2\,\mu_N$, which ranges from $0.064$ (if no one lost became ill) to $0.124$ (if $30\%$ did). The
complete-case estimate $0.08$ is unbiased only if the risk among those lost equals the risk among those followed: loss to follow-up
unrelated to outcome. A range like this, computed from extreme but plausible assumptions about the missing, is a simple and honest
\emph{sensitivity analysis}.
\end{solution}

\section{Measures of disease frequency}
\label{sec:frequency}

Section~\ref{sec:occurrence} introduced prevalence, risk and rate informally. This section defines them precisely, gives the
probability models under which they are estimated, derives confidence intervals, and proves the relations between them. The measures
of association in the next sections are ratios and differences of these quantities, so everything there rests on what is done here.

\subsection{Prevalence}
\label{sec:prevalence}

\begin{defn}
The \textbf{point prevalence} of a disease at time $t$ is the proportion of a population that has the disease at $t$. The \textbf{period
prevalence} over an interval is the proportion that has the disease at any time during the interval.
\end{defn}

Prevalence is a proportion, without units. It counts every existing case, however long ago it began, so it measures the burden of a
disease on a population, the quantity needed for planning services, rather than how often the disease begins.

In a cross-sectional survey of $n$ people drawn at random, of whom $X$ have the disease, $X \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)$, and the prevalence $p$ is
estimated by $\hat p = X/n$, unbiased with variance $p(1 - p)/n$. Three confidence intervals are in use.

\subhead{The Wald interval}

The normal approximation $\hat p \approx N(p, p(1 - p)/n)$ with the variance estimated by $\hat p(1 - \hat p)/n$ gives
\[
\hat p \pm z \sqrt{\hat p(1 - \hat p)/n} ,
\]
where $z = 1.96$ for $95\%$. It is simple, but its coverage is poor when $np$ is small: it can include negative values, and when $X = 0$ it
collapses to the single point $0$.

\subhead{The Wilson (score) interval}

Instead of estimating the variance, keep $p$ in it and find all $p$ for which $|\hat p - p| \leq z\sqrt{p(1 - p)/n}$. Squaring gives a quadratic
inequality in $p$,
\[
(1 + z^2/n)\,p^2 - (2\hat p + z^2/n)\,p + \hat p^2 \leq 0 ,
\]
whose roots are the limits
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:wilson}
\frac{\hat p + \dfrac{z^2}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\dfrac{\hat p(1 - \hat p)}{n} + \dfrac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + z^2/n} .
\end{equation}
The interval always lies inside $[0, 1]$ and is centred not at $\hat p$ but at a point pulled towards $\tfrac12$. Its coverage is close to
the nominal level even for small $n$, and it is the interval to use by default.

\subhead{The exact (Clopper--Pearson) interval}

The exact interval inverts two one-sided binomial tests: its lower limit $p_L$ is the value of $p$ for which $P(X \geq x) = 0.025$, and its
upper limit $p_U$ the value for which $P(X \leq x) = 0.025$, where $x$ is the observed count. These are found from the beta distribution.

\begin{lem}
\label{lem:binbeta}
If $X \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)$ and $1 \leq x \leq n$, then $P(X \geq x) = P(B \leq p)$ where $B \sim \text{Beta}(x, n - x + 1)$.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
Let $U_1, \dots, U_n$ be independent uniform on $(0, 1)$ and $X$ the number of them that are at most $p$; then $X \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)$. The event $X \geq x$
is the event that the $x$-th smallest of the $U_i$, $U_{(x)}$, is at most $p$. The $x$-th order statistic of $n$ uniforms has the
$\text{Beta}(x, n - x + 1)$ distribution.
\end{proof}

Hence $p_L$ is the $0.025$ quantile of $\text{Beta}(x, n - x + 1)$ and, applying the lemma to $n - X$, $p_U$ is the $0.975$ quantile of
$\text{Beta}(x + 1, n - x)$; by convention $p_L = 0$ when $x = 0$ and $p_U = 1$ when $x = n$. The coverage of this interval is never below $95\%$,
so it is conservative: usually somewhat wider than it need be.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:prevci}
In a survey $46$ of $400$ adults have hypertension, so $\hat p = 0.115$. The $95\%$ intervals are:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\hline
Method & Interval \\
\hline
Wald & $(0.084,\ 0.146)$ \\
Wilson & $(0.087,\ 0.150)$ \\
Clopper--Pearson & $(0.085,\ 0.150)$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
With this many cases the three agree closely. With $2$ cases in $40$ ($\hat p = 0.05$) they do not: Wald gives $(-0.018,\ 0.118)$, an
impossible lower limit, while Wilson gives $(0.014,\ 0.165)$ and Clopper--Pearson $(0.006,\ 0.169)$.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Risk}
\label{sec:risk}

\begin{defn}
The \textbf{risk} or \textbf{cumulative incidence} of a disease over an interval $(0, t]$ is the probability that a person free of the disease
at time $0$ develops it during the interval.
\end{defn}

In a closed cohort of $N$ people at risk at time $0$, followed completely for the whole period, the number $X$ who develop the disease is
$\text{Bin}(N, R)$, where $R$ is the risk. The estimate $X/N$ and the intervals of the last subsection apply unchanged. An \textbf{attack rate} is
the same quantity for an outbreak, where the period is the short duration of the outbreak (the proportion of those who ate at a
wedding who became ill), and the \textbf{case-fatality} is the risk of death over a stated period among those with the disease.

Despite the word ``rate'' in these names, all are proportions. Two complications prevent them from being as simple in practice as in
the definition:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Losses.} People leave the study before the end of the period, by migration or refusal, without having had the event. Their
outcome over the full period is unknown, so neither the numerator nor the denominator is known exactly. Section~\ref{sec:followup}
handles this with life tables; meanwhile, the rate defined next uses the time each person was actually observed.
\item \textbf{Competing risks.} People die of other causes before they could develop the disease. The risk of the disease then depends on
the death rate from other causes as well as on the disease itself (Problem~\ref{prob:compete}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Incidence rate and person-time}
\label{sec:rate}

\begin{defn}
The \textbf{incidence rate} of a disease in a population over a period is
\[
I = \frac{\text{number of new cases in the period}}{\text{total person-time at risk in the period}} .
\]
\end{defn}

Each person contributes the time from entering observation (or the start of the period) until the earliest of: developing the disease,
leaving observation, or the end of the period. The units are $\text{time}^{-1}$, conventionally written as cases per $1000$ person-years.
A rate of $5$ per $1000$ person-years is the same whether it comes from $1000$ people followed for one year or $100$ people for ten.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:persontime}
Eight workers are followed during a five-year study. Times are years from the start of the study.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cccl}
\hline
Worker & Enters & Leaves & Reason for leaving \\
\hline
1 & 0 & 5 & End of study \\
2 & 0 & 2.5 & Disease \\
3 & 1 & 5 & End of study \\
4 & 0 & 3 & Moved away \\
5 & 2 & 4.5 & Disease \\
6 & 0 & 5 & End of study \\
7 & 0.5 & 1.5 & Died of another cause \\
8 & 3 & 5 & End of study \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The person-years at risk are $5 + 2.5 + 4 + 3 + 2.5 + 5 + 1 + 2 = 25$ and there are $2$ cases, so $I = 2/25 = 0.08$ per person-year, or $80$ per
$1000$ person-years. No risk can be computed from these data without further assumptions: only $4$ workers were observed for all five
years, and the others were not observed long enough to say whether they would have become ill.
\end{exa}

In an open population whose size $N(t)$ changes slowly, the person-time over a period of length $\Delta$ is approximately $N_{\text{mid}}\Delta$,
where $N_{\text{mid}}$ is the population at the middle of the period. National annual death rates are computed in this way, as the deaths in the year divided by the mid-year population.

\subhead{The rate as a hazard}

The incidence rate estimates a population parameter: the \textbf{hazard} $\lambda(t)$, the instantaneous rate at which disease occurs among
those still at risk,
\[
\lambda(t) = \lim_{h \downarrow 0} \frac{P(t < T \leq t + h \mid T > t)}{h} ,
\]
where $T$ is the time to disease. The hazard determines the risk.

\begin{thm}[Risk and rate]
\label{thm:riskrate}
In the absence of competing risks, the risk over $(0, t]$ is
\[
R(t) = 1 - \exp\Bigl(-\int_0^t \lambda(u)\,du\Bigr) .
\]
If the hazard is constant, $R(t) = 1 - e^{-\lambda t}$, and $R(t) \approx \lambda t$ when $\lambda t$ is small.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
Let $S(t) = P(T > t) = 1 - R(t)$. Then $P(t < T \leq t + h \mid T > t) = \{S(t) - S(t + h)\}/S(t)$, so dividing by $h$ and letting $h \to 0$ gives
$\lambda(t) = -S'(t)/S(t) = -\frac{d}{dt}\log S(t)$. Integrating from $0$ with $S(0) = 1$, $\log S(t) = -\int_0^t \lambda(u)\,du$. For constant $\lambda$ the
integral is $\lambda t$, and $1 - e^{-x} = x - x^2/2 + \cdots \approx x$ for small $x$.
\end{proof}

\begin{exa}
A rate of $6.23$ per $1000$ person-years, if constant, gives a ten-year risk of $1 - e^{-0.0623} = 0.0604$; the approximation
$\lambda t = 0.0623$ is $3\%$ too high. For a rate of $50$ per $1000$ the ten-year risk is $1 - e^{-0.5} = 0.39$, against the approximation $0.5$,
which is useless.
\end{exa}

\subhead{The Poisson likelihood}

The statistical model for a rate is justified by the following result, which is why counts of cases over person-time are analysed as
Poisson variables even though nobody's follow-up was fixed in advance.

\begin{thm}
\label{thm:poissonlik}
Suppose $n$ people have a constant hazard $\lambda$, and person $i$ is observed for time $t_i$ with event indicator $\delta_i$ ($1$ if the disease
occurred at $t_i$, $0$ if observation ended without it), where the end of observation without disease is independent of the time to
disease. Let $D = \sum \delta_i$ and $T = \sum t_i$. Then the likelihood is
\[
L(\lambda) = \lambda^D e^{-\lambda T} ,
\]
which is proportional to the likelihood of a Poisson observation $D$ with mean $\lambda T$. The maximum likelihood estimate is $\hat\lambda = D/T$,
and the observed information gives $\text{SE}(\log \hat\lambda) = 1/\sqrt{D}$.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
With constant hazard, $T_i$ is exponential with density $\lambda e^{-\lambda t}$ and survivor function $e^{-\lambda t}$. A person whose disease is observed at $t_i$
contributes the density $\lambda e^{-\lambda t_i}$; a person observed disease-free until $t_i$ contributes $P(T_i > t_i) = e^{-\lambda t_i}$. (Independence of the
end of observation is what makes these the right contributions.) Both are $\lambda^{\delta_i} e^{-\lambda t_i}$, and the product over people is $\lambda^D e^{-\lambda T}$.
A Poisson variable with mean $\lambda T$ has probability $(\lambda T)^D e^{-\lambda T}/D!$, which as a function of $\lambda$ is a constant times the same expression.

The log-likelihood $D\log\lambda - \lambda T$ has derivative $D/\lambda - T$, zero at $\hat\lambda = D/T$, and second derivative $-D/\lambda^2$. In terms of
$\theta = \log\lambda$ the log-likelihood is $D\theta - Te^\theta$, with second derivative $-Te^\theta = -D$ at the maximum, so the observed information
for $\theta$ is $D$ and $\text{SE}(\hat\theta) = 1/\sqrt D$.
\end{proof}

The approximate interval for the rate is therefore $\hat\lambda\, e^{\pm z/\sqrt D}$. Its precision depends on the number of \emph{cases}, not on the
number of people or the amount of person-time: a study with $25$ cases estimates a rate to within a factor of about $e^{1.96/5} = 1.48$,
however large the cohort.

\subhead{The exact interval for a rate}

Treating $D$ as Poisson with mean $\mu = \lambda T$, exact limits for $\mu$ come from the gamma distribution, as the binomial ones came from the beta.

\begin{lem}
\label{lem:poisgamma}
If $X \sim \text{Poisson}(\mu)$ and $d \geq 1$, then $P(X \geq d) = P(G \leq \mu)$ where $G \sim \text{Gamma}(d, 1)$; equivalently $2G \sim \chi^2_{2d}$.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
Let events occur in a Poisson process of unit rate on $(0, \infty)$. The number in $(0, \mu]$ is $\text{Poisson}(\mu)$, and at least $d$ events occur in
$(0, \mu]$ exactly when the $d$-th event time is at most $\mu$. The $d$-th event time is the sum of $d$ independent unit exponentials, which is
$\text{Gamma}(d, 1)$, and twice a $\text{Gamma}(d, 1)$ variable is $\chi^2_{2d}$.
\end{proof}

Solving $P(X \geq d) = 0.025$ and $P(X \leq d) = 0.025$ for $\mu$ gives the exact $95\%$ limits for the rate,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:exactrate}
\lambda_L = \frac{\chi^2_{2D,\,0.025}}{2T}, \qquad \lambda_U = \frac{\chi^2_{2D + 2,\,0.975}}{2T} ,
\end{equation}
where $\chi^2_{k,\alpha}$ is the $\alpha$ quantile of $\chi^2_k$ and $\lambda_L = 0$ when $D = 0$.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:rateci}
A cohort accumulates $3850$ person-years and $24$ cases. The rate is $24/3850 = 6.23$ per $1000$ person-years. The log-scale interval is
\[
6.23\, e^{\pm 1.96/\sqrt{24}} = (4.18,\ 9.30) \text{ per } 1000 ,
\]
and the exact interval, from $\chi^2_{48,\,0.025} = 30.75$ and $\chi^2_{50,\,0.975} = 71.42$, is $(3.99,\ 9.28)$ per $1000$.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Prevalence, incidence and duration}
\label{sec:prevdur}

Prevalence depends on how often a disease begins and how long it lasts. The relation is exact in a population in a steady state.

\begin{thm}
\label{thm:steady}
In a population of constant size $N$ in which the incidence rate $I$, the prevalence $P$ and the mean duration $\bar D$ of the disease do not
change over time,
\[
\frac{P}{1 - P} = I\,\bar D .
\]
When the prevalence is small, $P \approx I\bar D$.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
At any time there are $NP$ cases and $N(1 - P)$ people at risk. New cases arise at rate $I\,N(1 - P)$ per unit time. Cases leave the prevalent
pool, by recovery or death, at rate $NP/\bar D$ per unit time: in a steady state the pool of $NP$ cases turns over once in the mean duration
$\bar D$. A steady state requires inflow to equal outflow, $I\,N(1 - P) = NP/\bar D$, which rearranges to the result. When $P$ is small, $1 - P \approx 1$.
\end{proof}

Two consequences matter for design. A treatment that keeps patients alive without curing them \emph{raises} prevalence, by increasing
$\bar D$. And any factor associated with longer survival with a disease is over-represented among prevalent cases, so a cross-sectional
comparison of cases with non-cases mixes the causes of the disease with the determinants of its duration (Problem~\ref{prob:prevbias}).

\begin{exa}
A chronic disease has incidence $4$ per $1000$ person-years and lasts on average $5$ years. In a steady state the prevalence odds are
$0.004 \times 5 = 0.02$, so $P = 0.02/1.02 = 0.0196$, about $2\%$. If a new treatment doubles survival with the disease, the prevalence rises to
$0.04/1.04 = 3.8\%$ with no change at all in how often the disease begins.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Specific and crude rates}
\label{sec:crude}

Rates are usually computed separately within categories of age and sex, because these affect almost every disease. A rate for a whole
population, ignoring such categories, is a \textbf{crude} rate; a rate within a category is a \textbf{specific} rate (age-specific,
sex-specific).

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:crude}
If a population is divided into strata $j = 1, \dots, J$ with person-time $T_j$ and specific rates $I_j$, its crude rate is
\[
I_{\text{crude}} = \sum_j w_j I_j, \qquad w_j = \frac{T_j}{\sum_k T_k} ,
\]
a weighted average of the specific rates with weights equal to the population's own distribution of person-time across the strata.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
The crude rate is $\sum_j D_j/\sum_k T_k$, where $D_j = I_j T_j$ is the number of cases in stratum $j$. Substituting gives $\sum_j I_j T_j/\sum_k T_k$.
\end{proof}

So two populations with identical specific rates can have very different crude rates if their age structures differ, and comparing
crude rates compares age structures as much as risk.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:towns}
Two towns have the same death rates: $2$ per $1000$ per year under age $50$ and $20$ per $1000$ at $50$ and over. Town A has $80{,}000$ people under
$50$ and $20{,}000$ over; town B, a retirement town, has $40{,}000$ under and $60{,}000$ over. The deaths in a year are
\[
\text{A}: 160 + 400 = 560, \qquad \text{B}: 80 + 1200 = 1280 ,
\]
crude rates of $5.6$ and $12.8$ per $1000$. Town B's crude rate is more than twice town A's although no person in it is at greater risk than
the same person in A. Removing the effect of age structure from such comparisons is the subject of Section~\ref{sec:standard}.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Problems}

\begin{problem}
Of $250$ children examined in a school, $3$ have a skin infection. Compute the Wald, Wilson and exact $95\%$ intervals for the
prevalence and comment. What is the exact upper limit if no child had been infected?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$\hat p = 0.012$. The Wald interval is
\[
0.012 \pm 1.96\sqrt{0.012 \times 0.988/250} = 0.012 \pm 0.0135 ,
\]
that is $(-0.0015,\ 0.0255)$, which includes impossible negative values. The Wilson interval, from \eqref{eq:wilson},
is $(0.0041,\ 0.0347)$, and the Clopper--Pearson interval is $(0.0025,\ 0.0347)$. The two good intervals are asymmetric about
$\hat p$, as they should be near a boundary.

With $x = 0$ the exact upper limit solves $(1 - p)^{250} = 0.025$, giving $p_U = 1 - 0.025^{1/250} = 0.0146$, close to
$-\ln(0.025)/250 = 3.69/250$. The same argument for a one-sided $95\%$ limit gives $-\ln(0.05)/n \approx 3/n$, the ``rule of three''.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
In a cohort followed for a total of $12{,}400$ person-years, no cases of a rare cancer occur. Give a $95\%$ upper confidence limit for
the incidence rate. How many person-years would be needed for the upper limit to fall below $1$ per $10{,}000$ person-years if again no
cases were seen?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
With $D = 0$, \eqref{eq:exactrate} gives $\lambda_U = \chi^2_{2,\,0.975}/(2T) = 7.378/(2 \times 12400) = 2.97$ per $10{,}000$ person-years; equivalently $P(D = 0) = e^{-\mu} = 0.025$
gives $\mu = 3.689$ and $\lambda_U = 3.689/T$. For $3.689/T < 10^{-4}$ needs $T > 36{,}890$ person-years.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
\label{prob:compete}
People are subject to a constant hazard $\lambda$ of a disease and a constant hazard $\mu$ of death from other causes, independently. Show that
the probability of developing the disease by time $t$ is
\[
\frac{\lambda}{\lambda + \mu}\bigl(1 - e^{-(\lambda + \mu)t}\bigr) .
\]
Evaluate it for $\lambda = 0.01$, $\mu = 0.04$ per year and $t = 20$, and compare with $1 - e^{-\lambda t}$.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
A person is alive and disease-free at time $u$ with probability $e^{-(\lambda + \mu)u}$, since the two hazards add. The probability of developing the
disease in $(u, u + du]$ is that times $\lambda\,du$. Integrating,
\[
\int_0^t \lambda e^{-(\lambda + \mu)u}\,du = \frac{\lambda}{\lambda + \mu}\bigl(1 - e^{-(\lambda + \mu)t}\bigr) .
\]
With the given values: $0.2(1 - e^{-1}) = 0.2 \times 0.632 = 0.126$, against $1 - e^{-0.2} = 0.181$ without competing deaths. The second figure is the risk in a
hypothetical population where nobody dies of anything else; the first is the proportion who actually develop the disease. Both are
legitimate, but they answer different questions, and a risk reported in an elderly population must say which is meant.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
Using Example~\ref{exa:persontime}, suppose instead that a researcher computed the risk as $2/8 = 0.25$ over five years. Explain what is
wrong. If the rate is constant, what five-year risk does the rate $0.08$ per year imply?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
The denominator $8$ treats all eight workers as observed for five years, but they contributed only $25$ of the possible $40$ person-years.
Workers 3, 5 and 8 entered late, worker 4 moved away and worker 7 died, so their disease status at five years is unknown; treating them
as disease-free underestimates the risk. Under a constant hazard the five-year risk is $1 - e^{-0.08 \times 5} = 1 - e^{-0.4} = 0.33$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
\label{prob:prevbias}
A chronic disease has incidence $2$ per $1000$ person-years in non-smokers and $4$ per $1000$ in smokers, so the incidence rate ratio is $2$.
Among smokers the disease lasts (until death) $4$ years on average, among non-smokers $8$ years. In a steady state, find the prevalence in each
group and the prevalence ratio. What would a cross-sectional study conclude?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Prevalence odds are $0.004 \times 4 = 0.016$ in smokers and $0.002 \times 8 = 0.016$ in non-smokers, so both prevalences are $0.016/1.016 = 1.57\%$ and
the prevalence ratio is $1$. Smoking doubles the incidence but halves the duration, and a cross-sectional study, which sees prevalence,
would find no association. By Theorem~\ref{thm:steady} the prevalence odds ratio equals the incidence rate ratio times the ratio of mean
durations, here $2 \times \tfrac12$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
In Example~\ref{exa:towns}, suppose town B has lower death rates than town A in both age groups: $1.5$ per $1000$ under $50$ and $18$ per $1000$ at
$50$ and over. Find town B's crude rate and compare it with town A's. Explain the result using Proposition~\ref{prop:crude}.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Deaths in B are $40 \times 1.5 + 60 \times 18 = 60 + 1080 = 1140$, a crude rate of $11.4$ per $1000$, still twice town A's $5.6$. The crude rate weights the
specific rates by the town's own age distribution: $0.4 \times 1.5 + 0.6 \times 18$ for B against $0.8 \times 2 + 0.2 \times 20$ for A. Town B puts $60\%$ of its
weight on the high old-age rate, town A only $20\%$, and that difference outweighs B's lower rates in both groups. The crude comparison
reverses the direction of the age-specific comparisons.
\end{solution}

\section{Measures of association}
\label{sec:association}

A measure of association compares the frequency of disease between exposed and unexposed people. This section defines the measures,
derives their sampling distributions and confidence intervals, gives the tests of no association, and shows which measure each kind of
case-control study estimates. Throughout, the $2 \times 2$ table is laid out as in Section~\ref{sec:occurrence}: $a$ and $b$ are the exposed and
unexposed cases, $c$ and $d$ the exposed and unexposed non-cases, $n_1 = a + c$, $n_0 = b + d$, $m_1 = a + b$, $m_0 = c + d$.

\subsection{Comparing two risks}

With risks $p_1$ among the exposed and $p_0$ among the unexposed, the three measures of Section~\ref{sec:occurrence} are
\[
\RR = \frac{p_1}{p_0}, \qquad \RD = p_1 - p_0, \qquad \OR = \frac{p_1/(1 - p_1)}{p_0/(1 - p_0)} .
\]
No association means $\RR = \OR = 1$ and $\RD = 0$. The ratios lie in $(0, \infty)$, the difference in $(-1, 1)$.

The measures answer different questions. The ratios measure the \emph{strength} of an association: how many times more frequent the
disease is among the exposed. Strength is what bears on causation, since a large ratio is hard to produce by confounding alone. The
difference measures the \emph{impact}: how many cases per person are added by the exposure, which is what matters for deciding
whether to remove it. Smoking raises the risk of lung cancer about tenfold and of heart disease about twofold, yet because heart
disease is so much more common it causes more extra deaths from heart disease than from lung cancer. Section~\ref{sec:attributable}
develops this into the attributable fractions.

The odds ratio has two properties the risk ratio lacks. It is the only one of the three that a case-control study can estimate
(Theorem~\ref{thm:orinv}). And it is symmetric in the outcome: the odds ratio for \emph{not} getting the disease is exactly $1/\OR$, while the
risk ratio for not getting it, $(1 - p_1)/(1 - p_0)$, is not $1/\RR$ (Problem~\ref{prob:symmetry}). Its drawback is that it is not what most people
mean by ``how many times more likely''; reported for a common outcome as if it were a risk ratio, it exaggerates
(Section~\ref{sec:estimable}).

\subsection{Inference for the risk ratio and risk difference}
\label{sec:rrci}

In a cohort study $a \sim \text{Bin}(n_1, p_1)$ and $b \sim \text{Bin}(n_0, p_0)$ independently, and the estimates are $\hat p_1 = a/n_1$ and $\hat p_0 = b/n_0$. The
sampling distributions of the ratio measures are much closer to normal on the log scale, and their variances are found by the delta
method.

\begin{lem}[Delta method]
\label{lem:delta}
If $\sqrt n(\hat\theta - \theta) \to N(0, \sigma^2)$ in distribution and $g$ is differentiable at $\theta$ with $g'(\theta) \neq 0$, then
$\sqrt n\{g(\hat\theta) - g(\theta)\} \to N(0, g'(\theta)^2\sigma^2)$. So $\Var g(\hat\theta) \approx g'(\theta)^2 \Var(\hat\theta)$.
\end{lem}

\begin{proof}
By Taylor's theorem $g(\hat\theta) - g(\theta) = g'(\theta)(\hat\theta - \theta) + r(\hat\theta)(\hat\theta - \theta)$, where $r(\hat\theta) \to 0$ as $\hat\theta \to \theta$. Since $\hat\theta \to \theta$ in
probability, $r(\hat\theta) \to 0$ in probability, and $\sqrt n(\hat\theta - \theta)$ is bounded in probability, so the remainder times $\sqrt n$ tends to $0$. The
result follows from Slutsky's theorem.
\end{proof}

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:logrr}
In a cohort study,
\[
\Var(\log \widehat{\RR}) \approx \frac{1 - p_1}{n_1 p_1} + \frac{1 - p_0}{n_0 p_0} ,
\]
estimated by
\[
\frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{b} - \frac{1}{n_0} ,
\]
and $\Var(\widehat{\RD}) = p_1(1 - p_1)/n_1 + p_0(1 - p_0)/n_0$ exactly.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
$\log\widehat{\RR} = \log\hat p_1 - \log\hat p_0$, the difference of two independent terms. With $g = \log$, $g'(p) = 1/p$, so
$\Var(\log\hat p_1) \approx p_1(1 - p_1)/(n_1 p_1^2) = (1 - p_1)/(n_1 p_1)$; substituting $\hat p_1 = a/n_1$ gives $(1 - a/n_1)/a = 1/a - 1/n_1$. The
same holds for $\hat p_0$, and the variances add. The risk difference is a difference of independent binomial proportions.
\end{proof}

The $95\%$ interval for the risk ratio is $\widehat{\RR}\,\exp(\pm 1.96\,\text{SE})$, with SE the square root of the estimated variance (often called
the Katz interval), and for the risk difference $\widehat{\RD} \pm 1.96\,\text{SE}$. The interval for the ratio is asymmetric about the estimate, as it
should be for a quantity bounded below by $0$.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:cohort}
In a cohort study $1200$ exposed workers and $2400$ unexposed workers are followed for five years.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|cc|c}
 & Exposed & Unexposed & Total \\
\hline
Cases & $60$ & $48$ & $108$ \\
Non-cases & $1140$ & $2352$ & $3492$ \\
\hline
Total & $1200$ & $2400$ & $3600$ \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The risks are $0.05$ and $0.02$, so $\widehat{\RR} = 2.5$. Then
\[
\text{SE}(\log\widehat{\RR}) = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{60} - \tfrac{1}{1200} + \tfrac{1}{48} - \tfrac{1}{2400}} = 0.190 ,
\]
and the interval is $2.5\,e^{\pm 1.96 \times 0.190} = (1.72,\ 3.63)$. The risk difference is $0.030$ with
$\text{SE} = \sqrt{0.05 \times 0.95/1200 + 0.02 \times 0.98/2400} = 0.0069$, giving $(0.016,\ 0.044)$: between $16$ and $44$ extra cases per $1000$ workers over
five years.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Inference for the odds ratio}
\label{sec:orci}

\begin{thm}[Woolf]
\label{thm:woolf}
In either a cohort study or a case-control study,
\[
\Var(\log\widehat{\OR}) \approx \frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{b} + \frac{1}{c} + \frac{1}{d} ,
\]
where $\widehat{\OR} = ad/(bc)$ and the cell counts on the right are the observed ones.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
In a cohort study $\log\widehat{\OR} = \operatorname{logit}\hat p_1 - \operatorname{logit}\hat p_0$. The derivative of $\operatorname{logit} p = \log\{p/(1 - p)\}$ is $1/\{p(1 - p)\}$, so by
Lemma~\ref{lem:delta}
\[
\Var(\operatorname{logit}\hat p_1) \approx \frac{1}{p_1^2(1 - p_1)^2} \cdot \frac{p_1(1 - p_1)}{n_1} = \frac{1}{n_1 p_1(1 - p_1)} .
\]
Substituting $\hat p_1 = a/n_1$, $1 - \hat p_1 = c/n_1$ gives $n_1/(ac) = (a + c)/(ac) = 1/a + 1/c$. Likewise the unexposed contribute $1/b + 1/d$.

In a case-control study the binomial samples are the cases, of whom $a$ of $m_1$ are exposed, and the controls, $c$ of $m_0$. The
estimate $ad/(bc)$ is the ratio of the exposure odds $a/b$ and $c/d$, and the same argument applied to $\operatorname{logit}(a/m_1)$ and
$\operatorname{logit}(c/m_0)$ gives $1/a + 1/b$ and $1/c + 1/d$. The sum is the same.
\end{proof}

The symmetry of the variance in the four cells reflects the symmetry of the odds ratio between exposure and disease. If any cell is
zero, $\widehat{\OR}$ is $0$ or infinite and the variance is infinite; the usual remedy is to add $\tfrac12$ to every cell
(\emph{Haldane's correction}) before computing both. Exact methods, which avoid the problem, come with the conditional distribution of
Section~\ref{sec:tests}.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:casecontrol}
A case-control study of $150$ cases and $300$ controls finds:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|cc|c}
 & Exposed & Unexposed & Total \\
\hline
Cases & $60$ & $90$ & $150$ \\
Controls & $66$ & $234$ & $300$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Then $\widehat{\OR} = (60 \times 234)/(90 \times 66) = 2.36$, and
\[
\text{SE}(\log\widehat{\OR}) = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{60} + \tfrac{1}{90} + \tfrac{1}{66} + \tfrac{1}{234}} = 0.217 ,
\]
so the $95\%$ interval is $2.36\,e^{\pm 1.96 \times 0.217} = (1.54,\ 3.62)$. In the cohort of Example~\ref{exa:cohort} the odds ratio is
$(60 \times 2352)/(48 \times 1140) = 2.58$, with interval $(1.75,\ 3.79)$, a little further from $1$ than the risk ratio $2.5$, as it must be
(Problem~\ref{prob:orrr}).
\end{exa}

\subsection{Testing for no association}
\label{sec:tests}

\subhead{The chi-squared test}

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:chisq}
For a $2 \times 2$ table, Pearson's statistic $X^2 = \sum (O - E)^2/E$, with expected counts $E$ computed from the margins, is
\[
X^2 = \frac{n(ad - bc)^2}{n_1 n_0 m_1 m_0} ,
\]
and it equals $z^2$, where $z = (\hat p_1 - \hat p_0)/\sqrt{\hat p(1 - \hat p)(1/n_1 + 1/n_0)}$ is the two-sample test statistic for proportions with pooled
$\hat p = m_1/n$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
The expected count in the exposed-case cell is $n_1 m_1/n$, and
\[
a - \frac{n_1 m_1}{n} = \frac{a(a + b + c + d) - (a + c)(a + b)}{n} = \frac{ad - bc}{n} .
\]
Since the margins of $O$ and $E$ agree, the other three deviations are $\pm(ad - bc)/n$ too. Hence $X^2 = \{(ad - bc)/n\}^2 \sum 1/E$, and
\begin{align*}
\sum \frac{1}{E} &= n\Bigl(\frac{1}{n_1 m_1} + \frac{1}{n_1 m_0} + \frac{1}{n_0 m_1} + \frac{1}{n_0 m_0}\Bigr) \\
&= \frac{n(n_0 + n_1)(m_0 + m_1)}{n_1 n_0 m_1 m_0} = \frac{n^3}{n_1 n_0 m_1 m_0} .
\end{align*}
For the second claim, $\hat p_1 - \hat p_0 = a/n_1 - b/n_0 = (an_0 - bn_1)/(n_1 n_0) = (ad - bc)/(n_1 n_0)$, and
$\hat p(1 - \hat p)(1/n_1 + 1/n_0) = m_1 m_0 n/(n^2 n_1 n_0)$. Squaring and dividing gives $n(ad - bc)^2/(n_1 n_0 m_1 m_0)$.
\end{proof}

Under no association $X^2$ is approximately $\chi^2_1$. In Example~\ref{exa:cohort}, $X^2 = 24.7$ ($P < 0.0001$), and in
Example~\ref{exa:casecontrol}, $X^2 = 16.1$ ($P = 0.00006$). The approximation needs expected counts that are not too small; a common rule
is all four at least $5$.

\subhead{The conditional distribution and Fisher's exact test}

\begin{thm}
\label{thm:hyper}
In a cohort study with $p_1 = p_0$, the conditional distribution of $a$ given the total number of cases $m_1$ is hypergeometric:
\[
P(a = k \mid m_1) = \frac{\binom{n_1}{k}\binom{n_0}{m_1 - k}}{\binom{n}{m_1}} ,
\]
with mean $n_1 m_1/n$ and variance $n_1 n_0 m_1 m_0/\{n^2(n - 1)\}$. The same distribution holds in a case-control study under $q_1 = q_0$,
conditioning on $n_1$.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
With common risk $p$, $P(a = k,\ b = m_1 - k) = \binom{n_1}{k}\binom{n_0}{m_1 - k} p^{m_1}(1 - p)^{n - m_1}$, and $m_1 \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)$ has probability
$\binom{n}{m_1}p^{m_1}(1 - p)^{n - m_1}$. The ratio is the stated probability, free of $p$. It is the distribution of the number of exposed people among
$m_1$ drawn without replacement from $n$ of whom $n_1$ are exposed, whose mean and variance are the standard hypergeometric moments.
The case-control statement is the same argument with the roles of exposure and disease exchanged.
\end{proof}

Conditioning removes the unknown common risk, so the distribution can be used exactly. \textbf{Fisher's exact test} computes the
$P$-value as the conditional probability of a table at least as extreme as the one observed. The hypergeometric mean and variance are
the building blocks of the Mantel--Haenszel test, later in the course.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:fisher}
In a small outbreak investigation, $6$ of $10$ people who ate a dish became ill, against $2$ of $12$ who did not. Here $n = 22$, $n_1 = 10$,
$m_1 = 8$, and $\widehat{\OR} = (6 \times 10)/(2 \times 4) = 7.5$. Given $8$ cases, the number among the $10$ who ate the dish is hypergeometric with
probabilities
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccccccccc}
\hline
$k$ & 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 & 7 & 8 \\
\hline
$P$ & .0016 & .0248 & .1300 & .2972 & .3251 & .1734 & .0433 & .0045 & .0001 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The one-sided $P$-value is $P(a \geq 6) = 0.0433 + 0.0045 + 0.0001 = 0.048$. The two-sided value, adding the probabilities of all tables no
more probable than the observed one ($k = 0, 1, 6, 7, 8$), is $0.074$. The chi-squared test gives $X^2 = 4.43$, $P = 0.035$, too small, because
three expected counts are under $5$.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Comparing rates}
\label{sec:rateratio}

With $D_1$ cases in $T_1$ person-years among the exposed and $D_0$ in $T_0$ among the unexposed, the \textbf{incidence rate ratio} and
\textbf{rate difference} are estimated by
\[
\widehat{\text{IRR}} = \frac{D_1/T_1}{D_0/T_0}, \qquad \widehat{\text{IRD}} = \frac{D_1}{T_1} - \frac{D_0}{T_0} .
\]
By Theorem~\ref{thm:poissonlik} the counts behave as independent Poisson variables with means $\lambda_1 T_1$ and $\lambda_0 T_0$, so
$\text{SE}(\log\widehat{\text{IRR}}) = \sqrt{1/D_1 + 1/D_0}$ and $\text{SE}(\widehat{\text{IRD}}) = \sqrt{D_1/T_1^2 + D_0/T_0^2}$.

\begin{thm}
\label{thm:ratecond}
If $D_1 \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_1 T_1)$ and $D_0 \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_0 T_0)$ independently, then given $D = D_1 + D_0$,
\[
D_1 \sim \text{Bin}(D, \pi), \qquad \pi = \frac{\text{IRR}\;T_1}{\text{IRR}\;T_1 + T_0} ,
\]
which depends on the rates only through $\text{IRR} = \lambda_1/\lambda_0$.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
$D \sim \text{Poisson}(\mu_1 + \mu_0)$ with $\mu_i = \lambda_i T_i$, so
\begin{align*}
P(D_1 = k \mid D) &= \frac{e^{-\mu_1}\mu_1^k/k! \cdot e^{-\mu_0}\mu_0^{D - k}/(D - k)!}{e^{-(\mu_1 + \mu_0)}(\mu_1 + \mu_0)^D/D!} \\
&= \binom{D}{k}\Bigl(\frac{\mu_1}{\mu_1 + \mu_0}\Bigr)^k\Bigl(\frac{\mu_0}{\mu_1 + \mu_0}\Bigr)^{D - k} .
\end{align*}
Dividing numerator and denominator of $\mu_1/(\mu_1 + \mu_0)$ by $\lambda_0$ gives $\pi$.
\end{proof}

Under no association $\pi = T_1/(T_1 + T_0)$, the share of person-time that is exposed, and the exact test is a binomial test. An exact
interval for $\pi$ (Clopper--Pearson) transforms into one for the rate ratio through $\text{IRR} = \{\pi/(1 - \pi)\}\,T_0/T_1$, which is increasing in $\pi$.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:rates}
Among exposed workers $30$ cases occur in $2500$ person-years; among unexposed workers, $25$ cases occur in $5000$
person-years. The rates are $12$ and $5$ per $1000$ person-years, so
\[
\widehat{\text{IRR}} = 2.4, \qquad \text{SE}(\log\widehat{\text{IRR}}) = \sqrt{1/30 + 1/25} = 0.271 ,
\]
and the interval is $(1.41,\ 4.08)$. Conditionally on the $55$ cases, under no association $D_1 \sim \text{Bin}(55, 1/3)$. The observed $30$,
against an expected $18.3$, gives a one-sided exact $P$ of $0.0010$. The exact interval for $\pi$ is $(0.406,\ 0.680)$, which transforms
to $(1.36,\ 4.26)$ for the rate ratio. The rate difference is $7.0$ per $1000$ person-years, with interval $(2.3,\ 11.7)$.
\end{exa}

\subsection{What a case-control odds ratio estimates}
\label{sec:ccsampling}

Section~\ref{sec:estimable} showed that the case-control odds ratio estimates the disease odds ratio, which approximates the risk ratio
for a rare disease. How the controls are chosen decides what it estimates, and two ways of choosing them remove the need for the
rare disease assumption altogether.

Consider a cohort followed over a period, in which $A_1$ of $N_1$ exposed and $A_0$ of $N_0$ unexposed people become cases, with $T_1$, $T_0$
person-years at risk. All cases are taken; controls are sampled in one of three ways.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Cumulative} sampling: controls from those still free of disease at the end of the period. Their exposure odds estimate
$(N_1 - A_1)/(N_0 - A_0)$, and the odds ratio estimates
\[
\frac{A_1/(N_1 - A_1)}{A_0/(N_0 - A_0)} ,
\]
the risk odds ratio.
\item \textbf{Case-cohort} (case-base) sampling: controls from the whole cohort as it was at the start, regardless of later disease.
Their exposure odds estimate $N_1/N_0$, and the odds ratio estimates $(A_1/A_0)/(N_1/N_0) = (A_1/N_1)/(A_0/N_0)$, the \emph{risk ratio}.
\item \textbf{Density} (risk-set) sampling: whenever a case occurs, controls are drawn from those at risk at that moment. A person is then
eligible as a control for as long as they are at risk, so the chance that a control is exposed is proportional to exposed person-time.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{thm}[Density sampling]
\label{thm:density}
Under density sampling, if the exposure odds among controls estimate $T_1/T_0$, the odds ratio estimates the incidence rate ratio
$\lambda_1/\lambda_0$, with no rare disease assumption.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
The expected numbers of cases are $A_1 = \lambda_1 T_1$ and $A_0 = \lambda_0 T_0$, so the exposure odds among cases are $\lambda_1 T_1/(\lambda_0 T_0)$. Each control is
a person drawn from those at risk at a time point, so it is exposed with probability equal to the exposed share of the person-time at
risk, and the control exposure odds are $T_1/T_0$. The ratio is $\{\lambda_1 T_1/(\lambda_0 T_0)\}/(T_1/T_0) = \lambda_1/\lambda_0$.
\end{proof}

A person sampled as a control under density sampling may later become a case, and should then appear in both roles; a person may also
be sampled as a control more than once. Excluding such people reintroduces the rare disease assumption. Nested case-control studies
within large cohorts, in which exposure is measured (say, from stored blood) only for the cases and a few matched controls per case,
use density sampling and estimate rate ratios directly.

\subsection{Problems}

\begin{problem}
In a cohort study, $32$ of $400$ exposed and $18$ of $600$ unexposed people develop the disease. Estimate the risk ratio, the risk
difference and the odds ratio, each with a $95\%$ confidence interval.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$\hat p_1 = 0.08$ and $\hat p_0 = 0.03$.
$\widehat{\RR} = 2.67$, with
\[
\text{SE}(\log\widehat{\RR}) = \sqrt{1/32 - 1/400 + 1/18 - 1/600} = 0.287
\]
and interval $(1.52,\ 4.68)$. $\widehat{\RD} = 0.050$, with
\[
\text{SE}(\widehat{\RD}) = \sqrt{0.08 \times 0.92/400 + 0.03 \times 0.97/600} = 0.0152
\]
and interval $(0.020,\ 0.080)$. $\widehat{\OR} = (32 \times 582)/(18 \times 368) = 2.81$, with
\[
\text{SE}(\log\widehat{\OR}) = \sqrt{1/32 + 1/18 + 1/368 + 1/582} = 0.302
\]
and interval $(1.56,\ 5.08)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
\label{prob:symmetry}
Show that the odds ratio for remaining free of disease is $1/\OR$. Find the risk ratio for remaining free of disease when $p_1 = 0.2$ and
$p_0 = 0.1$, and compare it with $1/\RR$.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
The odds of remaining free are $(1 - p_i)/p_i$, the reciprocal of the disease odds, so their ratio is the reciprocal of $\OR$. With
$p_1 = 0.2$ and $p_0 = 0.1$, $\RR = 2$ but the risk ratio for remaining free is $0.8/0.9 = 0.889$, not $0.5$. The same data are a doubling of the
risk of disease and an $11\%$ reduction in the chance of staying well. The risk ratio depends on which outcome is called the event;
the odds ratio does not ($2.25$ one way, $1/2.25$ the other).
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
A case-control study finds no exposed cases among $6$ cases and $20$ exposed among $60$ controls, so the table is $a = 0$, $b = 6$, $c = 20$,
$d = 40$. Compute the odds ratio and its Woolf interval after adding $\tfrac12$ to every cell. What does the interval say?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
With the correction, $\widehat{\OR} = (0.5 \times 40.5)/(6.5 \times 20.5) = 0.152$ and $\text{SE}(\log\widehat{\OR}) = \sqrt{2 + 1/6.5 + 1/20.5 + 1/40.5} = 1.49$, so the interval
is $0.152\,e^{\pm 2.92} = (0.008,\ 2.8)$. The point estimate suggests strong protection, but the interval includes values well above $1$:
six cases cannot distinguish a protective exposure from a moderately harmful one. The point estimate depends strongly on the
arbitrary $\tfrac12$ and should not be reported alone.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
In a population, exposed people contribute $20{,}000$ person-years at risk with an incidence rate of $6$ per $1000$, and unexposed people
$80{,}000$ person-years with $2$ per $1000$. A density-sampled case-control study takes all cases and four controls per case. Find the
expected table and its odds ratio.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Cases: $120$ exposed and $160$ unexposed, $280$ in all. Controls: $1120$, of whom a share $20{,}000/100{,}000 = 0.2$ are exposed, so $224$ exposed and $896$
unexposed. The odds ratio is $(120 \times 896)/(160 \times 224) = 3.0 = 6/2$, the rate ratio, as Theorem~\ref{thm:density} says.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
In Example~\ref{exa:fisher}, verify $P(a = 6 \mid m_1 = 8) = 0.0433$ from the hypergeometric formula, and find the mean and variance of $a$ under
no association.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$P(a = 6) = \binom{10}{6}\binom{12}{2}/\binom{22}{8} = 210 \times 66/319{,}770 = 13{,}860/319{,}770 = 0.0433$. The mean is
$n_1 m_1/n = 10 \times 8/22 = 3.64$ and the variance $n_1 n_0 m_1 m_0/\{n^2(n - 1)\} = 10 \times 12 \times 8 \times 14/(484 \times 21) = 1.32$. The observed $6$ is
$(6 - 3.64)/\sqrt{1.32} = 2.05$ standard deviations above the mean.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
In a case-cohort study, a random $5\%$ of a cohort of $20{,}000$ is sampled at the start as the subcohort; $250$ of its $1000$ members
are exposed. Over ten years the cohort produces $150$ exposed cases and $200$ unexposed cases. Estimate the ten-year risk ratio. Why is
the rare disease assumption not needed?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
The odds ratio of cases against the subcohort is $(150/200)/(250/750) = 2.25$. The subcohort's exposure odds estimate
$N_1/N_0$, so the odds ratio estimates
\[
\frac{A_1/A_0}{N_1/N_0} = \frac{A_1/N_1}{A_0/N_0} ,
\]
the ten-year risk ratio. The controls represent the whole cohort at the start, including people who later become cases, so the
comparison is between the cases and the people at risk at baseline, which is exactly what a risk ratio compares. No approximation is
involved.
\end{solution}

\section{Attributable risk}
\label{sec:attributable}

A risk ratio says how strongly an exposure is associated with a disease; it does not say how much disease the exposure is responsible
for. A tenfold risk from an exposure that almost nobody has accounts for few cases, while a doubling of risk from an exposure that half
the population has may account for a third of them. The \emph{attributable} measures answer the question a health authority asks: how
much of the disease would be avoided if the exposure were removed? They have a causal reading only to the extent that the association
is causal and unconfounded, and the formulas below assume this.

\subsection{Attributable risk among the exposed}

\begin{defn}
The \textbf{attributable risk} among the exposed is the risk difference $p_1 - p_0$: the excess risk in the exposed over what they would have
had without exposure. The \textbf{attributable fraction among the exposed} is the proportion of the exposed group's cases that is due to
exposure,
\[
\text{AF}_E = \frac{p_1 - p_0}{p_1} = \frac{\RR - 1}{\RR} .
\]
\end{defn}

The second form follows by dividing numerator and denominator by $p_0$; it can be estimated from a case-control study by putting the
odds ratio in place of $\RR$. With $\RR = 2.5$ (Example~\ref{exa:cohort}) the attributable risk among the exposed is $0.05 - 0.02 = 0.03$ over five
years and $\text{AF}_E = 1.5/2.5 = 0.6$: three in five cases among exposed workers would not have occurred without the exposure.

When an exposure \emph{lowers} risk, as a vaccine does, the corresponding quantity is the \textbf{preventable fraction among the exposed},
\[
\text{PF}_E = \frac{p_0 - p_1}{p_0} = 1 - \RR ,
\]
the proportion of the cases the exposed would have had that the exposure prevented. In vaccine trials it is called the \textbf{vaccine
efficacy}.

The risk difference also has a clinical form. In a trial with a treatment that lowers the risk by $\RD = p_0 - p_1$, the \textbf{number
needed to treat}, $\text{NNT} = 1/\RD$, is the number of patients who must be treated to prevent one event; for a harmful exposure $1/(p_1 - p_0)$ is
the \emph{number needed to harm}. In Example~\ref{exa:cohort}, one extra case occurs for every $1/0.03 = 33$ workers exposed for five years.

\subsection{The population attributable fraction}

\begin{defn}
Let $p$ be the risk in the whole population and $p_0$ the risk among the unexposed. The \textbf{population attributable risk} is $p - p_0$, and
the \textbf{population attributable fraction} is
\[
\text{PAF} = \frac{p - p_0}{p} ,
\]
the proportion of all cases in the population that would not have occurred had nobody been exposed.
\end{defn}

The definition uses $p_0$ as the risk the whole population would have without exposure, which is the assumption that the unexposed are
exchangeable with the exposed (Section~\ref{sec:causation}). Two formulas compute the PAF from quantities that the usual designs estimate.

\begin{thm}[Levin's formula]
\label{thm:levin}
If a proportion $P_e$ of the population is exposed, then
\[
\text{PAF} = \frac{P_e(\RR - 1)}{1 + P_e(\RR - 1)} .
\]
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
The population risk is the average of the group risks, $p = P_e p_1 + (1 - P_e)p_0 = p_0\{1 + P_e(\RR - 1)\}$. Hence $p - p_0 = p_0 P_e(\RR - 1)$, and dividing by $p$
gives the result.
\end{proof}

\begin{thm}[Miettinen's formula]
\label{thm:miettinen}
If a proportion $P_c$ of the \emph{cases} is exposed, then
\[
\text{PAF} = P_c\,\frac{\RR - 1}{\RR} = P_c\,\text{AF}_E .
\]
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
All cases due to exposure are among the exposed cases, and among those a fraction $\text{AF}_E$ is due to exposure. Formally, the number of
excess cases per head of population is $P_e(p_1 - p_0)$, and the total number of cases per head is $p$, so
\[
\text{PAF} = \frac{P_e(p_1 - p_0)}{p} = \frac{P_e p_1}{p} \cdot \frac{p_1 - p_0}{p_1} .
\]
The first factor is the proportion of cases that are exposed, $P_c$, and the second is $\text{AF}_E$.
\end{proof}

The PAF depends on both the strength of the association and how common the exposure is. Levin's formula needs the prevalence of
exposure in the population and suits cohort or cross-sectional data. Miettinen's formula needs the prevalence of exposure among cases, which a case-control study measures directly, with the odds ratio in place of $\RR$. Miettinen's form has a further advantage that matters
in later sections: when the risk ratio has to be adjusted for confounding, substituting the adjusted ratio into Miettinen's formula gives a
valid PAF, while substituting it into Levin's does not.

\begin{exa}
In Example~\ref{exa:cohort} a third of the cohort is exposed and the overall risk is $108/3600 = 0.03$. Directly,
$\text{PAF} = (0.03 - 0.02)/0.03 = 1/3$. By Levin, $P_e = 1/3$ and
\[
\text{PAF} = \frac{(1/3)(1.5)}{1 + (1/3)(1.5)} = \frac{0.5}{1.5} = \frac13 .
\]
By Miettinen, $P_c = 60/108 = 0.556$ and $\text{PAF} = 0.556 \times 0.6 = 1/3$. If the cohort is representative, a third of the cases in the
population would be avoided by removing the exposure.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Estimating the PAF from a case-control study}

\begin{thm}
\label{thm:pafcc}
In a case-control study with cells $a, b$ (exposed and unexposed cases) and $c, d$ (exposed and unexposed controls), the estimate of the
PAF obtained from Miettinen's formula with $\widehat{\OR}$ satisfies
\[
1 - \widehat{\text{PAF}} = \frac{b/m_1}{d/m_0} ,
\]
the proportion of cases unexposed divided by the proportion of controls unexposed, and
\[
\Var\{\log(1 - \widehat{\text{PAF}})\} \approx \frac{a}{m_1 b} + \frac{c}{m_0 d} .
\]
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
With $\hat P_c = a/m_1$ and $\widehat{\OR} = ad/(bc)$,
\[
\widehat{\text{PAF}} = \hat P_c - \frac{\hat P_c}{\widehat{\OR}} = \frac{a}{m_1} - \frac{a}{m_1} \cdot \frac{bc}{ad} = \frac{a}{m_1} - \frac{bc}{m_1 d} .
\]
So $1 - \widehat{\text{PAF}} = (m_1 - a)/m_1 + bc/(m_1 d) = b/m_1 + bc/(m_1 d) = (b/m_1)(d + c)/d$, and $(d + c)/d = m_0/d$.

For the variance, $\log(1 - \widehat{\text{PAF}}) = \log(b/m_1) - \log(d/m_0)$, a difference of the logs of two independent binomial proportions. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:delta}, as in Proposition~\ref{prop:logrr}, the log of a proportion $\hat\pi$ from $m$ trials has variance about $(1 - \pi)/(m\pi)$. For $b/m_1$
this is estimated by $(a/m_1)/b = a/(m_1 b)$, and for $d/m_0$ by $c/(m_0 d)$.
\end{proof}

The interval is computed for $1 - \text{PAF}$ on the log scale and transformed back, which keeps the upper limit of the PAF below $1$.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:pafcc}
In Example~\ref{exa:casecontrol}, $a = 60$, $b = 90$, $c = 66$, $d = 234$, $m_1 = 150$, $m_0 = 300$. Then
\[
1 - \widehat{\text{PAF}} = \frac{90/150}{234/300} = \frac{0.600}{0.780} = 0.769 ,
\]
so $\widehat{\text{PAF}} = 0.231$, which agrees with $\hat P_c(\widehat{\OR} - 1)/\widehat{\OR} = 0.4 \times 1.364/2.364$. The standard error is
\[
\text{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{60}{150 \times 90} + \frac{66}{300 \times 234}} = 0.0734 ,
\]
so $1 - \text{PAF}$ lies in $0.769\,e^{\pm 0.144} = (0.666,\ 0.888)$ and the PAF in $(0.112,\ 0.334)$. Removing the exposure would prevent between about
a ninth and a third of the cases. The estimate assumes the controls represent the source population and that the odds ratio is
unconfounded and close to the risk ratio.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Several exposure levels and several factors}

\subhead{An exposure with several levels}

If an exposure has levels $j = 0, 1, \dots, J$ with risk ratios $\RR_j$ relative to the reference level $0$, and a proportion $P_{cj}$ of cases is at
level $j$, Miettinen's argument applied level by level gives
\[
\text{PAF} = \sum_{j \geq 1} P_{cj}\,\frac{\RR_j - 1}{\RR_j} .
\]
Collapsing the levels into ``exposed'' and ``unexposed'' and using a single average risk ratio gives a different, wrong answer
(Problem~\ref{prob:levels}).

\subhead{Attributable fractions for several factors do not add}

When two exposures both affect a disease, the fraction attributable to each can be computed, but the fractions are not shares of a
fixed total. A case caused by the joint action of two exposures would be prevented by removing either, so it is counted in both
fractions.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:twofactors}
Suppose the risk of a disease is $p_0 = 0.001$ in people with neither of two exposures, smoking multiplies it by $10$ and an occupational
exposure by $5$, and the two act multiplicatively, so people with both have risk $0.05$. Half the population smokes and a tenth has the
occupational exposure, independently. The population risk is
\[
p = 0.001(0.5 + 0.5 \times 10)(0.9 + 0.1 \times 5) = 0.001 \times 5.5 \times 1.4 = 0.0077 .
\]
Removing smoking leaves $0.001 \times 1.4 = 0.0014$, so $\text{PAF}_{\text{smoking}} = (0.0077 - 0.0014)/0.0077 = 0.818$. Removing the occupational
exposure leaves $0.001 \times 5.5 = 0.0055$, so $\text{PAF}_{\text{occ}} = 0.286$. The two sum to $1.10$, more than all the cases. Removing both leaves
$0.001$, so the joint PAF is $0.870$. Each separate figure is correct for its own question; what is wrong is to add them, or to read them
as ``$82\%$ of cases are caused by smoking and the rest by something else''.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Preventable fractions in a population}

For a protective exposure, the counterpart of the PAF is the proportion of the cases that would occur without the exposure which it
prevents in the population as a whole.

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:pfpop}
If a proportion $P_e$ of the population has a protective exposure with risk ratio $\RR < 1$, the \textbf{population preventable fraction} is
\[
\text{PF} = \frac{p_0 - p}{p_0} = P_e(1 - \RR) .
\]
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
$p = P_e p_1 + (1 - P_e)p_0$, so $p_0 - p = P_e(p_0 - p_1)$, and dividing by $p_0$ gives $P_e(1 - p_1/p_0)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{exa}
In an outbreak, the attack rate is $2\%$ among vaccinated children and $10\%$ among unvaccinated children. The vaccine efficacy is
$1 - 0.02/0.10 = 0.80$. With $70\%$ coverage, the vaccination programme has prevented $\text{PF} = 0.7 \times 0.8 = 56\%$ of the cases that would have
occurred without it. Raising coverage to $90\%$ would raise this to $72\%$, if the efficacy and the risk among the unvaccinated stayed the
same. In practice they do not, because vaccinated children do not pass infection to unvaccinated ones (herd immunity). The formulas
here ignore this, and so understate the benefit of high coverage.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Problems}

\begin{problem}
Show that Levin's and Miettinen's formulas agree, by expressing $P_c$ in terms of $P_e$ and $\RR$.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$P_c = P_e p_1/p = P_e\RR\,p_0/[p_0\{1 + P_e(\RR - 1)\}] = P_e\RR/\{1 + P_e(\RR - 1)\}$. Then
\[
P_c\,\frac{\RR - 1}{\RR} = \frac{P_e\RR}{1 + P_e(\RR - 1)} \cdot \frac{\RR - 1}{\RR} = \frac{P_e(\RR - 1)}{1 + P_e(\RR - 1)} ,
\]
which is Levin's formula.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
A case-control study gives $a = 40$ exposed and $b = 60$ unexposed cases, and $c = 30$ exposed and $d = 170$ unexposed controls. Estimate the
odds ratio, the attributable fraction among the exposed and the PAF with a $95\%$ confidence interval.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$\widehat{\OR} = (40 \times 170)/(60 \times 30) = 3.78$ and $\widehat{\text{AF}}_E = 2.78/3.78 = 0.735$. From Theorem~\ref{thm:pafcc},
$1 - \widehat{\text{PAF}} = (60/100)/(170/200) = 0.706$, so $\widehat{\text{PAF}} = 0.294$ $(= 0.4 \times 0.735)$. The standard error of $\log(1 - \widehat{\text{PAF}})$ is
$\sqrt{40/(100 \times 60) + 30/(200 \times 170)} = 0.0869$, so $1 - \text{PAF}$ lies in $0.706\,e^{\pm 0.170} = (0.595,\ 0.837)$ and the PAF in $(0.163,\ 0.405)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
\label{prob:levels}
An exposure has three levels, called none, light and heavy, with population prevalences $0.5$, $0.3$, $0.2$ and risk ratios $1$, $2$, $4$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Find the PAF directly and by the formula for several levels.
\item Collapse light and heavy into ``exposed''. Find the risk ratio for exposed against unexposed and the PAF by Levin's formula. Comment.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
(a) Relative to the risk $p_0$ at level none, the population risk is $p_0(0.5 + 0.3 \times 2 + 0.2 \times 4) = 1.9p_0$, so $\text{PAF} = 0.9/1.9 = 0.474$. The
proportions of cases at the three levels are $0.5/1.9$, $0.6/1.9$, $0.8/1.9$, that is $0.263$, $0.316$, $0.421$. Then
$0.316 \times \tfrac12 + 0.421 \times \tfrac34 = 0.158 + 0.316 = 0.474$.

(b) The exposed have risk $p_0(0.3 \times 2 + 0.2 \times 4)/0.5 = 2.8p_0$, so $\RR = 2.8$, and with $P_e = 0.5$ Levin gives $0.5 \times 1.8/(1 + 0.5 \times 1.8) = 0.9/1.9 = 0.474$.
Collapsing does no harm \emph{here}, because the reference group is unchanged and the collapsed risk ratio is the correct average.
It goes wrong if the collapsed ratio is taken from elsewhere (another population with a different mix of light and heavy exposure), or
if the question is the effect of reducing heavy exposure to light, which only the separate ratios can answer.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
In Example~\ref{exa:twofactors}, find the fraction of cases among people with both exposures that is attributable to the combination,
and explain why the separate PAFs sum to more than $1$.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
People with both exposures have risk $0.05$ against $0.001$ with neither, so $(0.05 - 0.001)/0.05 = 98\%$ of their cases are attributable to the
combination. They make up $5\%$ of the population and contribute $0.05 \times 0.05 = 0.0025$ of the population risk of $0.0077$, a third of all
cases. Each of these cases is prevented by removing smoking \emph{or} by removing the occupational exposure, so it is counted in both
PAFs. The sum exceeds $1$ by the share of cases that either removal would prevent.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
In a five-year trial with $1000$ patients per arm, the risk of a stroke is $0.12$ on placebo and $0.08$ on treatment. Find the number needed
to treat and a $95\%$ interval for it.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$\RD = 0.04$, $\text{NNT} = 25$. $\text{SE}(\RD) = \sqrt{0.08 \times 0.92/1000 + 0.12 \times 0.88/1000} = 0.0134$, so $\RD \in (0.0138,\ 0.0662)$. Inverting, the NNT
lies between $1/0.0662 = 15$ and $1/0.0138 = 73$. The inversion is valid only because the interval for $\RD$ excludes $0$; if it did not, the
interval for the NNT would run from a number needed to treat, through infinity, to a number needed to harm.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
Show that $\text{PAF} \leq \text{AF}_E$ and $\text{PAF} \leq P_c$, with equality in the first if and only if everyone with the disease is exposed. When is the PAF close
to $\text{AF}_E$?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
By Miettinen's formula $\text{PAF} = P_c\,\text{AF}_E$ with $0 \leq P_c \leq 1$ and $0 \leq \text{AF}_E \leq 1$ (for $\RR \geq 1$), so $\text{PAF}$ is at most each factor. Equality
$\text{PAF} = \text{AF}_E$ (with $\text{AF}_E > 0$) requires $P_c = 1$. The PAF is close to $\text{AF}_E$ when almost all cases are exposed, as for lung cancer and smoking, where
$\text{AF}_E$ is about $0.9$ and the PAF is about $0.8$ to $0.9$.
\end{solution}

\section{Study design in practice}
\label{sec:design}

Section~\ref{sec:designs} described the basic designs and Section~\ref{sec:ccsampling} what a case-control odds ratio estimates. This
section turns to the decisions that make a cohort or case-control study valid and efficient: where the subjects come from, how
controls are chosen and how many, when to match, and how large the study must be.

\subsection{Cohort studies}

\subhead{Choosing the cohort}

A cohort is chosen for the exposure it offers and for the ease of following it. Rare exposures, typically occupational or medical, are
studied in \textbf{special-exposure cohorts}: workers in one industry, patients given a drug. Common exposures such as smoking or diet
are studied in \textbf{general-population cohorts}, which are chosen for completeness of follow-up rather than for being representative: nurses,
doctors, civil servants, residents of one town. A cohort need not be representative of any population to give valid comparisons between
its exposed and unexposed members; it needs the two to be comparable.

The comparison group may be \textbf{internal} (members of the same cohort with less or no exposure) or \textbf{external} (the general
population, through national rates). External comparison is cheap but suffers from the \textbf{healthy worker effect}: people in
employment are healthier than the general population, which includes those too ill to work, so an occupational cohort usually shows
lower mortality than national rates even when the work is harmful. Internal comparisons avoid this.

\subhead{Follow-up}

Losses to follow-up threaten validity in the way Proposition~\ref{prop:nonresp} described: if the reasons for loss are related to both
exposure and outcome, the risks in those followed are biased. Small losses that are unrelated to outcome cost only precision. The
practical aim is near-complete follow-up, through record linkage to death and cancer registers where possible, and a check that the
people lost resemble those followed in the characteristics recorded at entry.

\subsection{Case-control studies}

\subhead{The study base}

A case-control study is best thought of as an efficient way of sampling from a cohort that is not enumerated. The \textbf{study base} is
the population, and its person-time, from which the cases arose. If it can be defined first (the residents of a district over a period,
the members of an insurance scheme), it is a \textbf{primary base}: all cases arising in it are sought, and controls are sampled from
it. If the cases are defined first (all patients with the disease admitted to one hospital), the base is \textbf{secondary}: the
population of people who would have been admitted to that hospital had they developed the disease. It is real but hard to sample,
which is why hospital-based studies are prone to bias.

The guiding principle is that controls must be sampled from the study base independently of their exposure. They
should represent the exposure distribution of the person-time that gave rise to the cases. Whether the controls resemble the cases in other respects,
or are ``healthy'', is beside the point.

\subhead{Sources of controls}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Population controls}, sampled from a register of the primary base, satisfy the principle most directly. Their
participation is often lower than that of cases, which can bias the study if participation depends on exposure.
\item \textbf{Hospital controls}, patients admitted for other diseases, share the referral pattern of the cases and cooperate readily.
They are valid only if their diseases are unrelated to the exposure: patients with chronic bronchitis are useless controls in a study
of smoking.
\item \textbf{Neighbourhood} or \textbf{friend controls} are chosen by the case or by proximity. They tend to share the case's exposures (friends
smoke together), which biases the odds ratio towards $1$.
\end{itemize}

\subhead{Incident and prevalent cases}

Cases should be \textbf{incident}, that is newly diagnosed during the study period. Prevalent cases over-represent those who survive long with
the disease, and their exposure may be a determinant of survival rather than of occurrence (Problem~\ref{prob:prevbias}). Newly
diagnosed cases also recall past exposure before the illness has changed their habits.

\subhead{How many controls per case}

When cases are scarce, precision can be bought by taking several controls per case. The return diminishes quickly.

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:kcontrols}
With $m_1$ cases and $k m_1$ controls, and exposure probabilities $q_1$ among cases and $q_0$ among controls,
\[
\Var(\log\widehat{\OR}) \approx \frac{1}{m_1}\Bigl\{\frac{1}{q_1(1 - q_1)} + \frac{1}{k\,q_0(1 - q_0)}\Bigr\} .
\]
When $q_1$ and $q_0$ are close, the efficiency relative to an unlimited number of controls is $k/(k + 1)$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
From the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:woolf}, the cases contribute $1/\{m_1 q_1(1 - q_1)\}$ and the controls $1/\{k m_1 q_0(1 - q_0)\}$. As $k \to \infty$ only the first term
remains, and with $q_1 = q_0$ the ratio of the limit to the variance is $1/(1 + 1/k) = k/(k + 1)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccccc}
\hline
Controls per case $k$ & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 10 \\
Relative efficiency & 0.50 & 0.67 & 0.75 & 0.80 & 0.83 & 0.91 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Going from one control to four gains as much as doubling the number of cases would from one-to-one; beyond four or five the gain
rarely justifies the cost.

\subsection{Matching}
\label{sec:matching}

\textbf{Matching} chooses the controls so that their distribution of some variable, usually a strong confounder such as age or sex, is the
same as the cases'. In \textbf{frequency matching} the controls are sampled within strata in proportion to the cases; in
\textbf{individual matching} each case is given one or more controls with the same value (same sex, age within two years).

Matching in a case-control study does \emph{not} by itself remove confounding by the matching variable. It makes the cases and controls
alike on that variable, but the controls are then no longer a random sample of the base, and their exposure distribution is distorted
towards that of the cases. The matched variable must be allowed for in the analysis, by stratification on the matched sets. What
matching achieves is \emph{efficiency}: each stratum of the analysis contains both cases and controls, so none is wasted.

\textbf{Overmatching} means matching on a variable that is associated with exposure but is not a confounder, typically a consequence of
exposure or a close correlate of it. It brings the exposure distributions of cases and controls together for no gain, losing
efficiency and, if the variable lies on the causal path, biasing the odds ratio towards $1$.

\subhead{Matched pairs}

With one control per case, each pair falls into one of four types according to the exposure of the case and of the control.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|cc}
 & Control exposed & Control unexposed \\
\hline
Case exposed & $n_{11}$ & $n_{10}$ \\
Case unexposed & $n_{01}$ & $n_{00}$ \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Concordant pairs ($n_{11}$, $n_{00}$) carry no information about the association; the analysis rests on the discordant pairs.

\begin{thm}
\label{thm:pairs}
Suppose that within each matched pair the odds of exposure among cases are $\psi$ times those among controls, with the baseline odds free to
vary between pairs. Given that a pair is discordant, the probability that it is the case who is exposed is $\psi/(1 + \psi)$, whatever the pair's
baseline odds. Hence, conditionally on $n_{10} + n_{01}$, $n_{10}$ is binomial with this probability, the conditional maximum likelihood estimate of
the odds ratio is
\[
\hat\psi = \frac{n_{10}}{n_{01}} ,
\]
and the test of $\psi = 1$ is \textbf{McNemar's test}, $X^2 = (n_{10} - n_{01})^2/(n_{10} + n_{01})$ on one degree of freedom.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
In pair $i$ let the control's probability of exposure be $\pi_i$, with odds $\omega_i = \pi_i/(1 - \pi_i)$, and the case's odds $\psi\omega_i$, so the case's
probability is $\psi\omega_i/(1 + \psi\omega_i)$. The exposures are independent, so
\begin{align*}
P(\text{case exposed, control not}) &= \frac{\psi\omega_i}{1 + \psi\omega_i} \cdot \frac{1}{1 + \omega_i} , \\
P(\text{control exposed, case not}) &= \frac{1}{1 + \psi\omega_i} \cdot \frac{\omega_i}{1 + \omega_i} .
\end{align*}
Given discordance, the first has probability $\psi\omega_i/(\psi\omega_i + \omega_i) = \psi/(1 + \psi)$; $\omega_i$ cancels. The discordant pairs are therefore independent
Bernoulli trials with success probability $\psi/(1 + \psi)$, and the binomial maximum likelihood estimate $n_{10}/(n_{10} + n_{01})$ of this probability
gives $\hat\psi = n_{10}/n_{01}$. Under $\psi = 1$ the probability is $\tfrac12$, and the score test of a binomial proportion equal to $\tfrac12$ is McNemar's statistic.
\end{proof}

Conditioning eliminates the pair-specific odds $\omega_i$, which could not be estimated, since each pair has only two people. Ignoring the
matching and computing the crude odds ratio from the four margins does not estimate $\psi$ in general, and is biased towards $1$ when the
matching variable is related to exposure.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:pairs}
In $100$ matched pairs, the case alone is exposed in $30$ pairs and the control alone in $12$. Then $\hat\psi = 30/12 = 2.5$, and McNemar's
statistic is $(30 - 12)^2/42 = 7.71$, $P = 0.005$. The approximate interval uses $\text{SE}(\log\hat\psi) = \sqrt{1/30 + 1/12} = 0.342$, giving
$(1.28,\ 4.88)$. An exact interval comes from the Clopper--Pearson interval for $\pi = \psi/(1 + \psi)$ with $30$ successes in $42$, which is
$(0.554,\ 0.843)$, transformed by $\psi = \pi/(1 - \pi)$ to $(1.24,\ 5.36)$.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Sample size for comparing two groups}
\label{sec:samplesize}

A study is too small if it is unlikely to detect an effect of the size that matters, and too large if it spends resources and exposes
people to measurement beyond what the question needs. The calculation needs four inputs: the significance level $\alpha$, the power
$1 - \beta$, the frequency of outcome (or exposure) in the reference group, and the smallest effect worth detecting.

\begin{thm}
\label{thm:ss}
To compare proportions $\pi_1$ and $\pi_0$ with a two-sided level-$\alpha$ test of $\pi_1 = \pi_0$ and power $1 - \beta$, with $n$ subjects in group $1$ and $kn$ in
group $0$, the required $n$ is approximately
\[
n = \frac{\Bigl\{z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{(1 + 1/k)\,\bar\pi(1 - \bar\pi)} + z_\beta\sqrt{\pi_1(1 - \pi_1) + \pi_0(1 - \pi_0)/k}\Bigr\}^2}{(\pi_1 - \pi_0)^2} ,
\]
where $\bar\pi = (\pi_1 + k\pi_0)/(1 + k)$ and $z_\gamma$ is the upper $\gamma$ point of the standard normal.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
The test rejects when $|\hat\pi_1 - \hat\pi_0| > z_{\alpha/2}\,\sigma_0/\sqrt n$, where $\sigma_0^2 = (1 + 1/k)\bar\pi(1 - \bar\pi)$ is $n$ times the variance of $\hat\pi_1 - \hat\pi_0$ under the null
hypothesis. Under the alternative, $\hat\pi_1 - \hat\pi_0$ is approximately normal with mean $\delta = \pi_1 - \pi_0$ and variance $\sigma_1^2/n$, where
$\sigma_1^2 = \pi_1(1 - \pi_1) + \pi_0(1 - \pi_0)/k$. Ignoring the negligible probability of rejecting in the wrong direction, the power is
\[
P\Bigl(\hat\pi_1 - \hat\pi_0 > \frac{z_{\alpha/2}\sigma_0}{\sqrt n}\Bigr) = \Phi\Bigl(\frac{\delta\sqrt n - z_{\alpha/2}\sigma_0}{\sigma_1}\Bigr) .
\]
Setting this equal to $1 - \beta$ requires $(\delta\sqrt n - z_{\alpha/2}\sigma_0)/\sigma_1 = z_\beta$, so $\sqrt n = (z_{\alpha/2}\sigma_0 + z_\beta\sigma_1)/\delta$.
\end{proof}

In a cohort study $\pi_1, \pi_0$ are the risks $p_1, p_0$, and $n$ is the number of exposed subjects. In a case-control study they are the exposure
probabilities $q_1, q_0$ among cases and controls, and $n$ is the number of cases. There the effect is specified as an odds ratio and the
control exposure prevalence $q_0$, from which
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:q1}
q_1 = \frac{\OR\,q_0}{1 + q_0(\OR - 1)} ,
\end{equation}
obtained by solving $q_1/(1 - q_1) = \OR\,q_0/(1 - q_0)$.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:sscohort}
A cohort study is to detect a risk ratio of $1.5$ when the risk among the unexposed is $0.10$, with equal groups, $\alpha = 0.05$ and power
$0.80$ ($z_{\alpha/2} = 1.960$, $z_\beta = 0.842$). With $\pi_1 = 0.15$, $\pi_0 = 0.10$, $\bar\pi = 0.125$,
\begin{gather*}
\sigma_0 = \sqrt{2 \times 0.125 \times 0.875} = 0.4677, \\
\sigma_1 = \sqrt{0.15 \times 0.85 + 0.10 \times 0.90} = 0.4664, \\
n = \frac{(1.960 \times 0.4677 + 0.842 \times 0.4664)^2}{0.05^2} = 685.8
\end{gather*}
so $686$ in each group after rounding up. A simulation of $40{,}000$ such studies gave power $0.804$.
\end{exa}

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:sscc}
A case-control study is to detect an odds ratio of $2$ for an exposure carried by $20\%$ of controls. By \eqref{eq:q1},
$q_1 = 0.4/1.2 = 0.333$. With one control per case, Theorem~\ref{thm:ss} gives $172$ cases and $172$ controls. With four controls per case it gives
$103$ cases and $412$ controls: fewer cases, many more subjects. Which is cheaper depends on whether cases or controls are the scarce
resource.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Sample size for comparing rates}

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:ssrates}
To detect an incidence rate ratio $\theta$ with two-sided level $\alpha$ and power $1 - \beta$, when the exposed share of person-time is $T_1/(T_1 + T_0)$, the
total number of cases needed is approximately
\[
D = \frac{(z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)^2}{\pi(1 - \pi)(\log\theta)^2}, \qquad \pi = \frac{\theta T_1}{\theta T_1 + T_0} .
\]
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:ratecond}, given $D$ cases, $D_1 \sim \text{Bin}(D, \pi)$, and $\log\widehat{\text{IRR}} = \operatorname{logit}\hat\pi - \log(T_1/T_0)$. By the delta method
$\Var(\operatorname{logit}\hat\pi) \approx 1/\{D\pi(1 - \pi)\}$. A level-$\alpha$ test with power $1 - \beta$ against $\log\theta$ needs the standard error to equal
$\log\theta/(z_{\alpha/2} + z_\beta)$; solving for $D$ gives the formula.
\end{proof}

The design question is then turned into person-time, since the number of cases expected per person-year of follow-up determines how long, or
how large, the cohort must be.

\begin{exa}
To detect a rate ratio of $2$ with equal person-time in the two groups ($\pi = 2/3$), at $\alpha = 0.05$ and power $0.80$,
\[
D = \frac{(1.960 + 0.842)^2}{(2/9)(\log 2)^2} = 73.5 ,
\]
so about $74$ cases are needed. If the unexposed rate is $5$ per $1000$ person-years, every $1000$ person-years in each group yield
$10 + 5 = 15$ cases, so $74$ cases need about $4900$ person-years in each group. The formula uses the variance at
the alternative; a simulation of the conditional test gave power $0.83$, so the formula errs slightly on the safe side here.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Power, precision and the size of the effect}

The sample-size formula can be read in three directions. Fixing $n$ and the effect gives the power; fixing $n$ and the power gives the
\textbf{minimum detectable effect}; fixing the effect and the power gives $n$. In epidemiology the number of available cases is often
fixed (all cases of a rare cancer in a region over five years), and the useful calculation is the second: what is the smallest risk
ratio this study could reliably detect?

Two features of the formula govern design. The required size grows as the inverse square of the effect on the log scale, so halving
$\log\RR$ quadruples the study. And for a rare outcome or a rare exposure, the number of \emph{events} in the rarer cell drives
precision, which is why cohort studies of rare diseases and case-control studies of rare exposures are both inefficient.

An alternative to power is to design for \textbf{precision}: choose $n$ so that the confidence interval for the measure of association has
a given width. This is often more honest for an estimation study, whose aim is to measure an association rather than to test whether it
is zero.

\subsection{Problems}

\begin{problem}
How many subjects are needed in each of two equal groups of a cohort study to detect a doubling of a five-year risk of $0.05$, at $\alpha = 0.05$
with power $0.90$?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$\pi_1 = 0.10$, $\pi_0 = 0.05$, $\bar\pi = 0.075$, $z_\beta = 1.282$. Then
\begin{gather*}
\sigma_0 = \sqrt{2 \times 0.075 \times 0.925} = 0.3725, \\
\sigma_1 = \sqrt{0.10 \times 0.90 + 0.05 \times 0.95} = 0.3708, \\
n = \frac{(1.960 \times 0.3725 + 1.282 \times 0.3708)^2}{0.05^2} = 581.3
\end{gather*}
so $582$ in each group after rounding up, $1164$ in all.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
A cohort study has $500$ exposed and $500$ unexposed subjects. What is its power to detect risks of $0.15$ against $0.10$ at $\alpha = 0.05$?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$\delta = 0.05$, $\sigma_0 = \sqrt{2 \times 0.125 \times 0.875} = 0.468$, $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{0.1275 + 0.09} = 0.466$. The power is
$\Phi\{(0.05\sqrt{500} - 1.96 \times 0.468)/0.466\} = \Phi(0.431) = 0.67$. The study has only a two-in-three chance of detecting a risk ratio of $1.5$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
Only $100$ cases are available for a case-control study of an exposure with prevalence $0.2$ among controls. Find the power to detect
an odds ratio of $2$ with $1$, $3$ and $10$ controls per case.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
With $q_1 = 0.333$ and $q_0 = 0.2$, the power formula of Theorem~\ref{thm:ss} with $n = 100$ gives $0.57$ for $k = 1$, $0.76$ for $k = 3$ and $0.84$ for $k = 10$.
Tripling the controls raises the power substantially; going from $3$ to $10$ adds much less, as Proposition~\ref{prop:kcontrols} predicts.
Even with unlimited controls the power would stay below $0.9$, because the $100$ cases fix the first term of the variance.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
In Example~\ref{exa:pairs} there are also $18$ pairs in which both are exposed and $40$ in which neither is. Compute the crude odds ratio from
the $100$ cases and $100$ controls, ignoring the matching, and compare it with $\hat\psi = 2.5$.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Exposed cases: $n_{11} + n_{10} = 18 + 30 = 48$; exposed controls: $n_{11} + n_{01} = 18 + 12 = 30$. The crude odds ratio is
$(48 \times 70)/(52 \times 30) = 3360/1560 = 2.15$. It is closer to $1$ than the matched estimate. Pairs are alike in the matching variable, which here is
associated with exposure, and ignoring the matching treats the controls as if they were a random sample of the base when they have
been drawn towards the cases' exposure distribution.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
A cohort study is planned to detect a rate ratio of $1.5$ with power $0.90$ at $\alpha = 0.05$. The rate among the unexposed is $4$ per $1000$
person-years, and exposed and unexposed will contribute equal person-time. How many cases and how many person-years are needed?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$\pi = 1.5/2.5 = 0.6$, so $D = (1.960 + 1.282)^2/\{0.24\,(\log 1.5)^2\} = 10.51/(0.24 \times 0.1644) = 266$ cases. Each pair of exposed and unexposed person-years
yields $0.004 + 0.006 = 0.010$ cases, so about $26{,}600$ person-years are needed in each group. A modest effect on a rare outcome needs a large
cohort.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
Show that in Proposition~\ref{prop:kcontrols} the variance with $k$ controls per case and $m_1$ cases equals the variance with $k = 1$ and
$m_1' = 2m_1 k/(k + 1)$ cases when $q_1 = q_0$. Interpret.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
With $q_1 = q_0 = q$ the variance is $(1 + 1/k)/\{m_1 q(1 - q)\}$, and with $k = 1$ and $m_1'$ cases it is $2/\{m_1' q(1 - q)\}$. These are equal when
$m_1' = 2m_1/(1 + 1/k) = 2m_1 k/(k + 1)$. With four controls per case, $100$ cases are worth $160$ cases with one control each: extra controls substitute
for cases, but at most $100$ cases with unlimited controls are worth $200$ matched one-to-one.
\end{solution}

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:validity}

\subsection{Random error and bias}

An estimate of a measure of association can be wrong for two kinds of reason. \textbf{Random error} comes from sampling variation; it
shrinks as the study grows and is described by a confidence interval. \textbf{Systematic error}, or \textbf{bias}, is a difference between
what the study estimates on average and the true value in the source population; it does not shrink with size, and no confidence
interval reflects it. A study is \textbf{internally valid} if it is free of bias for the population it sampled. Whether its result
applies to other populations, its \textbf{external validity} or generalisability, is a separate and usually more judgemental question.

Biases are traditionally grouped into three kinds:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{selection bias}, from the way subjects come to be in the study or in the analysis;
\item \textbf{information bias}, from errors in measuring exposure, disease or other variables;
\item \textbf{confounding}, from the mixing of the effect of exposure with the effects of other factors.
\end{enumerate}
The first two are flaws in how the data were obtained. The third is a property of the source population, present even in perfectly
collected data, and it is the one the methods of the following sections are designed to remove.

\subsection{Selection bias}
\label{sec:selection}

Selection bias arises when the probability of being included in the study, or in the analysis, depends on both exposure and disease.
A simple model shows exactly when it biases the odds ratio.

\begin{thm}
\label{thm:selection}
Suppose that people in the source population with disease status $i$ and exposure status $j$ enter the study with probability $s_{ij}$
($i = 1$ for cases, $0$ for non-cases; $j = 1$ for exposed, $0$ for unexposed). Then the odds ratio in the study population is
\[
\OR_{\text{obs}} = \OR \times \frac{s_{11}\,s_{00}}{s_{10}\,s_{01}} ,
\]
where $\OR$ is the odds ratio in the source population.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
If $N_{ij}$ is the number of people in cell $(i, j)$ of the source population, the expected number in the study is $s_{ij}N_{ij}$. The odds ratio of
the expected study table is
\[
\frac{(s_{11}N_{11})(s_{00}N_{00})}{(s_{10}N_{10})(s_{01}N_{01})} = \frac{N_{11}N_{00}}{N_{10}N_{01}} \cdot \frac{s_{11}s_{00}}{s_{10}s_{01}} .
\]
\end{proof}

The \textbf{selection bias factor} $s_{11}s_{00}/(s_{10}s_{01})$ equals $1$ whenever selection depends on disease only ($s_{1j} = s_1$, $s_{0j} = s_0$), which is
exactly what case-control sampling does, and whenever it depends on exposure only, which is what cohort sampling does. Bias arises only
when the selection probabilities depend on exposure and disease \emph{jointly}, in the sense that the ratio of the case's selection
probabilities for exposed and unexposed differs from the non-case's.

\begin{exa}
In a case-control study $90\%$ of eligible cases agree to take part whatever their exposure. Among population controls, people with
the exposure (say, heavy drinkers) are less willing: $50\%$ of exposed and $70\%$ of unexposed controls take part. The bias factor is
$(0.9 \times 0.7)/(0.9 \times 0.5) = 1.4$, so a true odds ratio of $1$ would appear as $1.4$. Response rates of $90\%$ and $60\%$ are not by themselves
a problem; their dependence on exposure among the controls is.
\end{exa}

\subhead{Common forms of selection bias}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Non-response} and \textbf{loss to follow-up} related to both exposure and outcome.
\item \textbf{Hospital controls}, when the conditions for which they were admitted are associated with the exposure
(Problem~\ref{prob:hospital}).
\item \textbf{Berkson's bias}: when admission to hospital depends on having either of two conditions, the conditions become associated
among hospital patients even if they are independent in the population (Problem~\ref{prob:berkson}).
\item The \textbf{healthy worker effect}: employment selects healthier people, so comparing workers with the general population is
biased against finding harm.
\item \textbf{Survival bias}: prevalent cases are survivors (Section~\ref{sec:prevdur}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Information bias}

Errors in measuring a binary exposure or disease were studied in Section~\ref{sec:misclass}. Nondifferential misclassification of a binary exposure biases the odds ratio towards $1$, while differential misclassification can bias it in either direction. Its common sources
are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{recall bias}, when cases remember or report past exposure differently from controls;
\item \textbf{interviewer bias}, when an interviewer who knows the subject's disease status probes harder for exposure in cases;
\item \textbf{detection bias}, when exposure leads to closer medical surveillance and so to earlier or more complete diagnosis of the
disease (women taking hormones see doctors more often, so more of their slow-growing cancers are found).
\end{itemize}
The defences are in the data collection: exposure measured from records made before the disease, interviewers kept unaware of case
status and of the hypothesis, the same procedures for every subject, and objective measures where they exist.

\subsection{Confounding}
\label{sec:confounding}

Section~\ref{sec:causation} defined confounding as the failure of exchangeability: the exposed and unexposed would have had different
risks even without the exposure. In practice confounding is attributed to particular variables.

\begin{defn}
A variable $C$ is a \textbf{confounder} of the association between exposure $E$ and disease $D$ if
\begin{enumerate}
\item $C$ is a risk factor for $D$ among the unexposed (a cause of $D$, or a proxy for one);
\item $C$ is associated with $E$ in the source population;
\item $C$ is not affected by $E$: it is not on the causal path from $E$ to $D$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{defn}

Age is the classic confounder: it raises the risk of most diseases and is associated with most exposures. The third condition matters.
A variable through which the exposure acts, a \textbf{mediator}, satisfies the first two conditions, but adjusting for it removes part of
the effect being studied.

\subhead{How large can confounding be?}

\begin{thm}[Bias from an unmeasured binary confounder]
\label{thm:confbias}
Let $C$ be a binary confounder with risk ratio $R_C$ for disease (the same among exposed and unexposed), present in a proportion $\pi_1$ of the
exposed and $\pi_0$ of the unexposed, and let the risk ratio for exposure be $\theta$ within each level of $C$. Then the crude risk ratio is
\[
\RR_{\text{crude}} = \theta \times \frac{1 + \pi_1(R_C - 1)}{1 + \pi_0(R_C - 1)} .
\]
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
Let $r$ be the risk among the unexposed with $C$ absent. The unexposed have risk $r$ or $rR_C$, so their average risk is
$r\{(1 - \pi_0) + \pi_0 R_C\} = r\{1 + \pi_0(R_C - 1)\}$. The exposed have risks $\theta r$ and $\theta r R_C$, averaging $\theta r\{1 + \pi_1(R_C - 1)\}$. The ratio of the
averages is the stated expression.
\end{proof}

The second factor is the \textbf{confounding bias factor}. It equals $1$ if $\pi_1 = \pi_0$ (no association of $C$ with exposure) or $R_C = 1$ (no
effect of $C$ on disease), which are conditions 2 and 1 of the definition.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:coffee}
A study finds that coffee drinkers have a higher risk of a cancer. Suppose coffee has no effect ($\theta = 1$), but smoking multiplies the
risk by $10$, and $60\%$ of coffee drinkers smoke against $30\%$ of others. The crude risk ratio is
\[
\frac{1 + 0.6 \times 9}{1 + 0.3 \times 9} = \frac{6.4}{3.7} = 1.73 ,
\]
an association produced entirely by smoking.
\end{exa}

\begin{thm}[Cornfield's inequalities]
\label{thm:cornfield}
If an observed risk ratio $\RR_{\text{obs}} > 1$ is entirely due to a binary confounder $C$ (so $\theta = 1$), then both
\[
R_C \geq \RR_{\text{obs}} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{\pi_1}{\pi_0} \geq \RR_{\text{obs}} .
\]
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
With $\theta = 1$ and $x = R_C - 1 > 0$, $\RR_{\text{obs}} = (1 + \pi_1 x)/(1 + \pi_0 x)$, which exceeds $1$ only if $\pi_1 > \pi_0$. As a function of $x$ it is increasing
(its derivative has the sign of $\pi_1 - \pi_0$) and tends to $\pi_1/\pi_0$ as $x \to \infty$, so $\RR_{\text{obs}} \leq \pi_1/\pi_0$. For the other inequality,
\begin{align*}
\frac{1 + \pi_1 x}{1 + \pi_0 x} \leq 1 + x &\iff 1 + \pi_1 x \leq (1 + x)(1 + \pi_0 x) \\
&\iff \pi_1 \leq 1 + \pi_0(1 + x) ,
\end{align*}
which holds because $\pi_1 \leq 1$.
\end{proof}

Cornfield and colleagues used this argument in 1959 against the claim that the association between smoking and lung cancer, a risk
ratio of about $9$, was due to a genetic factor that predisposed people both to smoke and to develop the cancer. Such a factor would
have to multiply the risk of lung cancer at least ninefold and be at least nine times as common among smokers as among non-smokers.
No such factor was known, and one so strong would hardly have gone unnoticed. The argument is the ancestor of modern
\emph{sensitivity analysis} for unmeasured confounding.

\subhead{Confounding is not non-collapsibility}

When there is no confounding, one might expect the crude measure to equal the common stratum-specific measure. This holds for the
risk difference and the risk ratio, but not for the odds ratio.

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:collapse}
If $C$ is independent of $E$ and the risk ratio (or risk difference) is the same in every stratum of $C$, the crude risk ratio (risk difference)
equals it.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Let $w_k = P(C = k)$, the same among exposed and unexposed by independence, and let the stratum risks be $p_{0k}$ and $p_{1k} = \theta p_{0k}$. The crude
risks are $\sum_k w_k p_{1k} = \theta\sum_k w_k p_{0k}$ and $\sum_k w_k p_{0k}$, with ratio $\theta$. For the difference, $p_{1k} = p_{0k} + \delta$ gives crude risks differing by
$\delta\sum_k w_k = \delta$.
\end{proof}

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:noncollapse}
Two equal strata of $C$ each have half their members exposed, so $C$ is independent of $E$ and there is no
confounding. In stratum 1 the risks are $0.8$ among the exposed and $0.5$ among the unexposed; in stratum 2 they are $0.5$ and $0.2$.
The odds ratios in the two strata are
\[
\frac{0.8/0.2}{0.5/0.5} = 4 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{0.5/0.5}{0.2/0.8} = 4 .
\]
The crude risks are $0.65$ and $0.35$, and the crude odds ratio is
\[
\frac{0.65/0.35}{0.35/0.65} = 3.45 ,
\]
not $4$. The risk difference is $0.3$ in both strata and $0.3$ crude, as Proposition~\ref{prop:collapse} requires. The odds ratio is
\textbf{non-collapsible}: averaging over a risk factor that is unrelated to exposure still moves it towards $1$. The difference between
$3.45$ and $4$ is not confounding, and a change in the odds ratio on adjustment is therefore not by itself evidence of confounding. For a
rare disease the effect is negligible.
\end{exa}

A \textbf{path} between $E$ and $D$ is a sequence of arrows, in any direction, connecting them. A path that begins with an arrow into $E$, such as
$E \leftarrow C \to D$, is a \textbf{back-door path}; it carries association that is not causal. A path is \textbf{blocked} if it passes through a variable
that has been adjusted for, unless that variable is a \textbf{collider} (two arrowheads meeting, as at $S$ in panel 3), in which case the path is
blocked when the collider is \emph{not} adjusted for and opened when it is. The working rule, the \textbf{back-door criterion}, is that
adjusting for a set of variables removes confounding if the set contains no descendants of $E$ and blocks every back-door path from $E$ to $D$.

The collider structure explains selection bias in a unified way. Restricting a study to hospital patients conditions on admission $S$.
If admission is affected by both the exposure and the disease, conditioning on it opens the path $E \to S \leftarrow D$, and exposure and
disease become associated among the patients whether or not they are associated in the population. Berkson's bias is this, and so is
the bias from differential participation of Theorem~\ref{thm:selection}.

\subsection{Controlling confounding}

Confounding can be prevented in the design or removed in the analysis.
\begin{itemize}
\item In the design: \textbf{randomisation}, which makes exposure independent of every pre-existing variable; \textbf{restriction} to one
level of the confounder (only non-smokers), which removes it at the cost of generalisability; and \textbf{matching}
(Section~\ref{sec:matching}), which must be followed by a matched analysis.
\item In the analysis: \textbf{standardisation} (Section~\ref{sec:standard}), \textbf{stratification} with pooled estimates such as the
Mantel--Haenszel estimators, and \textbf{regression} models, which do the same with many variables at once.
\end{itemize}
Each analytic method removes confounding only by the variables that were measured, and only as well as they were measured: adjusting
for a confounder measured with error leaves \textbf{residual confounding}. No analysis removes confounding by variables that were not
recorded, which is why Cornfield's argument and its modern relatives remain necessary.

\subsection{Problems}

\begin{problem}
\label{prob:hospital}
In a case-control study of a cancer and coffee drinking, the controls are patients admitted with other conditions. Among people who
are not cases, coffee drinkers are admitted to hospital with those conditions at $1.5$ times the rate of non-drinkers (some of the
conditions are caused by coffee), while among cases admission does not depend on coffee. If coffee has no effect on the cancer, what odds
ratio will the study find on average?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:selection} with $s_{11} = s_{10}$ and $s_{01} = 1.5\,s_{00}$, the bias factor is $(s_{11}s_{00})/(s_{10} \times 1.5\,s_{00}) = 1/1.5 = 0.67$. The study will tend to
find an odds ratio of $0.67$, suggesting that coffee protects. The controls over-represent coffee drinkers, so the cases look less
exposed than the base they came from.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
\label{prob:berkson}
In a population, $10\%$ have disease A and $20\%$ have condition B, independently. People with A alone are admitted to hospital with
probability $0.5$, with B alone with probability $0.3$, with both with probability $0.65$ (the two reasons acting independently) and with
neither with probability $0.05$. Find the odds ratio between A and B among hospital patients.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
The proportions of the population who are in hospital in each category are: both, $0.1 \times 0.2 \times 0.65 = 0.013$; A only, $0.1 \times 0.8 \times 0.5 = 0.040$; B only,
$0.9 \times 0.2 \times 0.3 = 0.054$; neither, $0.9 \times 0.8 \times 0.05 = 0.036$. The odds ratio among patients is $(0.013 \times 0.036)/(0.040 \times 0.054) = 0.22$. Two independent
conditions appear strongly negatively associated in hospital, because a patient admitted without one of them must usually have the
other. Admission is a collider.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
A study reports a crude risk ratio of $2.0$ for an exposure. An unmeasured binary confounder triples the risk of disease and is present
in $50\%$ of the exposed and $20\%$ of the unexposed. What is the risk ratio adjusted for it?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
The bias factor is $(1 + 0.5 \times 2)/(1 + 0.2 \times 2) = 2/1.4 = 1.43$, so by Theorem~\ref{thm:confbias} the adjusted risk ratio is $2.0/1.43 = 1.40$. The confounder
explains part of the association but not all of it. Cornfield's conditions, $R_C \geq 2$ and $\pi_1/\pi_0 \geq 2$, both hold here ($3$ and $2.5$), yet
the confounder does not explain the association away: the conditions are necessary for a confounder to explain an association
fully, not sufficient.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
Show that if $C$ is independent of $E$ there is no confounding by $C$ on the risk ratio scale even when the stratum risk ratios differ: the crude
risk ratio is then a weighted average of the stratum risk ratios.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
With weights $w_k = P(C = k)$ common to exposed and unexposed, the crude risk ratio is
\[
\frac{\sum_k w_k p_{1k}}{\sum_k w_k p_{0k}} = \sum_k \frac{w_k p_{0k}}{\sum_l w_l p_{0l}} \cdot \frac{p_{1k}}{p_{0k}} ,
\]
a weighted average of the stratum ratios $p_{1k}/p_{0k}$ with weights proportional to the expected cases among the unexposed in each stratum. When
the stratum ratios differ there is \emph{effect modification}, a separate matter taken up with stratified analysis; the crude
ratio is a meaningful summary of them, not a confounded one.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
For each situation say whether the third variable is a confounder, a mediator or a collider, and whether to adjust for it.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item Exposure: obesity; disease: heart attack; third variable: blood pressure.
\item Exposure: smoking; disease: lung cancer; third variable: age.
\item Exposure: a drug; disease: birth defect; third variable: whether the pregnancy ended in a live birth (only live births are studied),
where both the drug and the defect raise the risk of miscarriage.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
(a) Obesity raises blood pressure, which raises the risk of heart attack: a mediator. Adjusting for it estimates only the effect of
obesity not acting through blood pressure, which is a different question; for the total effect, do not adjust.
(b) Age affects both smoking habits and cancer risk and is not affected by smoking: a confounder; adjust.
(c) Live birth is affected by both the drug and the defect: a collider. Restricting to live births conditions on it and can bias the
association, typically towards a protective-looking effect, since exposed pregnancies with defects are especially likely to be lost.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
In Example~\ref{exa:noncollapse}, find the crude and stratum-specific risk ratios, and explain why the crude risk ratio does not equal
either stratum value even though there is no confounding.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
The stratum risk ratios are $0.8/0.5 = 1.6$ and $0.5/0.2 = 2.5$, and the crude is $0.65/0.35 = 1.86$. The risk ratio is not constant across strata
(the odds ratio is), so there is effect modification on the ratio scale. The crude $1.86$ is the weighted average of the problem before,
with weights proportional to $0.5$ and $0.2$, the unexposed risks: $(0.5 \times 1.6 + 0.2 \times 2.5)/0.7 = 1.86$. With no confounding the crude risk ratio is a
fair summary of the stratum ratios; the crude odds ratio $3.45$, by contrast, is smaller than both stratum odds ratios.
\end{solution}

\section{Standardisation of rates}
\label{sec:standard}

Proposition~\ref{prop:crude} showed that a crude rate is a weighted average of stratum-specific rates, weighted by the population's own
distribution across the strata. Two populations with different age structures therefore have crude rates that differ for two reasons:
because their age-specific rates differ, and because their weights differ. In the language of Section~\ref{sec:confounding}, age
confounds the comparison of crude rates. \textbf{Standardisation} removes this by computing, for each population, a summary rate that
uses the \emph{same} weights for both. It is the oldest method of adjusting for confounding and still the standard way of comparing
mortality between countries, regions and periods.

Throughout, population $k$ has person-time $T_{kj}$, deaths (or cases) $D_{kj}$ and rate $I_{kj} = D_{kj}/T_{kj}$ in stratum $j = 1, \dots, J$.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:regions}
Two regions have the following deaths, person-years and death rates per $1000$ person-years in three age groups.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|rrr|rrr}
\hline
 & \multicolumn{3}{c|}{Region A} & \multicolumn{3}{c}{Region B} \\
Age & Deaths & Person-yrs & Rate & Deaths & Person-yrs & Rate \\
\hline
0--39 & 30 & 60{,}000 & 0.5 & 12 & 30{,}000 & 0.4 \\
40--64 & 90 & 30{,}000 & 3.0 & 75 & 30{,}000 & 2.5 \\
65+ & 200 & 10{,}000 & 20.0 & 640 & 40{,}000 & 16.0 \\
\hline
Total & 320 & 100{,}000 & 3.2 & 727 & 100{,}000 & 7.27 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Region B's crude death rate is more than twice region A's, yet in every age group its rate is $20\%$ or so \emph{lower}. Region B is old:
$40\%$ of its person-time is over $65$, against $10\%$ in A. The crude comparison is reversed by confounding by age.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Direct standardisation}

\begin{defn}
Given a \textbf{standard population} with weights $w_j \geq 0$, $\sum_j w_j = 1$ (its proportions of person-time in the strata), the \textbf{directly standardised
rate} of population $k$ is
\[
\text{DSR}_k = \sum_j w_j I_{kj} ,
\]
which is the crude rate the standard population would have if it experienced the stratum-specific rates of
population $k$. The ratio of two directly
standardised rates is the \textbf{comparative mortality figure} (CMF), $\text{DSR}_1/\text{DSR}_0$.
\end{defn}

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:dsr}
\begin{enumerate}
\item If the stratum rate ratio $I_{1j}/I_{0j} = \theta$ is the same in every stratum, then $\text{CMF} = \theta$ whatever the standard.
\item In general the CMF is a weighted average of the stratum rate ratios, with weights $w_j I_{0j}/\sum_l w_l I_{0l}$.
\item If the counts $D_{kj}$ are Poisson, $\Var(\text{DSR}_k) = \sum_j w_j^2 I_{kj}/T_{kj}$, estimated by $\sum_j w_j^2 D_{kj}/T_{kj}^2$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
(1) $\text{DSR}_1 = \sum_j w_j\theta I_{0j} = \theta\,\text{DSR}_0$. (2) $\text{DSR}_1/\text{DSR}_0 = \sum_j w_j I_{0j}(I_{1j}/I_{0j})/\sum_l w_l I_{0l}$. (3) $I_{kj} = D_{kj}/T_{kj}$ with $D_{kj}$ Poisson
with mean $\lambda_{kj}T_{kj}$ has variance $\lambda_{kj}/T_{kj}$, and the strata are independent.
\end{proof}

The choice of standard matters little when the rate ratios are nearly constant across strata, and matters a great deal when they are
not; then the CMF is an average of quite different numbers and the stratum-specific ratios should be reported instead of, or as well
as, the summary. Common standards are the combined population of the groups compared, a national population, or a conventional
``world'' or ``European'' standard population that allows comparison across published studies.

An approximate interval for $\text{DSR}_k$ is $\text{DSR}_k \pm 1.96\,\text{SE}$; when the deaths are few, intervals based on the gamma distribution are
better. For the CMF, the delta method gives $\Var(\log\text{CMF}) \approx \Var(\text{DSR}_1)/\text{DSR}_1^2 + \Var(\text{DSR}_0)/\text{DSR}_0^2$.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:dsr}
Take as standard the two regions combined: $90{,}000$, $60{,}000$ and $50{,}000$ person-years, so $w = (0.45,\ 0.30,\ 0.25)$. Per $1000$ person-years,
\begin{align*}
\text{DSR}_A &= 0.45 \times 0.5 + 0.30 \times 3.0 + 0.25 \times 20 = 6.125 , \\
\text{DSR}_B &= 0.45 \times 0.4 + 0.30 \times 2.5 + 0.25 \times 16 = 4.93 .
\end{align*}
The CMF of B relative to A is $4.93/6.125 = 0.805$: after allowing for age, region B's mortality is about $20\%$ lower, in line with the
stratum ratios $0.80$, $0.83$, $0.80$. The standard errors are
\[
\text{SE}(\text{DSR}_A) = \sqrt{0.45^2\frac{30}{60^2} + 0.30^2\frac{90}{30^2} + 0.25^2\frac{200}{10^2}} = 0.368
\]
per $1000$ (with person-time in thousands), and similarly $\text{SE}(\text{DSR}_B) = 0.188$. The intervals are $(5.40,\ 6.85)$ and $(4.56,\ 5.30)$. Most of the
variance of $\text{DSR}_A$ comes from its oldest stratum, which carries a quarter of the weight but only a tenth of A's person-time.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Indirect standardisation and the SMR}

Direct standardisation needs stratum-specific rates for each population compared. When a population is small, as an occupational
cohort usually is, those rates are unstable or, with few deaths, cannot be computed at all. Indirect standardisation reverses the roles:
it applies \emph{standard rates} to the study population's own structure.

\begin{defn}
Let $\lambda_j^{\text{s}}$ be standard stratum-specific rates. The \textbf{expected} number of deaths in the study population is $E = \sum_j T_j \lambda^{\text{s}}_j$, and with
$O = \sum_j D_j$ observed deaths the \textbf{standardised mortality ratio} is
\[
\text{SMR} = \frac{O}{E} .
\]
\end{defn}

The SMR compares the deaths that occurred with those that would have occurred had the study population experienced the standard rates.
Only the total number of deaths in the study population is needed, not their distribution across strata.

\begin{thm}
\label{thm:smr}
Suppose $D_j \sim \text{Poisson}(\theta\lambda^{\text{s}}_j T_j)$ independently, so that the study rates are a constant multiple $\theta$ of the standard rates, which are
treated as known. Then $O \sim \text{Poisson}(\theta E)$, the maximum likelihood estimate of $\theta$ is the SMR, and exact confidence limits are
\[
\frac{\chi^2_{2O,\,0.025}}{2E} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{\chi^2_{2O + 2,\,0.975}}{2E} .
\]
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
A sum of independent Poisson variables is Poisson with the sum of the means, $\theta\sum_j\lambda^{\text{s}}_j T_j = \theta E$. The likelihood is
$\prod_j(\theta E_j)^{D_j}e^{-\theta E_j}/D_j! \propto \theta^O e^{-\theta E}$, with $E_j = \lambda^{\text{s}}_j T_j$, maximised at $\theta = O/E$. The limits are those of \eqref{eq:exactrate} with
person-time replaced by $E$, by Lemma~\ref{lem:poisgamma}.
\end{proof}

Treating the standard rates as known is reasonable when they come from a national population, whose rates are estimated from vastly
more deaths than the study's. The test of $\theta = 1$ uses the Poisson distribution with mean $E$ directly; for large $E$, $(O - E)/\sqrt{E}$ is
approximately standard normal.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:smr}
Take as standard the national rates $0.45$, $2.8$ and $18$ per $1000$ person-years in the three age groups. For region A,
\[
E_A = 60 \times 0.45 + 30 \times 2.8 + 10 \times 18 = 27 + 84 + 180 = 291 ,
\]
against $O_A = 320$, so $\text{SMR}_A = 1.10$, with exact interval $(0.98,\ 1.23)$ and two-sided exact $P = 0.098$. For region B,
$E_B = 13.5 + 84 + 720 = 817.5$ and $O_B = 727$, so $\text{SMR}_B = 0.89$, with interval $(0.83,\ 0.96)$. Region A's mortality is about $10\%$ above
the national level, region B's about $11\%$ below. The ratio of SMRs, $0.89/1.10 = 0.81$, happens to agree with the CMF here, because the
regions' rates are nearly proportional to the national ones; in general it need not (Section~\ref{sec:smrcompare}).
\end{exa}

\subsection{Comparing SMRs}
\label{sec:smrcompare}

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:smrweights}
The SMR is a weighted average of the stratum-specific ratios $I_j/\lambda^{\text{s}}_j$, with weights $E_j/E$ proportional to the study population's own expected
deaths in each stratum.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
$O/E = \sum_j D_j/E = \sum_j (E_j/E)(D_j/E_j)$, and $D_j/E_j = I_j T_j/(\lambda^{\text{s}}_j T_j) = I_j/\lambda^{\text{s}}_j$.
\end{proof}

So each SMR uses its own population's weights. Two SMRs computed against the same standard are \emph{not} standardised to a common
structure, and their ratio can mislead when the stratum ratios vary. This is the price of indirect standardisation: an SMR compares one
population with the standard, not two populations with each other.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:smrparadox}
With standard rates $1$ and $10$ per $1000$ in two age groups, population A has rates $2$ and $10$ and person-time $9000$ and $1000$ years;
population B has rates $2.2$ and $11$ ($10\%$ higher than A's in both groups) and person-time $1000$ and $9000$. Then
\begin{align*}
\text{A}: \quad O &= 18 + 10 = 28, & E &= 9 + 10 = 19, & \text{SMR} &= 1.47 ; \\
\text{B}: \quad O &= 2.2 + 99 = 101.2, & E &= 1 + 90 = 91, & \text{SMR} &= 1.11 .
\end{align*}
Population B has the higher rate in every age group, but the lower SMR. A's SMR is dominated by the young stratum, where its rate is
twice the standard; B's by the old stratum, where its rate is only $10\%$ above it. Direct standardisation with any standard gives a CMF of
$1.1$ for B against A.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Choosing between the methods}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{p{3.4cm}p{5.5cm}p{5.5cm}}
\hline
 & Direct & Indirect \\
\hline
Needs & stratum rates of each study population; a standard population & total deaths in each study population; standard rates \\
Weights & the standard's structure & each study population's own structure \\
Comparable between study populations & yes & only if rate ratios are constant across strata \\
Stability with small numbers & poor & good \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Both summaries are adequate when the stratum ratios are roughly constant, and both hide something important when they are not. In
that case the proper report is the stratum-specific comparison, with a summary only as a supplement. The Mantel--Haenszel methods of
the next sections give pooled estimates with better statistical properties under the assumption of a common ratio, and a later section
sets them side by side with standardisation on one set of data.

\subsection{Problems}

\begin{problem}
Recompute the directly standardised rates of Example~\ref{exa:regions} using (a) region B's person-time as the standard, (b) the young
standard $w = (0.6,\ 0.3,\ 0.1)$. Compare the CMFs with the value $0.805$ from the combined standard.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
(a) $w = (0.3,\ 0.3,\ 0.4)$: $\text{DSR}_A = 0.15 + 0.9 + 8.0 = 9.05$ and $\text{DSR}_B = 0.12 + 0.75 + 6.4 = 7.27$, region B's crude rate, as it must be when B's own
structure is the standard. $\text{CMF} = 0.803$. (b) $\text{DSR}_A = 0.3 + 0.9 + 2.0 = 3.2$, $\text{DSR}_B = 0.24 + 0.75 + 1.6 = 2.59$, $\text{CMF} = 0.809$. The standardised
rates themselves change greatly with the standard (from $3.2$ to $9.05$ for A), so they have no meaning except relative to one another;
the CMF hardly changes, because the stratum ratios are nearly constant.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
In Example~\ref{exa:dsr}, find a $95\%$ confidence interval for the CMF.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$\Var(\log\text{CMF}) \approx (0.368/6.125)^2 + (0.188/4.93)^2 = 0.00362 + 0.00145 = 0.00507$, so $\text{SE} = 0.0712$ and the interval is
$0.805\,e^{\pm 0.140} = (0.700,\ 0.925)$. Region B's age-adjusted mortality is between $7\%$ and $30\%$ below A's.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
A cohort of chemical workers experiences $18$ deaths from a cancer, against $9.6$ expected from national rates. Compute the SMR, its exact
$95\%$ confidence interval and the exact $P$-value for $\theta = 1$. Why might the SMR understate the effect of the work?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$\text{SMR} = 18/9.6 = 1.88$. The limits are $\chi^2_{36,\,0.025}/19.2 = 1.11$ and $\chi^2_{38,\,0.975}/19.2 = 2.96$. Under $\theta = 1$, $O \sim \text{Poisson}(9.6)$, and $P(O \geq 18) = 0.0098$,
so the two-sided $P$ is about $0.020$. The comparison is with the general population, which includes people too ill to work; the
healthy worker effect makes the expected count too high for an employed group and biases the SMR downwards. The effect is weaker for
cancers than for all-cause mortality, but present.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
Show that if every stratum of a population has rate $\theta\lambda^{\text{s}}_j$, the SMR is unbiased for $\theta$ and $\Var(\text{SMR}) = \theta/E$. Deduce that the precision of an
SMR is governed by the expected number of deaths.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$O \sim \text{Poisson}(\theta E)$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:smr}, so $E(\text{SMR}) = \theta E/E = \theta$ and $\Var(\text{SMR}) = \theta E/E^2 = \theta/E$. The coefficient of variation is $1/\sqrt{\theta E}$,
which depends on the expected deaths only. Doubling the size of a cohort, or its follow-up, doubles $E$ and reduces the coefficient of
variation by a factor of $\sqrt 2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
Verify the figures of Example~\ref{exa:smrparadox} and compute the CMF of B relative to A with the combined population as standard.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
The combined person-time is $10{,}000$ in each stratum, so $w = (0.5,\ 0.5)$. Then $\text{DSR}_A = 0.5 \times 2 + 0.5 \times 10 = 6$ and $\text{DSR}_B = 0.5 \times 2.2 + 0.5 \times 11 = 6.6$,
giving $\text{CMF} = 1.1$. Indeed, since $I_{Bj} = 1.1\,I_{Aj}$ in both strata, Proposition~\ref{prop:dsr} gives $\text{CMF} = 1.1$ for any standard, while the ratio of
SMRs is $1.11/1.47 = 0.75$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
Show that when the study population itself is used as the standard for indirect standardisation (that is, $\lambda^{\text{s}}_j = I_j$), the SMR is $1$,
and when it is used as the standard for direct standardisation, the DSR is its crude rate. What does this say about choosing a standard?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
With $\lambda^{\text{s}}_j = I_j$, $E = \sum_j T_j I_j = \sum_j D_j = O$. With $w_j = T_j/\sum_l T_l$, $\text{DSR} = \sum_j (T_j/\sum_l T_l)(D_j/T_j)$ is the crude rate. A standard
is a reference, and comparing a population with itself gives the trivial answer. The standard should be external to the comparison or,
for direct standardisation, a population whose structure is in the range of those compared.
\end{solution}

\section{Confounding and stratified analysis}
\label{sec:stratified}

Standardisation adjusts rates by reweighting. The more general approach, for any measure and any design, is \textbf{stratification}: divide
the data into strata within which the confounder is constant (or nearly so), estimate the association within each stratum, and then
decide whether the stratum estimates can be summarised by one number. This section develops the second step and the decision; the
Mantel--Haenszel procedure of Section~\ref{sec:mh} gives the summary estimator in most common use.

\subsection{Stratification}

Within a stratum in which the confounder $C$ takes a single value, $C$ cannot be associated with exposure, so it cannot confound
(condition 2 of the definition in Section~\ref{sec:confounding}). The stratum-specific estimates are free of confounding by $C$, though
not by other variables.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:strata}
A case-control study of an exposure and a disease records age in three groups. In the notation of the fourfold
table, $a$ and $b$ are the exposed and unexposed cases and $c$ and $d$ the exposed and unexposed controls.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrcc}
\hline
Age & $a$ & $b$ & $c$ & $d$ & $\widehat{\OR}_k$ & $v_k$ \\
\hline
Under 45 & 9 & 41 & 40 & 320 & 1.76 & 0.164 \\
45--59 & 30 & 45 & 60 & 180 & 2.00 & 0.078 \\
60+ & 64 & 26 & 50 & 50 & 2.46 & 0.094 \\
\hline
All ages & 103 & 112 & 150 & 550 & 3.37 & \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Here $v_k = 1/a + 1/b + 1/c + 1/d$ is the estimated variance of $\log\widehat{\OR}_k$.
The crude odds ratio is $3.37$, the stratum odds ratios between $1.76$ and $2.46$. Older people are both more often exposed (the proportion
of controls exposed rises from $11\%$ to $50\%$) and more often cases, so age inflates the crude association. The stratum estimates
differ from one another by no more than chance would suggest (Example~\ref{exa:woolftest}), and a single adjusted odds ratio of about $2$
summarises them.
\end{exa}

The crude estimate can differ from the stratum-specific ones in either direction, and can even reverse direction. That extreme case is
\textbf{Simpson's paradox} (Problem~\ref{prob:simpson}). There is nothing paradoxical about it once confounding is understood: the crude
comparison mixes groups with different distributions of the confounder.

\subsection{Effect modification}
\label{sec:effectmod}

\begin{defn}
A variable $C$ is an \textbf{effect modifier} on a given scale if the measure of association between $E$ and $D$ on that scale differs between the
levels of $C$. The measure is then said to be \textbf{heterogeneous}; if it is the same in every stratum it is \textbf{homogeneous}.
\end{defn}

Confounding and effect modification are different things. Confounding is a bias, to be removed. Effect modification is a feature of the
world, to be described: if a drug works in men but not in women, the most useful report says so, and a single average hides it. A variable
can be a confounder, an effect modifier, both, or neither.

Effect modification depends on the scale of measurement.

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:scale}
Suppose the exposure has the same risk ratio $\theta \neq 1$ in two strata whose baseline risks $p_{01} \neq p_{02}$ differ. Then the risk differences in the
two strata differ. Conversely, a common risk difference with different baseline risks implies different risk ratios.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
The risk differences are $(\theta - 1)p_{01}$ and $(\theta - 1)p_{02}$, unequal since $\theta \neq 1$ and $p_{01} \neq p_{02}$. For the converse, a common difference $\delta \neq 0$ gives risk
ratios $1 + \delta/p_{01}$ and $1 + \delta/p_{02}$, which differ.
\end{proof}

So ``is there effect modification?'' has no answer until the scale is named. An exposure that triples the risk in a stratum with baseline
risk $0.01$ and in one with baseline $0.1$ has a homogeneous risk ratio of $3$ but risk differences of $0.02$ and $0.2$. For public health the
second scale often matters more: removing the exposure prevents ten times as many cases per person in the high-risk stratum.

When the effect modifier is itself a second exposure, a standard summary of departure from additivity of risks is the \textbf{relative
excess risk due to interaction},
\[
\text{RERI} = \RR_{11} - \RR_{10} - \RR_{01} + 1 ,
\]
where $\RR_{ij}$ is the risk ratio for exposure levels $(i, j)$ relative to $(0, 0)$. It is $0$ when the excess risks of the two exposures add, and
positive when the joint excess exceeds the sum. With $\RR_{10} = 2$, $\RR_{01} = 2.5$ and $\RR_{11} = 6$, the risk ratios combine almost multiplicatively ($2 \times 2.5 = 5$)
and $\text{RERI} = 2.5$: more than additive. Additive interaction is the scale that bears on how many cases joint exposure causes, and so on
which groups an intervention should target.

\subsection{Pooling homogeneous estimates}
\label{sec:pooling}

If the measure is homogeneous, say a common log odds ratio $\beta$, each stratum gives an estimate $\hat\beta_k = \log\widehat{\OR}_k$ with approximate
variance $v_k$, and the strata are independent. Among all weighted averages, the best uses inverse-variance weights.

\begin{thm}
\label{thm:ivw}
Let $\hat\beta_1, \dots, \hat\beta_K$ be independent and unbiased for $\beta$, with variances $v_k$. Among estimators $\sum_k c_k\hat\beta_k$ with $\sum_k c_k = 1$, the variance is
minimised by $c_k \propto 1/v_k$. With $w_k = 1/v_k$ the minimum-variance estimator and its variance are
\[
\hat\beta_W = \frac{\sum_k w_k\hat\beta_k}{\sum_k w_k}, \qquad \Var(\hat\beta_W) = \frac{1}{\sum_k w_k} .
\]
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
Any such estimator is unbiased, with variance $\sum_k c_k^2 v_k$. By the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality,
\[
1 = \Bigl(\sum_k c_k\Bigr)^2 = \Bigl(\sum_k c_k\sqrt{v_k} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{v_k}}\Bigr)^2 \leq \sum_k c_k^2 v_k \sum_k \frac{1}{v_k} ,
\]
so $\sum_k c_k^2 v_k \geq 1/\sum_k w_k$, with equality when $c_k\sqrt{v_k}$ is proportional to $1/\sqrt{v_k}$, that is $c_k \propto 1/v_k$. Then $c_k = w_k/\sum_l w_l$ and the variance is
$\sum_k w_k^2 v_k/(\sum_l w_l)^2 = 1/\sum_l w_l$.
\end{proof}

Applied to log odds ratios with Woolf's variances, this is the \textbf{Woolf pooled estimator}. Its weakness is that the weights and the
stratum estimates come from the same small tables: a stratum with a zero cell has an infinite log odds ratio and must be corrected or
dropped, and with many sparse strata (as in matched studies, where each set is a stratum) the estimator is badly biased. The
Mantel--Haenszel estimator of Section~\ref{sec:mh} avoids both problems.

\subsection{Testing homogeneity}

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:woolfq}
Under homogeneity, with the $\hat\beta_k$ approximately normal and the $v_k$ treated as known,
\[
Q = \sum_k w_k(\hat\beta_k - \hat\beta_W)^2
\]
has approximately the $\chi^2_{K - 1}$ distribution.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Let $z_k = \sqrt{w_k}(\hat\beta_k - \beta)$, independent standard normal, and $u$ the unit vector with components $\sqrt{w_k}/\sqrt{\sum_l w_l}$. Then
$\sqrt{w_k}(\hat\beta_k - \hat\beta_W) = z_k - u_k\,(u^\top z)$, since $u^\top z = \sum_l w_l(\hat\beta_l - \beta)/\sqrt{\sum_l w_l} = \sqrt{\sum_l w_l}\,(\hat\beta_W - \beta)$. So $Q = \|(I - uu^\top)z\|^2$,
the squared length of the projection of a standard normal vector onto the $(K - 1)$-dimensional subspace orthogonal to $u$, which is $\chi^2_{K - 1}$.
\end{proof}

The test has low power when the strata are small, so a non-significant result is weak evidence of homogeneity. Its value is in
flagging large, clear differences, which should then be reported as effect modification.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:woolftest}
In Example~\ref{exa:strata} the weights $1/v_k$ are $6.11$, $12.86$ and $10.63$, and the log odds ratios $0.563$, $0.693$ and $0.901$. Then
\[
\hat\beta_W = \frac{6.11 \times 0.563 + 12.86 \times 0.693 + 10.63 \times 0.901}{29.60} = 0.741 ,
\]
so the pooled odds ratio is $e^{0.741} = 2.10$, with $\text{SE} = 1/\sqrt{29.60} = 0.184$ and $95\%$ interval $(1.46,\ 3.01)$. The homogeneity statistic is
\begin{align*}
Q &= 6.11(0.563 - 0.741)^2 + 12.86(0.693 - 0.741)^2 \\
&\quad + 10.63(0.901 - 0.741)^2 = 0.49
\end{align*}
on $2$ degrees of freedom, $P = 0.78$. Nothing suggests that the odds ratio varies with age, and the age-adjusted estimate $2.10$ replaces the
crude $3.37$. The test of no association, $z = 0.741/0.184 = 4.03$, is highly significant.
\end{exa}

\subsection{A strategy for stratified analysis}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Compute the crude measure and the stratum-specific measures, with confidence intervals.
\item Judge whether the stratum measures differ materially, by inspection and by a test of homogeneity, bearing in mind its low power.
\item If they do, report them separately: there is effect modification on this scale. Consider whether another scale is homogeneous.
\item If they do not, compute a pooled adjusted estimate (Mantel--Haenszel or inverse-variance) and its interval.
\item Compare the adjusted with the crude estimate. A material difference (a change of $10\%$ or more is a common working rule) indicates
confounding by the stratifying variable, and the adjusted estimate is reported. For the odds ratio, recall that some change can come
from non-collapsibility alone (Example~\ref{exa:noncollapse}).
\end{enumerate}

Stratification runs out of data quickly. With several confounders, each with several levels, the strata multiply and most contain few
people; the summary estimators of the next section cope with many small strata, and regression models (Section~\ref{sec:regression})
cope with many confounders at once.

\subsection{Problems}

\begin{problem}
\label{prob:simpson}
In a published comparison of treatments for kidney stones (Charig et al., 1986), results were reported by stone size. Treatment A succeeded in $81$ of $87$ small stones and $192$ of $263$ large stones;
treatment B in $234$ of $270$ small and $55$ of $80$ large. Find the success rates by stone size and overall, and explain.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Small stones: A $0.931$, B $0.867$. Large stones: A $0.730$, B $0.688$. Overall: A $273/350 = 0.780$, B $289/350 = 0.826$. A is better for both sizes but worse
overall. Treatment A was used mostly for large stones ($263$ of its $350$), which are harder to treat, and B mostly for small ones. Stone
size is a confounder: it affects success and is associated with the treatment given. The stratified comparison is the valid one.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
A case-control study stratified by sex gives, for men, $20$ exposed and $80$ unexposed cases and $40$ exposed and $200$ unexposed controls;
for women, $40$, $20$, $30$ and $150$. Compute the stratum odds ratios with intervals, the Woolf test of homogeneity and the crude odds ratio.
How should the results be reported?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Men: $\widehat{\OR} = (20 \times 200)/(80 \times 40) = 1.25$, $v = 0.0925$, interval $(0.69,\ 2.27)$. Women: $\widehat{\OR} = (40 \times 150)/(20 \times 30) = 10.0$, $v = 0.115$, interval
$(5.1,\ 19.4)$. The pooled log odds ratio has weights $10.81$ and $8.70$; $Q = 20.8$ on $1$ degree of freedom, $P < 0.0001$. The crude odds ratio is
$(60 \times 350)/(100 \times 70) = 3.0$. The odds ratio is strongly heterogeneous: the exposure is associated with the disease in women but hardly in
men. Neither the crude $3.0$ nor a pooled estimate (about $3.2$) describes either sex, and the results should be reported separately.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
Show that if the odds ratio is homogeneous across two strata with different baseline risks and $\OR \neq 1$, the risk ratio cannot also be
homogeneous.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Suppose $\OR = \psi$ and $\RR = \theta$ in both strata. With baseline risk $p_0$ and $p_1 = \theta p_0$, $\psi = \theta(1 - p_0)/(1 - \theta p_0)$. Solving for $p_0$:
$\psi - \psi\theta p_0 = \theta - \theta p_0$, so $p_0(\theta - \psi\theta) = \theta - \psi$ and $p_0 = (\psi - \theta)/\{\theta(\psi - 1)\}$, a single value. Two strata with
different baseline risks cannot share both $\psi$ and $\theta$ unless $\psi = \theta = 1$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
Using the weights of Example~\ref{exa:woolftest}, find the pooled estimate if the youngest stratum is omitted, and comment on its
precision.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$\hat\beta_W = (12.86 \times 0.693 + 10.63 \times 0.901)/23.49 = 0.787$, an odds ratio of $2.20$, with $\text{SE} = 1/\sqrt{23.49} = 0.206$. The estimate hardly moves, but
the standard error rises from $0.184$ to $0.206$, as Theorem~\ref{thm:ivw} implies: every stratum adds to $\sum w_k$, however small its weight.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
In a cohort, the risk of a disease is $0.02$ in people with neither of two exposures, $0.04$ with exposure A only, $0.05$ with B only and $0.12$
with both. Compute the risk ratios, the RERI, and say whether there is interaction on the multiplicative scale and on the additive scale.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$\RR_{10} = 2$, $\RR_{01} = 2.5$, $\RR_{11} = 6$. On the multiplicative scale the joint ratio expected without interaction is $5$, against $6$ observed, a
modest departure. On the additive scale $\text{RERI} = 6 - 2 - 2.5 + 1 = 2.5$: the joint excess risk ($0.10$) is much larger than the sum of the separate
excesses ($0.02 + 0.03 = 0.05$). There is strong additive interaction; preventing either exposure in people who have both prevents more cases
than the separate effects suggest.
\end{solution}

\section{The Mantel--Haenszel procedure}
\label{sec:mh}

Mantel and Haenszel (1959) proposed, for case-control studies of cancer stratified by age and other factors, a summary odds ratio and a
test of association that need no iteration, tolerate empty cells and remain valid when the strata are many and small. Their methods,
extended since to risk ratios, risk differences and rate ratios, are the standard tools of stratified analysis. Throughout, stratum $k$ has
the table of Section~\ref{sec:association} with cells $a_k, b_k, c_k, d_k$, margins $n_{1k}, n_{0k}, m_{1k}, m_{0k}$ and total $n_k$.

\subsection{The Mantel--Haenszel odds ratio}

\begin{defn}
The \textbf{Mantel--Haenszel estimator} of a common odds ratio is
\[
\widehat{\OR}_{\text{MH}} = \frac{\sum_k a_k d_k/n_k}{\sum_k b_k c_k/n_k} .
\]
\end{defn}

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:mhweights}
$\widehat{\OR}_{\text{MH}}$ is a weighted average of the stratum odds ratios $\widehat{\OR}_k = a_k d_k/(b_k c_k)$, with weights proportional to $b_k c_k/n_k$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
$\sum_k a_k d_k/n_k = \sum_k (b_k c_k/n_k)\,\widehat{\OR}_k$; divide by $\sum_k b_k c_k/n_k$.
\end{proof}

The weights $b_k c_k/n_k$ are approximately inverse variances of $\widehat{\OR}_k$ when the odds ratio is near $1$, so the estimator is nearly efficient
there, and it loses little elsewhere. Unlike the Woolf estimator, it needs no correction for zero cells: a stratum with $b_k c_k = 0$ adds to
the numerator only, and one with $a_k d_k = 0$ to the denominator only.

\begin{thm}
\label{thm:mhconsistent}
Under a common odds ratio $\psi$, $\widehat{\OR}_{\text{MH}}$ is consistent for $\psi$ both as the strata become large with their number fixed, and as the
number of strata grows with their sizes bounded.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
Write $R_k = a_k d_k/n_k$ and $S_k = b_k c_k/n_k$. The key fact is that $E(R_k - \psi S_k) = 0$ in every stratum, whatever its size: under the
product-binomial model of a case-control stratum, with exposure probabilities $q_{1k}$ among $m_{1k}$ cases and $q_{0k}$ among $m_{0k}$ controls, $a_k$ and $d_k$ are
independent, so $E(a_k d_k) = m_{1k}q_{1k}\,m_{0k}(1 - q_{0k})$ and $E(b_k c_k) = m_{1k}(1 - q_{1k})\,m_{0k}q_{0k}$. Their ratio is the stratum odds ratio $\psi$. (In this
model $n_k = m_{1k} + m_{0k}$ is fixed, so dividing by it changes nothing.)

With $K$ fixed and the strata growing, $R_k/n_k$ and $S_k/n_k$ converge in probability to their means, and $\sum_k R_k/\sum_k S_k$ converges to
$\sum_k E(R_k)/\sum_k E(S_k) = \psi$. With the sizes bounded and $K \to \infty$, $\sum_k R_k/K$ and $\sum_k S_k/K$ are averages of independent bounded variables, so by
the law of large numbers their difference $\frac1K\sum_k (R_k - \psi S_k)$ tends to $0$, while $\frac1K\sum_k S_k$ stays bounded away from $0$ under mild conditions.
Hence the ratio tends to $\psi$.
\end{proof}

The second kind of consistency is what the Woolf estimator, and the unconditional maximum likelihood estimator, lack: with matched pairs
(each pair a stratum of size $2$) both converge to $\psi^2$ rather than $\psi$.

\subsection{Variance and confidence interval}

The variance estimator in general use is due to Robins, Breslow and Greenland (1986). With
\[
P_k = \frac{a_k + d_k}{n_k}, \quad Q_k = \frac{b_k + c_k}{n_k}, \quad R_k = \frac{a_k d_k}{n_k}, \quad S_k = \frac{b_k c_k}{n_k} ,
\]
and $R_+ = \sum_k R_k$, $S_+ = \sum_k S_k$,
\[
\widehat{\Var}(\log\widehat{\OR}_{\text{MH}}) = \frac{\sum_k P_k R_k}{2R_+^2} + \frac{\sum_k (P_k S_k + Q_k R_k)}{2R_+ S_+} + \frac{\sum_k Q_k S_k}{2S_+^2} .
\]
It is consistent under both kinds of asymptotics of Theorem~\ref{thm:mhconsistent}, and the $95\%$ interval is
\[
\widehat{\OR}_{\text{MH}}\exp(\pm 1.96\,\text{SE}) .
\]

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:mhor}
For the age-stratified data of Example~\ref{exa:strata}:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\hline
Age & $n_k$ & $R_k$ & $S_k$ & $P_k$ & $Q_k$ \\
\hline
Under 45 & 410 & 7.024 & 4.000 & 0.802 & 0.198 \\
45--59 & 315 & 17.143 & 8.571 & 0.667 & 0.333 \\
60+ & 190 & 16.842 & 6.842 & 0.600 & 0.400 \\
\hline
Total & & 41.009 & 19.414 & & \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
So $\widehat{\OR}_{\text{MH}} = 41.009/19.414 = 2.11$, close to the Woolf estimate $2.10$ and far from the crude $3.37$. The weights of
Proposition~\ref{prop:mhweights} are $0.21$, $0.44$ and $0.35$. The Robins--Breslow--Greenland variance is $0.0334$, so $\text{SE} = 0.183$ and the interval is
$2.11\,e^{\pm 0.358} = (1.48,\ 3.02)$.
\end{exa}

\subsection{The Mantel--Haenszel test}

\begin{thm}
\label{thm:cmh}
Under the hypothesis of no association in any stratum, conditionally on all the margins,
\[
E\Bigl(\sum_k a_k\Bigr) = \sum_k \frac{n_{1k}m_{1k}}{n_k}, \qquad \Var\Bigl(\sum_k a_k\Bigr) = \sum_k \frac{n_{1k}n_{0k}m_{1k}m_{0k}}{n_k^2(n_k - 1)} ,
\]
and the \textbf{Mantel--Haenszel statistic}
\[
X^2_{\text{MH}} = \frac{\bigl\{\sum_k a_k - \sum_k E(a_k)\bigr\}^2}{\sum_k \Var(a_k)}
\]
is approximately $\chi^2_1$ when $\sum_k \Var(a_k)$ is large.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:hyper} each $a_k$, given its margins, is hypergeometric with the stated mean and variance, and the strata are independent.
Sums of independent variables have summed means and variances. A sum of many independent bounded variables with large total variance is
approximately normal by the Lindeberg central limit theorem, which applies whether the strata are few and large or many and small; the
square of the standardised sum is then approximately $\chi^2_1$.
\end{proof}

The statistic compares the total number of exposed cases with its expectation under no association, summed across strata before
squaring. Associations in the same direction in different strata reinforce each other, and the test is most powerful against a common
odds ratio. A continuity correction, subtracting $\tfrac12$ from $|\sum a_k - \sum E(a_k)|$ before squaring, is often used.

\begin{exa}
In Example~\ref{exa:strata}, $\sum_k a_k = 103$. The expected values are $5.98$, $21.43$ and $54.00$, totalling $81.40$, and the variances $4.63$, $11.70$ and $11.43$,
totalling $27.76$. Then $X^2_{\text{MH}} = (103 - 81.40)^2/27.76 = 16.8$ ($16.0$ with the continuity correction), $P < 0.0001$. The age-adjusted association is
highly significant.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Matched pairs}

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:mhpairs}
If each matched pair is treated as a stratum, $\widehat{\OR}_{\text{MH}} = n_{10}/n_{01}$ and $X^2_{\text{MH}}$ without continuity correction is McNemar's statistic.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Each stratum has $n_k = 2$, with one case and one control. A concordant pair has $a_k d_k = b_k c_k = 0$. A pair in which only the case is exposed has
$a_k = d_k = 1$, $b_k = c_k = 0$, contributing $\tfrac12$ to $R_+$; one in which only the control is exposed contributes $\tfrac12$ to $S_+$. So $\widehat{\OR}_{\text{MH}} = (n_{10}/2)/(n_{01}/2)$.

For the test, a concordant pair has $n_{1k} = 0$ or $n_{0k} = 0$, so $\Var(a_k) = 0$ and $a_k = E(a_k)$; it contributes nothing. A discordant pair has all margins $1$ and $n_k = 2$,
so $E(a_k) = \tfrac12$ and $\Var(a_k) = 1/(4 \times 1) = \tfrac14$. Summing over discordant pairs, $\sum a_k - \sum E(a_k) = n_{10} - (n_{10} + n_{01})/2 = (n_{10} - n_{01})/2$ and
$\sum \Var(a_k) = (n_{10} + n_{01})/4$, giving $(n_{10} - n_{01})^2/(n_{10} + n_{01})$.
\end{proof}

So the conditional analysis of Theorem~\ref{thm:pairs} is the Mantel--Haenszel analysis with pairs as strata, and the same holds for matched
sets with several controls (Problem~\ref{prob:onetwo}). This is the sparse-data case of Theorem~\ref{thm:mhconsistent} in its purest form.

\subsection{Risk ratios and risk differences}

For cohort data with risks, the same idea gives
\[
\widehat{\RR}_{\text{MH}} = \frac{\sum_k a_k n_{0k}/n_k}{\sum_k b_k n_{1k}/n_k}, \qquad \widehat{\RD}_{\text{MH}} = \frac{\sum_k (a_k n_{0k} - b_k n_{1k})/n_k}{\sum_k n_{1k}n_{0k}/n_k} .
\]
The first is a weighted average of the stratum risk ratios with weights $b_k n_{1k}/n_k$, the second of the stratum risk differences with weights
$n_{1k}n_{0k}/n_k$. Greenland and Robins (1985) gave the variance estimator, consistent in large and sparse strata alike,
\[
\widehat{\Var}(\log\widehat{\RR}_{\text{MH}}) = \frac{\sum_k (m_{1k}n_{1k}n_{0k}/n_k^2 - a_k b_k/n_k)}{\bigl(\sum_k a_k n_{0k}/n_k\bigr)\bigl(\sum_k b_k n_{1k}/n_k\bigr)} .
\]
The test of no association is the same $X^2_{\text{MH}}$ as before, since conditionally on the margins it does not matter whether the table arose from
a cohort or a case-control design.

\subsection{Rate ratios}
\label{sec:mhrate}

With $D_{1k}$ cases in $T_{1k}$ person-years among the exposed and $D_{0k}$ in $T_{0k}$ among the unexposed in stratum $k$, $D_k = D_{1k} + D_{0k}$ and $T_k = T_{1k} + T_{0k}$,
\[
\widehat{\text{IRR}}_{\text{MH}} = \frac{\sum_k D_{1k}T_{0k}/T_k}{\sum_k D_{0k}T_{1k}/T_k} ,
\]
with
\[
\widehat{\Var}(\log\widehat{\text{IRR}}_{\text{MH}}) = \frac{\sum_k D_k T_{1k}T_{0k}/T_k^2}{\bigl(\sum_k D_{1k}T_{0k}/T_k\bigr)\bigl(\sum_k D_{0k}T_{1k}/T_k\bigr)} .
\]
The test of no association uses Theorem~\ref{thm:ratecond}: given $D_k$, $D_{1k}$ is binomial with probability $T_{1k}/T_k$ under the null hypothesis, so
\[
X^2 = \frac{\bigl(\sum_k D_{1k} - \sum_k D_k T_{1k}/T_k\bigr)^2}{\sum_k D_k T_{1k}T_{0k}/T_k^2} .
\]

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:doctors}
In the British doctors' study (Doll and Hill, 1966; data as in Breslow and Day, 1987) deaths from coronary heart disease among smokers
and non-smokers were:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrc}
\hline
 & \multicolumn{2}{c}{Smokers} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{Non-smokers} & \\
Age & Deaths & Person-yrs & Deaths & Person-yrs & IRR \\
\hline
35--44 & 32 & 52{,}407 & 2 & 18{,}790 & 5.74 \\
45--54 & 104 & 43{,}248 & 12 & 10{,}673 & 2.14 \\
55--64 & 206 & 28{,}612 & 28 & 5710 & 1.47 \\
65--74 & 186 & 12{,}663 & 28 & 2585 & 1.36 \\
75--84 & 102 & 5317 & 31 & 1462 & 0.90 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The crude rate ratio is $1.72$ and the Mantel--Haenszel rate ratio $1.42$, with $\text{SE}(\log) = 0.107$ and interval $(1.15,\ 1.76)$. The test gives
$630$ observed deaths among smokers against $595.2$ expected, variance $110.1$, $X^2 = 11.0$. The crude and adjusted ratios differ, but the more striking feature is that the stratum
ratios fall steeply with age, from $5.7$ to $0.9$. This is effect modification
on the ratio scale, and here the summary $1.42$ is a poor description: the relative effect of smoking on heart disease is large in middle age
and vanishes in old age, while the rate \emph{difference} (Problem~\ref{prob:doctors}) behaves quite differently.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Testing homogeneity: the Breslow--Day test}

Woolf's test of Section~\ref{sec:stratified} breaks down in sparse strata. Breslow and Day (1980) proposed a test that uses the
Mantel--Haenszel estimate: in each stratum find the cell count $\tilde a_k$ which, with the observed margins, has odds ratio $\widehat{\OR}_{\text{MH}}$ (the root
in the admissible range of a quadratic equation), and its variance $\tilde v_k = (1/\tilde a_k + 1/\tilde b_k + 1/\tilde c_k + 1/\tilde d_k)^{-1}$. Then
\[
X^2_{\text{BD}} = \sum_k \frac{(a_k - \tilde a_k)^2}{\tilde v_k}
\]
is approximately $\chi^2_{K - 1}$ under homogeneity. For Example~\ref{exa:strata} the fitted counts are $10.17$, $30.70$ and $62.36$, and $X^2_{\text{BD}} = 0.50$ on $2$ degrees
of freedom, $P = 0.78$, in agreement with Woolf's test.

\subsection{Problems}

\begin{problem}
For the sex-stratified data of Section~\ref{sec:stratified} (men: $20, 80, 40, 200$; women: $40, 20, 30, 150$), compute $\widehat{\OR}_{\text{MH}}$ and the weights of
Proposition~\ref{prop:mhweights}. Why is the summary inappropriate here?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Men: $n = 340$, $R = 4000/340 = 11.76$, $S = 3200/340 = 9.41$. Women: $n = 240$, $R = 6000/240 = 25.0$, $S = 600/240 = 2.5$. So $\widehat{\OR}_{\text{MH}} = 36.76/11.91 = 3.09$, with weights
$9.41/11.91 = 0.79$ on men's $1.25$ and $0.21$ on women's $10.0$. The stratum odds ratios are strongly heterogeneous ($Q = 20.8$), and the average $3.09$
describes neither sex. The Mantel--Haenszel estimator assumes a common odds ratio; when that fails, it estimates a weighted average whose
weights depend on the design, not a population parameter of interest.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
A cohort study is stratified by sex. Men: $20$ cases among $200$ exposed, $10$ among $400$ unexposed. Women: $40$ among $400$ exposed, $15$ among $300$
unexposed. Compute $\widehat{\RR}_{\text{MH}}$ with a $95\%$ interval, and $\widehat{\RD}_{\text{MH}}$. Compare with the crude risk ratio.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Men: $n = 600$, $a n_0/n = 20 \times 400/600 = 13.33$, $b n_1/n = 10 \times 200/600 = 3.33$. Women: $n = 700$, $40 \times 300/700 = 17.14$, $15 \times 400/700 = 8.57$. So
$\widehat{\RR}_{\text{MH}} = 30.48/11.90 = 2.56$. The variance numerator is $\sum (m_1 n_1 n_0/n^2 - ab/n)$: men $30 \times 200 \times 400/600^2 - 200/600 = 6.667 - 0.333 = 6.333$, women
$55 \times 400 \times 300/700^2 - 600/700 = 13.469 - 0.857 = 12.612$; total $18.945$. Then $\Var(\log\widehat{\RR}_{\text{MH}}) = 18.945/(30.48 \times 11.90) = 0.0522$, $\text{SE} = 0.229$,
interval $(1.64,\ 4.01)$. For the risk difference, $\sum(an_0 - bn_1)/n = (13.33 - 3.33) + (17.14 - 8.57) = 18.57$ and $\sum n_1 n_0/n = 133.3 + 171.4 = 304.8$, so $\widehat{\RD}_{\text{MH}} = 0.061$.
The crude risk ratio is $(60/600)/(25/700) = 2.80$; sex confounds the crude association slightly, since women are more often exposed and
at higher baseline risk.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
\label{prob:onetwo}
In a case-control study with two controls matched to each case, a matched set is described by whether the case is exposed and by the
number $j$ of exposed controls. Show that
\[
\widehat{\OR}_{\text{MH}} = \frac{\sum_j n_{1j}(2 - j)}{\sum_j n_{0j}\,j} ,
\]
where $n_{1j}$ ($n_{0j}$) is the number of sets with an exposed (unexposed) case and $j$ exposed controls. Evaluate it when $n_{10} = 15$, $n_{11} = 10$, $n_{12} = 5$,
$n_{01} = 8$, $n_{02} = 2$.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Each set is a stratum with $n_k = 3$. If the case is exposed, $a = 1$, $b = 0$, $c = j$, $d = 2 - j$, so $R = (2 - j)/3$ and $S = 0$. If the case is unexposed, $a = 0$,
$b = 1$, $c = j$, $d = 2 - j$, so $R = 0$ and $S = j/3$. Summing, the factors $\tfrac13$ cancel. With the data, the numerator is $15 \times 2 + 10 \times 1 + 5 \times 0 = 40$ and the
denominator $8 \times 1 + 2 \times 2 = 12$, so $\widehat{\OR}_{\text{MH}} = 3.33$. Sets in which the case and both controls share an exposure status contribute nothing.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
\label{prob:doctors}
In Example~\ref{exa:doctors}, compute the rate difference per $100{,}000$ person-years in each age group. Does the effect of smoking on
coronary mortality look heterogeneous on the additive scale?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
The rates per $100{,}000$ (smokers, non-smokers) are $61$ and $11$; $240$ and $112$; $720$ and $490$; $1469$ and $1083$; $1918$ and $2120$. The differences are $50$, $128$,
$230$, $386$ and $-202$. The excess rises with age up to $74$ and reverses in the oldest group, which is small and contains survivors (smokers
susceptible to heart disease have died earlier). The additive and multiplicative pictures differ: the relative effect falls steadily
with age, while the number of extra deaths per smoker grows through middle and early old age. Which matters depends on the question.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
Show that under a common odds ratio, $E(a_k d_k - \psi b_k c_k) = 0$ in a cohort stratum as well, where $a_k \sim \text{Bin}(n_{1k}, p_{1k})$ and $b_k \sim \text{Bin}(n_{0k}, p_{0k})$.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$d_k = n_{0k} - b_k$ and $c_k = n_{1k} - a_k$, with $a_k$, $b_k$ independent. So $E(a_k d_k) = n_{1k}p_{1k}\,n_{0k}(1 - p_{0k})$ and $E(b_k c_k) = n_{0k}p_{0k}\,n_{1k}(1 - p_{1k})$. Their
ratio is $p_{1k}(1 - p_{0k})/\{p_{0k}(1 - p_{1k})\} = \psi$, so $E(a_k d_k) - \psi E(b_k c_k) = 0$. Dividing by the fixed $n_k$ gives $E(R_k - \psi S_k) = 0$, and the consistency argument
of Theorem~\ref{thm:mhconsistent} goes through.
\end{solution}

\section{Follow-up studies: life tables and person-years}
\label{sec:followup}

In a follow-up study people enter observation at different times, are followed for different lengths of time, and leave for different
reasons: the event of interest, death from another cause, migration, or the end of the study. Section~\ref{sec:rate} handled this by
counting person-time and assuming a constant rate. This section relaxes that assumption in two ways. Life tables and the Kaplan--Meier
estimator describe how risk accumulates with time without assuming any form for the hazard. Person-years analysis lets the rate vary
with age and calendar time by splitting each person's follow-up across bands of both.

\subsection{Time scales and the Lexis diagram}

Every person in a follow-up study moves along three time scales at once: \textbf{calendar time}, \textbf{age}, and \textbf{time since entry}
(since exposure began, diagnosis, or randomisation). Rates of most diseases depend strongly on age and often on calendar period, so
person-time must be classified by both. The \textbf{Lexis diagram} (Figure~\ref{fig:lexis}) shows this: calendar time on one axis, age on
the other, and each person as a line at $45^\circ$, since age and calendar time advance together.

\subhead{Splitting person-time}

To compute rates by age band, each person's follow-up is cut at the band boundaries. A woman born on 1 July 1950 who enters a study on
1 January 1990 (aged $39.5$) and is followed until 1 July 2003 (aged $53$) contributes $0.5$ person-years at ages $35$--$39$, $5$ at $40$--$44$, $5$ at $45$--$49$
and $3$ at $50$--$54$. An event is assigned to the band in which it occurs. Summing over people gives the person-years $T_j$ and events $D_j$ in each
band, from which age-specific rates, standardised rates and SMRs (Section~\ref{sec:standard}) follow. Splitting by calendar period as
well, the cells of the Lexis diagram, is done the same way.

\subsection{The actuarial life table}

A \textbf{cohort life table} summarises survival in a group followed from a common starting point, such as diagnosis, with time since
entry divided into intervals. For interval $i$ let $l_i$ be the number alive and under observation at its start, $d_i$ the number who have the
event in it, and $w_i$ the number withdrawn alive (censored) in it.

\begin{defn}
The \textbf{actuarial} estimate of the conditional probability of the event in interval $i$, among those who reach it, is
\[
\hat q_i = \frac{d_i}{l_i - w_i/2} ,
\]
and the estimated probability of surviving to the end of interval $i$ is $\hat S_i = \prod_{h \leq i}(1 - \hat q_h)$.
\end{defn}

The denominator $l_i - w_i/2$, the \textbf{effective number at risk}, counts each withdrawal as at risk for half the interval, which is right on
average if withdrawals are spread evenly through it. The survival estimate multiplies conditional probabilities: to survive three
intervals one must survive the first, then the second given the first, and so on.

\begin{thm}[Greenwood's formula]
\label{thm:greenwood}
Treating the $d_i$ as binomial given the effective numbers at risk $n_i'$, with the intervals conditionally independent,
\[
\widehat{\Var}(\hat S_i) = \hat S_i^2 \sum_{h \leq i} \frac{d_h}{n_h'(n_h' - d_h)} .
\]
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
$\log\hat S_i = \sum_{h \leq i}\log(1 - \hat q_h)$. Each $\hat q_h$ is a binomial proportion with variance $q_h(1 - q_h)/n_h'$, and by the delta method
$\Var\{\log(1 - \hat q_h)\} \approx q_h/\{n_h'(1 - q_h)\}$. Conditionally independent terms have summed variances. Substituting $\hat q_h = d_h/n_h'$ gives
$q_h/\{n_h'(1 - q_h)\} = d_h/\{n_h'(n_h' - d_h)\}$. A second application of the delta method, $\Var(\hat S) \approx S^2\Var(\log\hat S)$, gives the result.
\end{proof}

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:lifetable}
$500$ patients are followed for five years after treatment. The life table is:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{crrrrccc}
\hline
Year & $l_i$ & $d_i$ & $w_i$ & $n_i'$ & $\hat q_i$ & $\hat S_i$ & $\text{SE}(\hat S_i)$ \\
\hline
0--1 & 500 & 40 & 20 & 490.0 & 0.0816 & 0.918 & 0.012 \\
1--2 & 440 & 35 & 25 & 427.5 & 0.0819 & 0.843 & 0.017 \\
2--3 & 380 & 30 & 30 & 365.0 & 0.0822 & 0.774 & 0.020 \\
3--4 & 320 & 20 & 40 & 300.0 & 0.0667 & 0.722 & 0.021 \\
4--5 & 260 & 15 & 45 & 237.5 & 0.0632 & 0.677 & 0.023 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The five-year survival is $0.677$, with interval $0.677 \pm 1.96 \times 0.023 = (0.632,\ 0.722)$. Simply dividing the $140$ events by $500$ would give a five-year
risk of $0.28$, too low, because the $160$ people withdrawn before five years were not observed long enough to have all their events counted.
\end{exa}

The life table assumes that withdrawals are \textbf{non-informative}: those withdrawn had the same future risk as those who stayed. A
patient who leaves the study because they feel too ill to attend breaks this assumption, and the life table then overestimates
survival.

\subsection{The Kaplan--Meier estimator}

When exact times are known, the intervals can be made so fine that each contains at most one distinct event time. Then
intervals without events contribute factors of $1$, and withdrawals never share an interval with an event except by a tie, which is
resolved by counting those censored at $t$ as at risk at $t$.

\begin{defn}
Let $t_1 < t_2 < \cdots$ be the distinct event times, $d_j$ the number of events at $t_j$ and $n_j$ the number at risk just before $t_j$ (those with
follow-up time $\geq t_j$). The \textbf{Kaplan--Meier} (product-limit) estimator is
\[
\hat S(t) = \prod_{j: t_j \leq t}\Bigl(1 - \frac{d_j}{n_j}\Bigr) ,
\]
a step function that drops only at event times.
\end{defn}

Greenwood's formula applies with $n_h'$ replaced by $n_j$. Kaplan and Meier (1958) showed that $\hat S$ is the nonparametric maximum likelihood
estimate of the survivor function under non-informative censoring.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:km}
Ten patients on a new treatment (A) have follow-up times in months $3$, $5$, $7^+$, $9$, $12$, $15^+$, $18$, $22^+$, $24^+$, $24^+$, where $^+$ marks a censored time. The
Kaplan--Meier estimate is:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccccc}
\hline
$t_j$ & $n_j$ & $d_j$ & $\hat S(t_j)$ & SE \\
\hline
3 & 10 & 1 & 0.900 & 0.095 \\
5 & 9 & 1 & 0.800 & 0.126 \\
9 & 7 & 1 & 0.686 & 0.152 \\
12 & 6 & 1 & 0.571 & 0.164 \\
18 & 4 & 1 & 0.429 & 0.174 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
At $9$ months only $7$ are at risk, because the patient censored at $7$ months has left the risk set without an event. The estimate stays at $0.429$
from $18$ to $24$ months. The standard errors are large: ten patients cannot estimate a survival curve precisely.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Comparing survival: the log-rank test}

To compare survival between two groups, form at each distinct event time $t_j$ the $2 \times 2$ table of group by event (yes or no) among those
at risk. With $n_{Aj}$ and $n_{Bj}$ at risk, $d_j$ events in total and $d_{Aj}$ in group A, under the null hypothesis of equal hazards the conditional
distribution of $d_{Aj}$ given the margins is hypergeometric (Theorem~\ref{thm:hyper}), with
\[
E_j = d_j\,\frac{n_{Aj}}{n_j}, \qquad V_j = \frac{n_{Aj}n_{Bj}d_j(n_j - d_j)}{n_j^2(n_j - 1)} .
\]

\begin{defn}
The \textbf{log-rank statistic} is
\[
X^2_{\text{LR}} = \frac{\bigl(\sum_j d_{Aj} - \sum_j E_j\bigr)^2}{\sum_j V_j} ,
\]
approximately $\chi^2_1$ under the null hypothesis.
\end{defn}

The log-rank test is the Mantel--Haenszel test (Theorem~\ref{thm:cmh}) with the risk sets at the event times as strata. The tables are
not independent, since the same people appear in many risk sets, but the successive differences $d_{Aj} - E_j$ are uncorrelated given the past
(they form a martingale), which is enough for the same normal approximation. The test is most powerful when the hazard ratio is
constant over time, the proportional hazards situation.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:logrank}
Ten patients on the standard treatment (B) have times $2$, $3$, $4$, $5^+$, $6$, $8$, $10$, $11^+$, $14$, $16$. Combining with Example~\ref{exa:km}, there are $12$ distinct event times
from $2$ to $18$ months. Group A had $O_A = 5$ deaths against $E_A = 8.30$ expected, with $\sum V_j = 2.73$, so $X^2_{\text{LR}} = (5 - 8.30)^2/2.73 = 3.99$, $P = 0.046$. Group B had $8$
deaths against $13 - 8.30 = 4.70$ expected. The ratio $(O_A/E_A)/(O_B/E_B) = 0.602/1.702 = 0.35$ is a rough estimate of the hazard ratio. With so few
patients the evidence that A is better is modest.
\end{exa}

\subsection{The current life table and life expectancy}

A \textbf{current} (period) life table applies the age-specific death rates $m_x$ of one calendar period to a hypothetical cohort, and
summarises them as \textbf{life expectancy}: the average length of life of a cohort that experienced those rates throughout. For an age
interval of length $n$, the death rate $m_x$ (deaths per person-year) must be converted to a probability of dying in the interval.

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:mtoq}
If deaths in the interval occur on average half-way through it, the probability of dying in the interval for a person alive at its
start is
\[
q_x = \frac{n\,m_x}{1 + n\,m_x/2} .
\]
If instead the hazard is constant over the interval, $q_x = 1 - e^{-n m_x}$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Of $l$ people alive at the start, $d = l q_x$ die in the interval. Survivors contribute $n$ person-years each and those who die $n/2$ on average,
so the person-years are $n(l - d) + nd/2 = n(l - d/2)$. Then $m_x = d/\{n(l - d/2)\}$, so $nm_x(l - d/2) = d$, and dividing by $l$, $nm_x(1 - q_x/2) = q_x$, which
rearranges to the result. The constant-hazard form is Theorem~\ref{thm:riskrate}.
\end{proof}

With $q_x$ for each interval, the survivors $l_x$ of a hypothetical cohort follow by multiplication, the person-years lived in each interval are
$L_x = n(l_x + l_{x+n})/2$, and the life expectancy at age $x$ is $e_x = \sum_{y \geq x} L_y/l_x$. Life expectancy at birth is a summary of a period's age-specific
death rates that is free of the age structure of the population, a form of standardisation (Problem~\ref{prob:lifeexp}).

\subsection{Problems}

\begin{problem}
In Example~\ref{exa:km}, verify the estimate and its standard error at $12$ months.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$\hat S(12) = (1 - \tfrac1{10})(1 - \tfrac19)(1 - \tfrac17)(1 - \tfrac16) = 0.9 \times 0.8889 \times 0.8571 \times 0.8333 = 0.5714$. The Greenwood sum is
\begin{align*}
&\frac{1}{10 \times 9} + \frac{1}{9 \times 8} + \frac{1}{7 \times 6} + \frac{1}{6 \times 5} \\
&\qquad = 0.0111 + 0.0139 + 0.0238 + 0.0333 = 0.0821 ,
\end{align*}
so $\text{SE} = 0.5714\sqrt{0.0821} = 0.164$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
Find the Kaplan--Meier estimate for group B of Example~\ref{exa:logrank} at $6$, $10$ and $16$ months. What is the median survival in each
group?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Group B: at risk $10, 9, 8$ at $2, 3, 4$ months (one event each), then the patient censored at $5$ leaves, so $6$ at risk at $6$ months. $\hat S(6) = 0.9 \times \tfrac89 \times \tfrac78 \times \tfrac56 = 0.583$.
Then $\tfrac45$ at $8$ and $\tfrac34$ at $10$: $\hat S(10) = 0.583 \times 0.8 \times 0.75 = 0.350$. After the censoring at $11$, two remain; events at $14$ and $16$ give
$\hat S(16) = 0.350 \times \tfrac12 \times 0 = 0$. The median is the first time $\hat S \leq 0.5$: $8$ months for B ($\hat S = 0.467$) and $18$ months for A ($\hat S = 0.429$).
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
In Example~\ref{exa:lifetable}, compute the five-year survival if withdrawals are ignored in the denominators ($n_i' = l_i$), and if they are
removed at the start of each interval ($n_i' = l_i - w_i$). Which assumption does each correspond to?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
With $n_i' = l_i$: $q = 0.0800, 0.0795, 0.0789, 0.0625, 0.0577$, giving $\hat S_5 = 0.689$. This treats the withdrawn as at risk for the whole interval, so it
overestimates survival. With $n_i' = l_i - w_i$: $q = 0.0833, 0.0843, 0.0857, 0.0714, 0.0698$, giving $\hat S_5 = 0.663$, which treats them as at risk for none of it and
underestimates survival. The actuarial value $0.677$ lies between, assuming withdrawal half-way through on average.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
A cohort member born on 15 March 1940 enters a study on 15 September 1985 and dies on 15 March 1997. Allocate the person-time to five-year
age bands and say in which band the death is counted.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Entry is at age $45.5$ and death at exactly $57$. Person-time: $4.5$ years at $45$--$49$ (to age $50$, on 15 March 1990), $5$ years at $50$--$54$, and $2$
years at $55$--$59$; total $11.5$. The death, at the start of the person's $58$th year, is counted in the $55$--$59$ band.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
\label{prob:lifeexp}
A population has constant death rates $m = 0.002$ per year below age $60$, $0.02$ from $60$ to $80$, and $0.15$ above $80$. Using $q = 1 - e^{-nm}$ for each
of the three intervals, find the proportions surviving to $60$ and to $80$, and the life expectancy at birth.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$l_{60} = e^{-0.12} = 0.887$ and $l_{80} = 0.887\,e^{-0.4} = 0.594$. Under a constant hazard $m$, the expected time lived in an interval of length $n$ by a person alive at its
start is $(1 - e^{-nm})/m$, and above $80$ it is $1/m$. So
\begin{align*}
e_0 &= \frac{1 - e^{-0.12}}{0.002} + 0.887 \times \frac{1 - e^{-0.4}}{0.02} + 0.594 \times \frac{1}{0.15} \\
&= 56.5 + 14.6 + 4.0 = 75.1
\end{align*}
years. The answer depends only on the death rates, not on how many people of each age the population contains.
\end{solution}

\section{Adjustment by regression}
\label{sec:regression}

Stratification controls confounding by comparing like with like, but it runs out of data. With five confounders of three levels each
there are $243$ strata, most of them nearly empty, and continuous confounders such as age must be cut into bands that leave residual
confounding within them. Regression models solve both problems by assuming a form for how risk depends on the confounders, which lets
information be shared across strata. The two models of this section, logistic regression for risks and odds and Poisson regression for
rates, are the model-based counterparts of the Mantel--Haenszel odds ratio and rate ratio.

\subsection{Logistic regression}

\begin{defn}
In the \textbf{logistic regression} model, the probability of disease for a person with exposure $E$ and covariates $C_1, \dots, C_p$ satisfies
\begin{align*}
\operatorname{logit} P(D = 1 \mid E, C) &= \log\frac{P(D = 1 \mid E, C)}{P(D = 0 \mid E, C)} \\
&= \alpha + \beta E + \gamma_1 C_1 + \cdots + \gamma_p C_p .
\end{align*}
\end{defn}

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:logitor}
In the logistic model, $e^\beta$ is the odds ratio for $E = 1$ against $E = 0$ at any fixed value of the covariates. More generally, $e^{\beta\Delta}$ is the odds ratio for
a difference $\Delta$ in a continuous $E$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
The log odds at $E = e + \Delta$ and $E = e$, with $C$ fixed, differ by $\beta\Delta$, since every other term is the same. The log of the odds ratio is this difference.
\end{proof}

So the model asserts that the odds ratio is the same in every stratum of the covariates: it is the homogeneity assumption of
Section~\ref{sec:pooling}, together with an assumption about how the baseline odds vary with $C$. If $C$ is a set of indicators for strata,
the second assumption is vacuous and the model is exactly the ``common odds ratio'' model that the Mantel--Haenszel estimator addresses.
Effect modification is modelled by adding product terms such as $\delta\,E C_1$, which make the odds ratio $e^{\beta + \delta C_1}$ depend on $C_1$.

\subhead{Maximum likelihood}

For individuals $i = 1, \dots, n$ with outcomes $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$, covariate vectors $x_i$ (including a leading $1$ and $E$) and $p_i = 1/(1 + e^{-x_i^\top\theta})$, the
log-likelihood is $\ell(\theta) = \sum_i \{y_i x_i^\top\theta - \log(1 + e^{x_i^\top\theta})\}$, and setting its derivative to zero gives the \textbf{score equations}
\[
\sum_i (y_i - p_i)\,x_i = 0 .
\]
The log-likelihood is concave, so the solution, when it exists, is unique; it is found by Newton--Raphson (iteratively reweighted least
squares). The inverse of the information matrix $\sum_i p_i(1 - p_i)x_i x_i^\top$ estimates the variance of $\hat\theta$, giving Wald intervals $\hat\beta \pm 1.96\,\text{SE}$
for the log odds ratio. Hypotheses are better tested by the \textbf{likelihood ratio} statistic, twice the difference in maximised
log-likelihoods of nested models, approximately $\chi^2$ with degrees of freedom equal to the number of parameters removed.

The score equation for each indicator variable says that the fitted number of cases with that characteristic equals the observed
number. With a stratum indicator, observed and fitted cases agree in every stratum; with the exposure, the fitted number of exposed
cases equals the observed. These are the same balancing conditions that underlie the Mantel--Haenszel test.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:logistic}
Fitting the logistic model with exposure and indicators for the age groups $45$--$59$ and $60+$ to the data of Example~\ref{exa:strata} gives
\[
\operatorname{logit} p = -2.086 + 0.740\,E + 0.683\,A_2 + 1.531\,A_3 ,
\]
with $\text{SE}(\hat\beta) = 0.182$. The age-adjusted odds ratio is $e^{0.740} = 2.10$, with interval $(1.47,\ 2.99)$, against the Mantel--Haenszel $2.11$ and the crude
$3.37$. The likelihood ratio test for $E$ (comparing with the model without it) gives $16.2$ on $1$ degree of freedom. The residual deviance,
$0.50$ on $2$ degrees of freedom, is the likelihood ratio test of the model against the saturated model, which adds the two exposure-by-age
product terms: there is no evidence of effect modification, as the Woolf and Breslow--Day tests also found. The fitted number of exposed
cases is $103$, exactly the observed number.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Logistic regression for case-control data}

The logistic model is written for the probability of disease given exposure, a ``prospective'' quantity, yet a case-control study
samples on disease. It is a remarkable fact that the model can be fitted to case-control data as if they were prospective.

\begin{thm}[Prentice and Pyke]
\label{thm:prentice}
Suppose that in the source population $\operatorname{logit} P(D = 1 \mid x) = \alpha + \beta^\top x$, and that cases are sampled with probability $f_1$ and non-cases with probability
$f_0$, independently of $x$. Then among sampled people
\[
\operatorname{logit} P(D = 1 \mid x,\ \text{sampled}) = \alpha^* + \beta^\top x, \qquad \alpha^* = \alpha + \log(f_1/f_0) .
\]
The coefficients $\beta$, and hence all odds ratios, are unchanged; only the intercept is shifted.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
Write $S$ for the event of being sampled. By Bayes' theorem,
\[
\frac{P(D = 1 \mid x, S)}{P(D = 0 \mid x, S)} = \frac{P(S \mid D = 1, x)\,P(D = 1 \mid x)}{P(S \mid D = 0, x)\,P(D = 0 \mid x)} = \frac{f_1}{f_0}\,e^{\alpha + \beta^\top x} ,
\]
since sampling depends on disease status only. Taking logs gives the result.
\end{proof}

So ordinary logistic regression applied to case-control data estimates the population odds ratios correctly, with standard errors
that are also valid (Prentice and Pyke, 1979, showed that the prospective likelihood gives the correct asymptotic variances). The
intercept, which carries the baseline risk, cannot be interpreted unless the sampling fractions are known. This extends
Theorem~\ref{thm:orinv} from one binary exposure to any number of covariates.

\subsection{Conditional logistic regression for matched data}

In a matched study each matched set has its own baseline odds, a separate intercept $\alpha_s$. There are as many intercepts as sets, so
ordinary maximum likelihood fails: with matched pairs it estimates $\psi^2$ instead of $\psi$, as noted in Section~\ref{sec:mh}. The cure is to
condition on the number of cases in each set, which eliminates $\alpha_s$.

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:clogit}
In a matched set of one case and $M$ controls with covariates $x_0$ (the case) and $x_1, \dots, x_M$, given that exactly one member is a case, the
probability that it is the one observed is
\[
\frac{e^{\beta^\top x_0}}{\sum_{j=0}^{M} e^{\beta^\top x_j}} ,
\]
which does not depend on the set's intercept $\alpha_s$.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Under the logistic model with intercept $\alpha_s$, the probability that member $j$ is the only case is proportional to
$e^{\alpha_s + \beta^\top x_j}\prod_{l}\{1 + e^{\alpha_s + \beta^\top x_l}\}^{-1}$: the product over all members of the probability of each one's status, where $j$ contributes its odds of being a case
times its probability of not being one. The product and the factor $e^{\alpha_s}$ are common to every $j$, and cancel in the ratio of the probability for
$j = 0$ to the sum over $j$.
\end{proof}

The \textbf{conditional likelihood} is the product of these terms over sets, maximised over $\beta$. For matched pairs with a binary exposure it
reduces to the binomial likelihood of Theorem~\ref{thm:pairs}: concordant pairs give $\tfrac12$ whatever $\beta$ is, and discordant pairs give
$\psi/(1 + \psi)$ or $1/(1 + \psi)$. Conditional logistic regression is to the matched study what the Mantel--Haenszel estimator is to the stratified
one, with the advantage that it handles several exposures and continuous covariates.

\subsection{Poisson regression for rates}

\begin{defn}
In the \textbf{Poisson regression} model for grouped follow-up data, cell $j$ (a combination of exposure, age band and period) has $D_j$
events in $T_j$ person-years, with $D_j \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_j T_j)$ independently and
\[
\log\lambda_j = x_j^\top\theta, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \log E(D_j) = \log T_j + x_j^\top\theta .
\]
The known term $\log T_j$ is called the \textbf{offset}.
\end{defn}

As in the logistic model, $e^\beta$ for the exposure is the rate ratio at fixed values of the other covariates. The model with indicators for
age bands and exposure is the ``common rate ratio'' model of Section~\ref{sec:mhrate}. Theorem~\ref{thm:poissonlik} justifies treating
counts of events over person-time as Poisson even though each person's follow-up was random: the likelihood is the same.

\begin{prop}
\label{prop:poisscore}
The score equations of the Poisson model are $\sum_j (D_j - T_j\hat\lambda_j)x_j = 0$. For any indicator variable in the model, the fitted number of events
among the cells it indicates equals the observed number.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
The log-likelihood is $\sum_j \{D_j x_j^\top\theta - T_j e^{x_j^\top\theta}\}$ plus a constant. Its gradient is $\sum_j (D_j - T_j e^{x_j^\top\theta})x_j$. The component for an indicator $x_{jk} \in \{0, 1\}$ is
$\sum_{j: x_{jk} = 1}(D_j - T_j\hat\lambda_j) = 0$.
\end{proof}

With age indicators and the exposure in the model, the fitted deaths among the exposed equal the observed deaths. The rate ratio is
therefore the factor by which the unexposed age-specific rates must be multiplied to make the expected deaths among the exposed equal the
observed ones, an \emph{internal} SMR with the rates estimated jointly.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:poisson}
For the British doctors' data of Example~\ref{exa:doctors}, the Poisson model with five age bands and smoking gives $\hat\beta = 0.355$ with
$\text{SE} = 0.107$, a rate ratio of $1.43$ with interval $(1.15,\ 1.76)$, almost identical to the Mantel--Haenszel estimate. The fitted number of deaths among
smokers is $630$, the observed number. But the residual deviance is $12.1$ on $4$ degrees of freedom ($P = 0.016$): the model of a common rate
ratio does not fit. Adding a term for smoking times age band (coded $0$ to $4$) gives
\[
\log\lambda = \text{age effects} + 1.136\,E - 0.309\,E \times \text{band} ,
\]
with $\text{SE} = 0.097$ for the product term. The likelihood ratio statistic for it is $10.6$ on $1$ degree of freedom, and the residual deviance falls to $1.5$
on $3$. The fitted rate ratio is $e^{1.136} = 3.1$ at ages $35$--$44$ and falls by a factor of $e^{-0.309} = 0.73$ per decade, to $0.91$ at $75$--$84$. The model describes
the effect modification found in Section~\ref{sec:mh} with two parameters instead of five.
\end{exa}

\subsection{Using models well}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The model is an assumption.} A regression coefficient adjusts for confounding only as well as the model describes the
dependence of risk on the confounders. A continuous confounder entered as a straight line when its effect is curved leaves residual
confounding; categorising it, or using splines, is safer.
\item \textbf{Choose confounders by causal reasoning}, as in Section~\ref{sec:dags}: adjust for common causes, not for mediators or
colliders. Automatic selection by significance tests is not a substitute.
\item \textbf{Check for effect modification} with product terms and report stratum-specific estimates when it is present.
\item \textbf{Sparse data} distort maximum likelihood estimates away from the null when there are few events per parameter; with many
matched sets or strata, conditional methods are needed.
\item \textbf{Trends.} For an exposure with ordered levels, entering it as a single score tests for a dose--response trend with one degree
of freedom, more powerful against a monotone effect than the test with one indicator per level.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Problems}

\begin{problem}
Show that for a single $2 \times 2$ table the logistic model $\operatorname{logit} p = \alpha + \beta E$ gives $\hat\beta = \log(ad/bc)$, the log of the crude odds ratio, and $\text{SE}(\hat\beta)$
equal to Woolf's.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
With two groups the model is saturated: the score equations make the fitted number of cases among the exposed equal $a$ and among all
subjects equal $a + b$, so the fitted risks are $a/n_1$ and $b/n_0$. Then $\hat\alpha = \operatorname{logit}(b/n_0) = \log(b/d)$ and $\hat\alpha + \hat\beta = \log(a/c)$, so $\hat\beta = \log(ad/bc)$. The
information matrix for $(\alpha, \beta)$ is $\begin{pmatrix} w_0 + w_1 & w_1 \\ w_1 & w_1 \end{pmatrix}$ with $w_1 = n_1\hat p_1(1 - \hat p_1) = ac/n_1$ and $w_0 = bd/n_0$. Its inverse has $(2, 2)$ entry $(w_0 + w_1)/(w_0 w_1) = 1/w_0 + 1/w_1$,
which is $n_0/(bd) + n_1/(ac) = 1/b + 1/d + 1/a + 1/c$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
In a case-control study, a logistic regression of disease on systolic blood pressure (mmHg) and other covariates gives a coefficient of
$0.047$ with standard error $0.012$ for blood pressure. Find the odds ratio, with a $95\%$ interval, for a difference of $10$ mmHg.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:logitor} the odds ratio is $e^{10 \times 0.047} = e^{0.47} = 1.60$, with interval $e^{10(0.047 \pm 1.96 \times 0.012)} = (1.26,\ 2.02)$. By
Theorem~\ref{thm:prentice} this is a valid estimate of the population odds ratio, although the study sampled on disease.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
In a population, $\operatorname{logit} P(D = 1 \mid E) = -5 + 0.8E$. A case-control study takes every case and $1\%$ of non-cases. What intercept will a logistic regression
on the study data estimate? If the fitted intercept is $-0.40$ and the sampling fractions are known, what are the estimated risks for
unexposed and exposed people in the population?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:prentice}, $\alpha^* = -5 + \log(1/0.01) = -5 + 4.605 = -0.395$. From a fitted $-0.40$ the population intercept is $-0.40 - 4.605 = -5.005$, and with $\hat\beta = 0.8$
the risks are $1/(1 + e^{5.005}) = 0.0067$ and $1/(1 + e^{4.205}) = 0.0147$. This is Proposition~\ref{prop:fractions} in model form.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
Using the interaction model of Example~\ref{exa:poisson}, give the fitted smoking rate ratio in each age band, and explain why
the product term is entered as a single score rather than as four indicators.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
$e^{1.136 - 0.309k}$ for $k = 0, \dots, 4$: $3.11$, $2.29$, $1.68$, $1.23$, $0.91$. A single score uses one degree of freedom to test and describe a steady decline of the
log rate ratio with age, which is what the stratum estimates suggest; four indicators would reproduce the five stratum ratios exactly
(the saturated model) and test heterogeneity of any kind with four degrees of freedom, with less power against a trend. The residual
deviance of $1.5$ on $3$ degrees of freedom shows that the linear trend describes the data adequately.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
For a matched set with one case and two controls and a binary exposure, write the conditional likelihood contribution when (a) the case
is exposed and one control is, (b) the case is unexposed and both controls are exposed, (c) all three are unexposed.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
With $\psi = e^\beta$, Proposition~\ref{prop:clogit} gives (a) $\psi/(\psi + \psi + 1) = \psi/(2\psi + 1)$; (b) $1/(1 + 2\psi)$; (c) $1/3$, free of $\psi$, so the set carries no
information. In general a set with an exposed case and $j$ exposed controls contributes $\psi/\{(j + 1)\psi + (2 - j)\}$, and one with an unexposed case
$1/\{j\psi + (3 - j)\}$. Maximising the product gives the conditional estimate; the Mantel--Haenszel estimate of Problem~\ref{prob:onetwo} is a
non-iterative approximation to it.
\end{solution}
