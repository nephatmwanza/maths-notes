1.1 Introduction

In the scientific study of physical phenomena we build mathematical models in order to predict what will be observed. These models are of two kinds.

Example 1.1.1 (Deterministic models). In a deterministic model the output is fixed completely by the inputs, with no element of chance.

(i)
Mechanics. An object with initial speed \(u\) and constant acceleration \(a\) has travelled \(s = ut + \frac 12at^2\) after time \(t\).
(ii)
Geometry. A sphere of radius \(r\) has volume \(V = \frac 43\pi r^3\).
(iii)
Ohm’s law. The voltage across a conductor is \(V = IR\), the product of the current and the resistance.

Example 1.1.2 (Non-deterministic (stochastic) models). In a stochastic model the same conditions can lead to different outcomes.

(i)
Telecommunications. The number of data packets arriving at a server during a peak hour.
(ii)
Reliability. The lifetime of a semiconductor before it fails.
(iii)
Finance and insurance. The daily change in a stock index, or the number of claims made to an insurer in a quarter.
(iv)
Public health. The number of new infections in a population over six months.

Probability theory provides the mathematical framework for describing and analysing such non-deterministic situations.
