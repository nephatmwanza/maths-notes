3.3 Conditional Probability Functions and Independence of Random Variables

Definition 3.3.1. Let \(X\) and \(Y\) have a joint probability function \(f_{X,Y}(x,y)\). Then the conditional distribution of \(X\) given \((Y=y^*)\) denoted by \(f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y^*)\) and is given by \[f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y^*)=\frac {f_{X,Y}(x,y^*)}{f_Y(y^*)}\] Thus if \(X\) and \(Y\) are discrete then \[f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y^*)=\frac {f_{X,Y}(x,y^*)}{f_Y(y^*)}=\frac {P(X=x, Y=y^*)}{P(Y=y^*)}.\] In general \(f_{X,Y}(x,y^*)\) is the joint function evaluated at \(Y=y^*\). \(f_Y(y^*)\) is the marginal probability function of \(Y\) evaluated at \(Y=y^*\).

Similarly the conditional distribution of \(Y\) given \(X=x^*\) given by \[f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x^*)=\frac {f_{X,Y}(x^*,y)}{f_X(x^*)}.\]

Example 3.3.2. Consider the following jointly distributed random variables \(X\) and \(Y\).

Find the conditional probability mass function of \(Y\) given that \(X = 0\).

Solution. \(f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid 0)=\) conditional distribution of \(Y\) given \(X=0\) \[f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid 0)=\frac {f_{X,Y}(0,y)}{f_X(0)}=\frac {P(X=0,Y=y)}{P(X=0)}=\frac {P(X=0,Y=y)}{0.3}.\] We have that \begin {align*} f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid 0) & = \frac {P(X=0,Y=0)}{0.3}, \quad y = 0\\ & = \frac {P(X=0,Y=1)}{0.3}\, , \quad y = 1\\ & = \frac {P(X=0,Y=2)}{0.3}\, , \quad y = 2\\ & = \frac {P(X=0,Y=3)}{0.3}\, , \quad y = 3. \end {align*}

Example 3.3.3. Suppose that the joint distribution of \(X\) and \(Y\) is given by \[f(x,y) = \begin {cases} \frac {e^{-x/y}\,e^{-y}}{y}\, , & 0 < x < \infty , \quad 0 < y < \infty \\ 0\,, \quad \text {otherwise} \end {cases}.\] Find \(P(X > 1\mid Y = 1)\).

Solution. We first obtain the conditional distribution of \(X\) given that \(Y = y\). \[f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y) = \frac {f(x,y)}{f_Y(y)}\] Then marginal \(f_Y(y)\) is \[f_Y(y) = \frac {1}{y}e^{-y}\int ^{\infty }_0 e^{-x/y}\, dx = \frac {1}{y}e^{-y}\left [-ye^{-x/y}\right ]^{\infty }_0 = e^{-y}.\] Hence, \[f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y = 1) = \frac {e^{-x}\, e^{-1}}{e^{-1}} = e^{-x}.\] Therefore \[P(X > 1\mid Y = 1) = \int ^{\infty }_1e^{-x}\, dx = \left [-e^{-x}\right ]^{\infty }_1 = e^{-1}.\] □

Definition 3.3.4. Let \(X\) and \(Y\) have a joint probability function \(f_{X,Y}(x,y)\) , \(X\) and \(Y\) are said to be independent if and only if \[f_{X,Y}(x,y)=f_X(x)f_Y(y)\] where \(f_X(x)\) and \(f_Y(y)\) are the marginal probability functions of \(X\) and \(Y\) respectively.

Theorem 3.3.5. If \(X\) and \(Y\) have a joint probability function \(f_{X,Y}(x,y)\) and \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent then \[f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y^*)=f_X(x)\quad \text {and}\quad f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x^*)=f_Y(y).\]

Proof. In the independent case \[f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y^*) = \frac {f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_Y(y^*)}=\frac {f_{X,Y}(X=x,Y=y^*)}{f_Y(y^*)} = \frac {f_X(x)\cdot f_Y(y^*)}{f_Y(y^*)} = f_X(x).\] □

Example 3.3.6. For the joint distribution of \(X\) and \(Y\) given by \[f_{X,Y}(x,y)=\frac {\binom {3}{x}\binom {2}{y}\binom {3}{2-x-y}}{\binom {8}{2}}\] the marginals of \(X\) and \(Y\) are \[f_X(x)=\frac {\binom {3}{x}\binom {5}{2-x}}{\binom {8}{2}}\quad x=0,1,2\] and \[f_Y(y)=\frac {\binom {2}{y}\binom {6}{2-y}}{\binom {8}{2}}\quad y=0,1,2\] \(X\) and \(Y\) are not independent. For example \(f_{X,Y}(2,1) = 0\), since two refills cannot include two blue and one red, while \[f_X(2)f_Y(1) = \frac {3}{28}\cdot \frac {12}{28}>0 .\] A single pair \((x, y)\) at which the joint probability differs from the product of the marginals is enough to show dependence.

Theorem 3.3.8. If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent random variables, \[E(XY) = E(X)\, E(Y)\] provided \(E(X)\) and \(E(Y)\) both exist.

Proof. Suppose that \(X\) and \(Y\) are continuous independent random variables. Then the joint p.d.f can be written as \(f_{X,Y}(x,y) = f_X(x)\, f_Y(y)\). Thus \begin {align*} E(XY) & = \int _x\int _y xy\, f_{X,Y}(x,y)\, dy\, dx\\ & = \int _x\int _y xy\, f_X(x)\, f_Y(y)\, dy \, dx\\ & = \left [\int _x x\, f_X(x)\, dx\right ]\, \left [\int _yy\,f_Y(y)\, dy\right ]\\ & = E(X)\, E(Y). \end {align*} □

Note 3.3.9. When random variables are not independent, a measure of the relationship between them, their covariance, enters into the picture.

Definition 3.3.10. If \(X\) and \(Y\) have a joint distribution function \(f_{X,Y}(x,y)\), then the covariance of \(X\) and \(Y\) denoted by \(Cov(X,Y)\) is given by \[\operatorname {Cov}(X,Y)=E\{(X-\mu _X)(Y-\mu _Y)\}\] where \(\mu _X=E(X)\) and \(\mu _Y=E(Y)\).

Remark 3.3.11. For the random variables \(X\) and \(Y\):

1.
\(\operatorname {Cov} (X,Y)=E (XY)-E(X)\,E(Y)=E(XY)-\mu _X\mu _Y\)
2.
\(\operatorname {Cov} (X,Y)= \operatorname {Cov}(Y,X)\)
3.
\(\operatorname {Cov} (X,X)= \operatorname {Var}(X)\)
4.
Cov\((a\,X,Y) = a\,\)Cov\((X,Y)\)
5.
Covariance is a measure of association.

Definition 3.3.12. If \(X\) and \(Y\) have joint distribution function \(f_{X,Y}(x,y)\) then the correlation coefficient of \(X\) and \(Y\) denoted by \(\rho _{XY}\) and is given by \[\rho _{XY}=\frac {\operatorname {Cov}(X,Y)}{\sqrt {\operatorname {Var}(X)\,\operatorname {Var}(Y)}}.\]

\[\rho _{XY}=\frac {\operatorname {Cov}(X,Y)}{\sqrt {\sigma _X^2\,\sigma _Y^2}}=\frac {\operatorname {Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma _X\,\sigma _Y}\]

\(\rho _{XY}\) is a standardised measure of association \(-1\leq \rho _{XY}\leq 1\).

Remark 3.3.13. If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent then, \(\operatorname {Cov}(X,Y)=0\), hence, \(\rho _{XY}=0\). \[\operatorname {Cov}(X,Y) = E(XY) - E(X)\,E(Y) = E(X)\, E(Y) - E(X)\, E(Y) = 0\]

Question. If \(\operatorname {Cov}(X,Y)=0\) does it mean that \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent?

Answer. Yes only if \(X\) and \(Y\) have joint normal distribution. Otherwise no! □

Theorem 3.3.14. The value of the correlation coefficient \(\rho _{XY}\) of \(X\) and \(Y\) is always between \(-1\) and 1. \[-1\leq \rho _{XY} \leq 1\]

Proof. Let \[Q(t)=E\{[(X-\mu _X) + (Y-\mu _Y)t]^2\},\] since \([(X-\mu _X) + (Y-\mu _Y)t]^2\geq 0\) \[\implies E\{[(X-\mu _X)+(Y-\mu _Y)t]^2\}\geq 0\] i.e. \(\quad Q(t)\geq 0\) \begin {align*} Q(t) & = E\left \{\left (X-\mu _X\right )^2+2\left (X-\mu _X\right )\left (Y-\mu _Y\right )t+t^2\left (Y-\mu _Y\right )^2\right \}\\ & = E\left (X-\mu _X\right )^2+2tE\left [\left (X-\mu _X\right )\left (Y-\mu _Y\right )\right ]+t^2E\left (Y-\mu _Y\right )^2\\ & = \operatorname {Var}(X)+2\operatorname {Cov}(X,Y)t+t^2\operatorname {Var}(Y). \end {align*}

Therefore \[Q(t)= \operatorname {Var}(Y)t^2+2\operatorname {Cov}(X,Y)t + \operatorname {Var}(X).\] \(Q(t)\) is a quadratic in \(t\), since \(Q(t)\geq 0\) implies its discriminant must be less than or equal to zero.i.e. \[\left [2\operatorname {Cov}(X,Y)\right ]^2-4\operatorname {Var}(Y)\,\operatorname {Var}(X)\leq 0\] \[4\left [\operatorname {Cov}(X,Y)\right ]^2\leq 4\operatorname {Var}(X)\,\operatorname {Var}(Y)\] \[\implies \frac {\left [\operatorname {Cov}(X,Y)\right ]^2}{\operatorname {Var}(X)\,\operatorname {Var}(Y)}\leq 1\] \[\implies \left [\frac {\operatorname {Cov}(X,Y)}{\sqrt {\operatorname {Var}(X)\,\operatorname {Var}(Y)}}\right ]^2\leq 1\] \[\rho ^2_{XY}\leq 1\] Therefore, \(-1\leq \rho _{XY}\leq 1\). □

Example 3.3.15. A random variable \(X\) has a uniform distribution on \([-1,1]\). Let \(Y = X^2\). Find the correlation coefficient between \(X\) and \(Y\).

Solution. Since \(Y = X^2\) \begin {align*} \operatorname {Cov}(X,Y) & = E(XY) - E(X)\, E(Y)\\ & = E(X^3) - E(X)\, E(X^2). \end {align*}

Since \(X\) has the probability density function \[f_X(x) = \begin {cases} \frac {1}{2}\, & \text {if}\,\, -1\leq x\leq 1,\\ 0 & \text {otherwise}, \end {cases}\] we find that \[E(X) = \int ^1_{-1}x\, \frac {1}{2}\, dx = 0\] and \[E(X^3) = \int ^1_{-1}x^3\, \frac {1}{2}\, dx = 0.\] Therefore \[\operatorname {Cov}(X,Y) = 0.\] Hence \(\rho = 0\) although \(X\) and \(Y\) are clearly dependent: \(Y\) is a function of \(X\). For instance \(P(X>\frac 12, Y<\frac 14) = 0\), while \(P(X>\frac 12)P(Y<\frac 14) = \frac 14\cdot \frac 12>0\). □

Results of this type complicate the interpretation of \(\rho \) but essentially it is a measure of the degree of linear dependence between \(X\) and \(Y\).

3.3.1 The Bivariate Normal Distribution

Definition 3.3.21. \(X\) and \(Y\) have the bivariate normal distribution with parameters \(\mu _X\), \(\mu _Y\), \(\sigma _X>0\), \(\sigma _Y>0\) and \(-1<\rho <1\) if their joint p.d.f. is \[f_{X,Y}(x,y) = \frac {1}{2\pi \sigma _X\sigma _Y\sqrt {1 - \rho ^2}}\exp \left \{-\frac {1}{2(1 - \rho ^2)}\left [u^2 - 2\rho uv + v^2\right ]\right \},\qquad (x, y)\in \mathbb {R}^2,\] where \(u = \frac {x - \mu _X}{\sigma _X}\) and \(v = \frac {y - \mu _Y}{\sigma _Y}\) are the standardised variables.

Theorem 3.3.22. If \((X, Y)\) is bivariate normal as above, then

(i)
\(X\sim N(\mu _X, \sigma _X^2)\) and \(Y\sim N(\mu _Y, \sigma _Y^2)\);
(ii)
given \(X = x\), \(Y\sim N\left (\mu _Y + \rho \frac {\sigma _Y}{\sigma _X}(x - \mu _X),\ \sigma _Y^2(1 - \rho ^2)\right )\), and given \(Y = y\), \(X\sim N\left (\mu _X + \rho \frac {\sigma _X}{\sigma _Y}(y - \mu _Y),\ \sigma _X^2(1 - \rho ^2)\right )\);
(iii)
\(\rho \) is the correlation coefficient of \(X\) and \(Y\), and \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent if and only if \(\rho = 0\).

Proof. Complete the square in \(v\): \(u^2 - 2\rho uv + v^2 = (v - \rho u)^2 + (1 - \rho ^2)u^2\). So \begin {align*} f_{X,Y}(x,y) &= \frac {1}{\sigma _X\sqrt {2\pi }}e^{-u^2/2}\\ &\quad \cdot \frac {1}{\sigma _Y\sqrt {2\pi (1 - \rho ^2)}}\exp \left \{-\frac {(v - \rho u)^2}{2(1 - \rho ^2)}\right \}. \end {align*}

In terms of \(y\), \[v - \rho u = \frac {1}{\sigma _Y}\left [y - \mu _Y - \rho \frac {\sigma _Y}{\sigma _X}(x - \mu _X)\right ],\] so the second factor is the density at \(y\) of a normal distribution with mean \(m(x) = \mu _Y + \rho \frac {\sigma _Y}{\sigma _X}(x - \mu _X)\) and variance \(\sigma _Y^2(1 - \rho ^2)\).

(i)
Integrating over \(y\), the second factor integrates to \(1\), leaving \(f_X(x) = \frac {1}{\sigma _X\sqrt {2\pi }}e^{-u^2/2}\), the \(N(\mu _X, \sigma _X^2)\) density. By symmetry \(Y\sim N(\mu _Y, \sigma _Y^2)\).
(ii)
\(f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x) = \frac {f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_X(x)}\) is exactly the second factor. The statement for \(X\) given \(Y\) follows by exchanging the roles of the variables.
(iii)
By (ii), \(E(Y\mid X) = m(X)\), so \begin {align*} E\left [(X - \mu _X)(Y - \mu _Y)\right ] &= E\left [(X - \mu _X)\left (m(X) - \mu _Y\right )\right ]\\ &= \rho \frac {\sigma _Y}{\sigma _X}E(X - \mu _X)^2 = \rho \sigma _X\sigma _Y, \end {align*}

and the correlation is \(\rho \). If \(\rho = 0\) the joint density factorises into \(f_X(x)f_Y(y)\), so \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent; conversely independent variables have zero correlation.

□

For the bivariate normal, then, zero correlation does imply independence. This is a special property of the normal family; example 3.3.15 showed that it fails in general. The conditional mean \(E(Y\mid X = x)\) is a straight line in \(x\), the regression line of \(Y\) on \(X\), and the conditional variance \(\sigma _Y^2(1 - \rho ^2)\) does not depend on \(x\): knowing \(X\) reduces the variance of \(Y\) by the factor \(1 - \rho ^2\).

Example 3.3.23. The heights in centimetres of fathers (\(X\)) and their adult sons (\(Y\)) are bivariate normal with \(\mu _X = 172\), \(\mu _Y = 175\), \(\sigma _X = 7\), \(\sigma _Y = 8\) and \(\rho = 0.5\).

(a)
Find the distribution of a son’s height given that his father is \(186\) cm tall.
(b)
Find the probability that such a son is taller than his father.
(c)
Find the probability that a randomly chosen son is taller than \(186\) cm.

Solution.

(a)
By theorem 3.3.22, \(Y\mid X = 186\) is normal with mean \(175 + 0.5\cdot \frac 87(186 - 172) = 175 + 8 = 183\) and variance \(64(1 - 0.25) = 48\), so standard deviation \(6.93\).
(b)
\(P(Y>186\mid X = 186) = P\left (Z>\frac {186 - 183}{6.93}\right ) = P(Z>0.43) = 0.333\).
(c)
Unconditionally \(Y\sim N(175, 64)\), so \(P(Y>186) = P\left (Z>\frac {11}{8}\right ) = P(Z>1.375) = 0.085\).

The son of a tall father is expected to be tall, \(183\) cm against the average of \(175\), but less exceptional than his father: the father is \(2\) standard deviations above the mean of fathers, and the son’s expected height is only \(1\) standard deviation above the mean of sons. This is Galton’s regression to the mean, and it is a consequence of \(|\rho |<1\), not of any biological mechanism.

3.3.2 Practice Problems

Problem 3.3.1. Suppose that \(X\sim \text {BETA}(a,b)\) and \(Y\mid x \sim B(n,x)\). Find (a) \(E(Y)\) and (b) \(\operatorname {Var}(Y)\).

Show solution

Solution. Here the success probability of a binomial is itself random – a mixture. The tools are the tower property and the conditional variance formula: \[E(Y) = E[E(Y\mid X)], \qquad \operatorname {Var}(Y) = E[\operatorname {Var}(Y\mid X)] + \operatorname {Var}[E(Y\mid X)].\] Given \(X=x\), \(Y\) is binomial, so \(E(Y\mid X) = nX\) and \(\operatorname {Var}(Y\mid X) = nX(1-X)\). For the beta distribution, \[E(X) = \frac {a}{a+b}, \qquad \operatorname {Var}(X) = \frac {ab}{(a+b)^{2}(a+b+1)}.\]

(a). \[E(Y) = E(nX) = nE(X) = \frac {na}{a+b}.\]

(b). Take the two pieces in turn. Since \(E(X^{2}) = \operatorname {Var}(X)+\left [E(X)\right ]^{2}\), \[E[\operatorname {Var}(Y\mid X)] = n\left [E(X)-E(X^{2})\right ] = n\left [E(X)-E(X)^{2}-\operatorname {Var}(X)\right ],\] \[\operatorname {Var}[E(Y\mid X)] = n^{2}\operatorname {Var}(X).\] Adding them and writing \(s = a+b\) for brevity, \begin {align*} \operatorname {Var}(Y) &= n\left [\frac {a}{s}-\frac {a^{2}}{s^{2}}-\frac {ab}{s^{2}(s+1)}\right ] + n^{2}\frac {ab}{s^{2}(s+1)}\\ &= \frac {nab\,(s+n)}{s^{2}(s+1)} = \frac {nab\,(a+b+n)}{(a+b)^{2}(a+b+1)}. \end {align*}

This is the beta–binomial distribution. Compare it with an ordinary binomial having the same mean, whose variance would be \(np(1-p) = \frac {nab}{(a+b)^{2}}\): the mixture carries an extra factor of \(\frac {a+b+n}{a+b+1}\), which exceeds 1 whenever \(n>1\). Letting the success probability vary makes the count more variable than a plain binomial – this is overdispersion, and it is why the beta–binomial is reached for when count data are more scattered than a binomial can explain.

Problem 3.3.2. Prove that for any jointly distributed random variables \(X\) and \(Y\), \[E[E(Y\mid X)] = E(Y).\]

Show solution

Solution. The result is the tower property used in the previous problem. The point to keep hold of is that \(E(Y\mid X)\) is a random variable – a function of \(X\) – so it has an expectation of its own, taken over the distribution of \(X\).

Take the continuous case; the discrete case is identical with sums in place of integrals. For a fixed value \(x\), \[E(Y\mid X=x) = \int _{-\infty }^{\infty } y\,f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)\,dy.\] Averaging this over \(X\) and using \(f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)\,f_X(x) = f_{X,Y}(x,y)\), \begin {align*} E[E(Y\mid X)] &= \int _{-\infty }^{\infty } E(Y\mid X=x)\,f_X(x)\,dx = \int _{-\infty }^{\infty }\int _{-\infty }^{\infty } y\,f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)\,f_X(x)\,dy\,dx\\ &= \int _{-\infty }^{\infty } y\left (\int _{-\infty }^{\infty } f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dx\right )dy = \int _{-\infty }^{\infty } y\,f_Y(y)\,dy = E(Y). \end {align*}

Integrating the joint density over \(x\) leaves the marginal of \(Y\); that is the whole proof. In words: the overall mean is the average of the group means, each weighted by how likely its group is. The law of total probability is the special case \(Y = I_A\), an indicator.
