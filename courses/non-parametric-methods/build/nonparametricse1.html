1 INTRODUCTION

We shall survey concepts relevant to Non-Parametric methods (NP). It is important to note that it is the method and Not the data that are non-parametric.

Parametric and Non-Parametric Methods
Parametric and Non-parametric are two broad classifications of statistical procedure.

Normal Distribution (ND)
Early in the course families of probability distribution are taught. One of the best known is the ND/Gaussian family, where individual members are specified by assigning constant values of two quantities called parameters, denoted by \(\mu \) and \(\sigma ^2\) representing the mean and variance.
i.e \(N(\mu ,\sigma ^2)\) denotes the ND with these parameters.

Given a set of independent observations from the ND we often want to infer something about the unknown parameter.

Sample \(\overline {X}\) is the point estimate of parameter \(\mu \) (population mean).

t-test is used to measure the strength of the evidence given by a sample to support an a prior hypothesized value of \(\mu _0\) for the population mean.

More usually, we may want to determine a C.I for the ’true’ population mean. This is an interval for which we have reasonable confidence that it contains the true but unknown value \(\mu \)(mean).

This is an example of parametric inference.

The ND is strictly relevant only to some types of continuous scale data such as measurements, works quite well if the measurements scale is course, values recorded to the nearest integer. It is also useful for approximations in a wider range of circumstances when applied to other types of data.

Binomial Distribution (BD)

*
It is relent to some types of counts.
*
Family has parameters \(n,P\) where \(n\rightarrow \) total number of observations and \(P \rightarrow \) probability that a particular one from two possible events occur at any observation.
*
Subject to certain conditions, the number of occurrence, \(r\) where \(0\leq r\leq n\) of that event in the \(n\) observations has a binomial distribution denoted by \(B(n,P)\).
If we observe \(r\) occurrences of an event in a set of \(n\) observations, then \(\widehat {P}=r/n\) is the point estimate of \(P\), probability of success at each independent observation.
*
We may want to access how strong sample evidence supports an a prior hypothesized value \(P_0\) say for \(P\) or obtain a C.I for the value of \(P\) for the population.
*
BD is relevant for counts in discontinuous outcome situations e.g the number of males in a family of size \(n\) is assumed to have BD with \(P=1/2\); the number of \(6s\) recorded in 10 casts of a fair die has a \((10,1/6)\).
*
Outcome of interest is referred to as ”favorable” event.
*
Although not appropriate if the event is say a \(+Ve\) diagnosis of an illness.

Other Distributions

-
Other well known families of parametric distributions include uniform, multnomial, Poisson, exponential, double exponential, gamma, beta and Wei-bull e.t.c
-
Sometimes it is reasonable on theoretical grounds/experience to assume that observations come from a particular family of distributions. Also experience suggest that for many measurement inferences based on the assumption for some ND may not be misleading even it the normality assumption is incorrect because of the CLT. It justifies these and other uses of the ND particularly in what are called asymptotic approximations.
