2.7 McNemar’s Test for Paired Proportions

The sign test compares two conditions applied to the same subjects when the response is a measurement. Often the response is only a yes or no: a voter favours a candidate or does not, a patient obtains relief or does not, a specimen tests positive or negative. Each subject then gives a pair of binary responses, one under each condition, and the question is whether the proportion answering “yes” differs between the two conditions. McNemar’s test, proposed by McNemar in 1947, answers it. It is the sign test in another form, which is why it belongs in this chapter.

The two proportions come from the same subjects, so they are not independent. The usual two-sample test for proportions assumes independent samples and is invalid here. The \(\chi ^{2}\) test of independence on the \(2\times 2\) table answers a different question — whether a subject’s two responses are associated, which they nearly always are — and says nothing about whether the proportions have changed.

Definition 2.28. Let each of \(n\) subjects give a binary response under condition 1 and under condition 2, and classify the pairs as

Condition 2: yes Condition 2: no Total
Condition 1: yes \(a\) \(b\) \(a+b\)
Condition 1: no \(c\) \(d\) \(c+d\)
Total \(a+c\) \(b+d\) \(n\)

The pairs counted by \(a\) and \(d\) are concordant: the subject answered the same way twice. Those counted by \(b\) and \(c\) are discordant. McNemar’s test of \[H_0:\ P(\text {yes under condition 1}) = P(\text {yes under condition 2})\] is based on \(b\) and \(c\) alone.

The concordant pairs drop out for a simple reason. The proportions answering yes are \(\frac {a+b}{n}\) under condition 1 and \(\frac {a+c}{n}\) under condition 2, and their difference \[\frac {a+b}{n}-\frac {a+c}{n} = \frac {b-c}{n}\] does not involve \(a\) or \(d\) at all. A subject who answers the same way twice says nothing about which condition produces more yeses, just as a tied pair says nothing in the sign test.

Theorem 2.29. Under \(H_0\), and conditionally on the number of discordant pairs \(m = b+c\), \[b\sim B\left (m, \tfrac 12\right ).\]

Proof. Let \(p_{10}\) and \(p_{01}\) be the probabilities that a subject answers yes–no and no–yes. Since \(P(\text {yes under 1}) = p_{11}+p_{10}\) and \(P(\text {yes under 2}) = p_{11}+p_{01}\), the null hypothesis is exactly \(p_{10} = p_{01}\). Given that a pair is discordant, it is of the yes–no type with probability \(\frac {p_{10}}{p_{10}+p_{01}}\), which equals \(\frac 12\) under \(H_0\). The subjects are independent, so among \(m\) discordant pairs the number of yes–no type is binomial with parameters \(m\) and \(\frac 12\). □

This is the sign test. Give each discordant pair a “\(+\)” if it is yes–no and a “\(-\)” if it is no–yes, and treat concordant pairs as ties, discarding them. Then \(b\) is the number of plus signs among \(m\) untied pairs.

Test procedure.

(i).
Small \(m\) (say \(m<25\)): exact test. Refer \(\min (b,c)\) to \(B\left (m,\frac 12\right )\). The one-sided \(p\)-value is \(P\left (B\left (m,\frac 12\right )\leq \min (b,c)\right )\), and for a two-sided test double it.
(ii).
Large \(m\): \(\chi ^{2}\) form. Use \[T = \frac {(b-c)^{2}}{b+c},\] which is approximately \(\chi ^{2}\) on one degree of freedom under \(H_0\), and reject when \(T\geq \chi ^{2}_{\alpha ,1}\); for \(\alpha = 0.05\) this is \(3.841\). With the continuity correction the statistic is \(\frac {(|b-c|-1)^{2}}{b+c}\).
(iii).
One-sided alternatives use the signed form \(Z = \frac {b-c}{\sqrt {b+c}}\) referred to \(N(0,1)\); \(T\) is its square.

Note 2.30. The \(\chi ^{2}\) form follows from the theorem. Under \(H_0\), \(b\) has mean \(\frac {m}{2}\) and variance \(\frac {m}{4}\), so \[Z = \frac {b-\frac {m}{2}}{\sqrt {m/4}} = \frac {2b-m}{\sqrt {m}} = \frac {b-c}{\sqrt {b+c}} \quad \text {and}\quad Z^{2} = \frac {(b-c)^{2}}{b+c} = T.\] The test depends only on the number of discordant pairs \(m\), not on the sample size \(n\). A survey of \(1000\) people of whom only \(12\) changed their minds is a small-sample problem, and the exact binomial test should be used.

Example 2.31. A new rapid test and the standard laboratory test are applied to the same \(40\) specimens:

Standard \(+\) Standard \(-\)
Rapid \(+\) \(20\) \(8\)
Rapid \(-\) \(2\) \(10\)

Do the two tests detect positives at the same rate? Use \(\alpha = 0.05\).

Hypotheses. \(H_0\): the two tests have the same positive rate, against \(H_1\): the rates differ.

Reduce to the discordant pairs. \(b = 8\) specimens were positive on the rapid test only and \(c = 2\) on the standard test only, so \(m = 10\). The rapid test calls \(\frac {28}{40} = 70\%\) positive and the standard test \(\frac {22}{40} = 55\%\).

Exact test. With \(m = 10\) the exact binomial test is the right one: \[p\text {-value} = 2P\left (B\left (10,\tfrac 12\right )\leq 2\right ) = 2\cdot \frac {1+10+45}{1024} = \frac {112}{1024} = 0.109.\]

Decision. Since \(0.109>0.05\), \(H_0\) is not rejected. Eight to two looks lopsided, but with only ten informative specimens a split at least that uneven occurs by chance about one time in nine.

Note 2.32. The approximations disagree here. The uncorrected statistic is \(T = \frac {6^{2}}{10} = 3.6\), with \(p = 0.058\), and the corrected one is \(\frac {5^{2}}{10} = 2.5\), with \(p = 0.114\). The corrected value is much closer to the exact \(0.109\). With \(m = 10\) the uncorrected \(\chi ^{2}\) form overstates the evidence, which is why the exact test is preferred when the discordant pairs are few.

Example 2.33. A sample of \(120\) migraine sufferers each used drug A for one attack and drug B for another, in random order, and recorded whether the drug gave relief within two hours:

B: relief B: no relief Total
A: relief \(50\) \(35\) \(85\)
A: no relief \(15\) \(20\) \(35\)
Total \(65\) \(55\) \(120\)

Is there a difference between the drugs?

Discordant pairs. \(b = 35\) patients had relief with A only, and \(c = 15\) with B only, so \(m = 50\), large enough for the \(\chi ^{2}\) form: \[T = \frac {(35-15)^{2}}{35+15} = \frac {400}{50} = 8.0 > 3.841.\] \(H_0\) is rejected at the \(5\%\) level, and the \(p\)-value is \(0.005\). The continuity-corrected value \(\frac {19^{2}}{50} = 7.22\) and the exact \(p\)-value \(2P\left (B\left (50,\tfrac 12\right )\leq 15\right ) = 0.0066\) agree.

Conclusion. The relief rates, \(\frac {85}{120} = 70.8\%\) for A and \(\frac {65}{120} = 54.2\%\) for B, differ significantly, and drug A is the more effective. The \(70\) patients who responded the same way to both drugs played no part in the decision.

Remark 2.34. The test gives a decision; the size of the effect is the difference in proportions, estimated by \(\hat \delta = \frac {b-c}{n}\). An approximate \(95\%\) confidence interval is \[\hat \delta \pm 1.96\,\frac {1}{n}\sqrt {(b+c)-\frac {(b-c)^{2}}{n}}.\] In the migraine example \(\hat \delta = \frac {20}{120} = 0.167\), the standard error is \(\frac {1}{120}\sqrt {50-\frac {400}{120}} = 0.057\), and the interval is \((0.055,\ 0.278)\): drug A gives relief to between about \(5\) and \(28\) more patients in every hundred.

Note 2.35. Assumptions. The \(n\) subjects are independent of one another, each gives one response under each condition, and each response falls into one of two categories. Nothing is assumed about how the two responses of one subject are related; that dependence is the reason for the test. Where the order of the conditions could matter, as with two drugs or before and after, it should be randomised or the comparison interpreted with care. With more than two conditions applied to the same subjects — yes or no under each of \(k\) treatments — the extension is Cochran’s \(Q\) test, which reduces to McNemar’s test when \(k = 2\).
