\section{Mathematical Preliminaries and Error Analysis}
\label{sec:prelim}

Numerical analysis designs methods that produce numbers, and then asks how wrong those numbers can be. Every method in this course
replaces an exact problem (a root, an integral, a solution of a differential equation) by a finite computation, and every computation
is carried out with numbers that have only finitely many digits. So two kinds of error are always present:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{truncation error}, made by the method itself, for example when an infinite series is cut off or a derivative is replaced
by a difference quotient;
\item \textbf{round-off error}, made by the arithmetic, because the computer stores and combines numbers to a fixed number of digits.
\end{itemize}
This section collects the calculus we shall use to bound truncation error, describes how computers represent numbers, and measures the
effect of round-off.

\subsection{Review of calculus}
\label{sec:calc}

The theorems below are proved in a first course in real analysis. They are restated here in the form in which numerical analysis uses
them: each one guarantees that some point exists, and the analysis then uses that point to bound an error.

\begin{thm}[Intermediate Value Theorem]
\label{thm:ivt}
If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and $K$ is any number between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then there is a number $c\in(a,b)$ with $f(c) = K$.
\end{thm}

In particular, if $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ have opposite signs, $f$ has a zero in $(a,b)$. This is the basis of the bisection method in
Section~\ref{sec:bisection}.

\begin{thm}[Extreme Value Theorem]
\label{thm:evt}
If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, there are points $c_1,c_2\in[a,b]$ with $f(c_1)\leq f(x)\leq f(c_2)$ for all $x\in[a,b]$. If in
addition $f$ is differentiable on $(a,b)$, each of $c_1, c_2$ is either an end point of $[a,b]$ or a point where $f' = 0$.
\end{thm}

The second sentence is how maxima are found in practice: compare the values of $f$ at the end points and at the critical points.

\begin{thm}[Rolle's Theorem and the Mean Value Theorem]
\label{thm:mvt}
Let $f$ be continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] If $f(a) = f(b)$, there is $c\in(a,b)$ with $f'(c) = 0$.
\item[(b)] In general there is $c\in(a,b)$ with $f(b) - f(a) = f'(c)(b-a)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{thm}

\begin{thm}[Generalized Rolle's Theorem]
\label{thm:genrolle}
Let $f\in C[a,b]$ be $n$ times differentiable on $(a,b)$. If $f$ vanishes at $n+1$ distinct points $x_0<x_1<\dots<x_n$ of $[a,b]$, then
$f^{(n)}(c) = 0$ for some $c\in(x_0,x_n)$.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
By Rolle's Theorem, $f'$ vanishes at a point of each of the $n$ intervals $(x_{i-1},x_i)$, so $f'$ has $n$ distinct zeros. Applying Rolle's
Theorem to $f'$ between consecutive zeros gives $n-1$ distinct zeros of $f''$, and so on; after $n$ steps $f^{(n)}$ has at least one
zero, lying in $(x_0,x_n)$.
\end{proof}

This theorem is the key to the error formula for polynomial interpolation, later in the course.

\begin{thm}[Weighted Mean Value Theorem for Integrals]
\label{thm:wmvt}
If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, $g$ is integrable on $[a,b]$ and $g$ does not change sign there, then for some $c\in[a,b]$
$$\int_a^b f(x)g(x)\,dx = f(c)\int_a^bg(x)\,dx .$$
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $g\geq0$ (otherwise use $-g$). Let $m$ and $M$ be the minimum and maximum of $f$. Then $mg\leq fg\leq Mg$, and integrating,
$m\int g\leq\int fg\leq M\int g$. If $\int g = 0$ any $c$ works. Otherwise $\int fg/\int g$ lies between $m$ and $M$, and by the
Intermediate Value Theorem it equals $f(c)$ for some $c$.
\end{proof}

This is the tool that turns an error integral into the closed error terms of the quadrature rules for numerical integration.

\begin{thm}[Taylor's Theorem]
\label{thm:taylor}
Suppose $f\in C^n[a,b]$, $f^{(n+1)}$ exists on $[a,b]$, and $x_0\in[a,b]$. Then for every $x\in[a,b]$ there is a number $\xi(x)$ between
$x_0$ and $x$ such that
$$f(x) = P_n(x) + R_n(x),$$
where
\begin{gather*}
P_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^n\frac{f^{(k)}(x_0)}{k!}(x-x_0)^k ,\\
R_n(x) = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi(x))}{(n+1)!}(x-x_0)^{n+1}.
\end{gather*}
\end{thm}

$P_n$ is the \textbf{$n$th Taylor polynomial} of $f$ about $x_0$ and $R_n$ is the \textbf{remainder} or \textbf{truncation error}. When
$x_0 = 0$ the polynomial is also called a Maclaurin polynomial.

\begin{proof}
Fix $x\neq x_0$ and choose the number $K$ so that
$$f(x) = P_n(x) + K(x-x_0)^{n+1}.$$
Define, for $t$ between $x_0$ and $x$,
$$g(t) = f(x) - \sum_{k=0}^n\frac{f^{(k)}(t)}{k!}(x-t)^k - K(x-t)^{n+1}.$$
Then $g(x) = f(x) - f(x) - 0 = 0$ and $g(x_0) = 0$ by the choice of $K$. By Rolle's Theorem, $g'(\xi) = 0$ for some $\xi$ strictly between
$x_0$ and $x$. Differentiating the sum, the terms telescope:
$$\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{f^{(k)}(t)}{k!}(x-t)^k = \sum_{k=0}^n\frac{f^{(k+1)}(t)}{k!}(x-t)^k - \sum_{k=1}^n\frac{f^{(k)}(t)}{(k-1)!}(x-t)^{k-1} = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(t)}{n!}(x-t)^n .$$
So
$$0 = g'(\xi) = -\frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{n!}(x-\xi)^n + (n+1)K(x-\xi)^n ,$$
and since $x-\xi\neq0$, $K = \dfrac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{note}
The point $\xi(x)$ is not known; the theorem only says that it lies between $x_0$ and $x$. Error bounds are obtained by replacing
$|f^{(n+1)}(\xi)|$ by its largest possible value on that interval. This pattern (an exact error formula with an unknown point, followed by
a bound) recurs throughout the course.
\end{note}

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:cos}
Let $f(x) = \cos x$ and $x_0 = 0$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] Find the second Taylor polynomial $P_2$ and use it to approximate $\cos 0.01$. Bound the error.
\item[(b)] Use $P_3$ instead and bound the error.
\item[(c)] Use $P_2$ to approximate $\int_0^{0.1}\cos x\,dx$ and bound the error.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
The derivatives of $\cos$ at $0$ are $1, 0, -1, 0, 1,\dots$, so
$$\cos x = 1 - \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{x^3}{6}\sin\xi(x)$$
with $\xi(x)$ between $0$ and $x$.

(a) $P_2(0.01) = 1 - 0.00005 = 0.99995$. The error is $\frac{(0.01)^3}{6}|\sin\xi|$ with $0<\xi<0.01$. Using only $|\sin\xi|\leq 1$
gives the bound $1.\overline{6}\times10^{-7}$. Using $|\sin\xi|\leq|\xi|<0.01$ gives the sharper bound
$$|\cos 0.01 - 0.99995|\leq\frac{(0.01)^3}{6}(0.01)\approx 1.67\times10^{-9}.$$
The actual error is $4.17\times10^{-10}$.

(b) Since the cubic term of the Maclaurin series of $\cos$ is zero, $P_3 = P_2$, but the remainder now has the form
$R_3(x) = \frac{x^4}{24}\cos\xi$, so
$$|\cos0.01 - 0.99995|\leq\frac{(0.01)^4}{24}\approx 4.17\times10^{-10}.$$
The same approximation, analysed with one more term, gets a bound that is almost exactly the true error.

(c) Integrating the identity term by term,
$$\int_0^{0.1}\cos x\,dx = \left[x - \frac{x^3}{6}\right]_0^{0.1} + \int_0^{0.1}\frac{x^3}{6}\sin\xi(x)\,dx = 0.0998\overline{3} + E,$$
with $|E|\leq\int_0^{0.1}\frac{x^3}{6}\,x\,dx = \frac{(0.1)^5}{30}\approx 3.3\times10^{-7}$. In fact $\int_0^{0.1}\cos x\,dx = \sin0.1 =
0.09983341665$, so the error is $8.33\times10^{-8}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exa}
How many terms of the series $e = \sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{1}{k!}$ are needed to compute $e$ with an error less than $10^{-6}$?
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
Taylor's Theorem for $e^x$ about $0$, evaluated at $x=1$, gives
$$e = \sum_{k=0}^n\frac{1}{k!} + \frac{e^{\xi}}{(n+1)!}, \qquad 0<\xi<1.$$
The error is less than $\frac{3}{(n+1)!}$, since $e^\xi<e<3$. We need $(n+1)!>3\times10^6$. Since $9! = 362880$ and $10! = 3628800$,
$n = 9$ suffices: ten terms, $k = 0,\dots,9$, give $e = 2.7182815\ldots$ with error below $8.3\times10^{-7}$.
\end{solution}


\subsection{Round-off errors and computer arithmetic}
\label{sec:fp}

\subhead{Floating-point numbers}
A computer stores a real number in \textbf{floating-point} form
$$\pm\,(0.d_1d_2\ldots d_t)_\beta\times\beta^{e} = \pm\left(\frac{d_1}{\beta}+\frac{d_2}{\beta^2}+\dots+\frac{d_t}{\beta^t}\right)\beta^e,$$
where $\beta$ is the \textbf{base}, $t$ the number of digits of the \textbf{mantissa}, $0\leq d_i\leq\beta-1$, and the \textbf{exponent} $e$
lies in a fixed range $L\leq e\leq U$. The number is \textbf{normalized} if $d_1\neq0$. Only finitely many numbers have this form, and
they are not evenly spaced: between consecutive powers of $\beta$ there are always the same number of them, so the gaps grow with the
size of the numbers.

\begin{defn}
The \textbf{IEEE 754 double-precision} format, used by almost all present-day computers, stores a number in 64 bits: one sign bit $s$, an
11-bit exponent $c$ and a 52-bit fraction $f$, representing
$$(-1)^s\,2^{c-1023}\,(1+f), \qquad 0\leq f<1 .$$
The leading $1$ is not stored, so the mantissa effectively has 53 binary digits.
\end{defn}

\begin{exa}
Decode the machine number
$$0\ \ 10000000011\ \ 1011100100010000\ldots0 .$$
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
The sign bit is $0$, so the number is positive. The exponent is $c = 2^{10}+2^1+2^0 = 1027$, so the power is $2^{1027-1023} = 2^4$. The
fraction is
$$f = \frac12+\frac18+\frac1{16}+\frac1{32}+\frac1{256}+\frac1{4096} = 0.72290039\ldots$$
Hence the number is $2^4(1+f) = 16\times1.722900390625 = 27.56640625$. Its neighbours in the format differ from it by $2^{4-52}\approx
3.6\times10^{-15}$, and every real number within half that distance of it is stored as $27.56640625$.
\end{solution}

\begin{note}
In double precision the largest number is $(2-2^{-52})2^{1023}\approx1.8\times10^{308}$ and the smallest normalized positive number is
$2^{-1022}\approx2.2\times10^{-308}$. Results larger than the first cause \textbf{overflow}; results smaller than the second cause
\textbf{underflow}. The spacing of the numbers next to $1$ is $\varepsilon_{\text{mach}} = 2^{-52}\approx2.22\times10^{-16}$, the
\textbf{machine epsilon}. Double precision therefore carries about $16$ significant decimal digits.
\end{note}

For hand calculation it is convenient to work with $k$-digit decimal numbers $\pm0.d_1d_2\ldots d_k\times10^n$, $d_1\neq0$. Any positive
real number can be written
$$y = 0.d_1d_2\ldots d_kd_{k+1}d_{k+2}\ldots\times10^n .$$
Its floating-point form $\fl(y)$ is obtained by \textbf{chopping}, which simply discards the digits $d_{k+1}d_{k+2}\ldots$, or by
\textbf{rounding}, which adds $5\times10^{n-(k+1)}$ to $y$ and then chops.

\begin{exa}
Give the five-digit chopping and rounding values of $\pi = 0.314159265\ldots\times10^1$.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
Chopping: $\fl(\pi) = 0.31415\times10^1 = 3.1415$. Rounding: the sixth digit is $9\geq5$, so the fifth digit is increased:
$\fl(\pi) = 0.31416\times10^1 = 3.1416$.
\end{solution}

\subhead{Measuring error}

\begin{defn}
Let $p^*$ be an approximation to $p$. The \textbf{absolute error} is $|p - p^*|$ and, if $p\neq0$, the \textbf{relative error} is
$\dfrac{|p-p^*|}{|p|}$. The \textbf{percentage error} is $100$ times the relative error.
\end{defn}

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:abserr}
Compare the absolute and relative errors of (a) $p = 0.3000\times10^1$ approximated by $p^* = 0.3100\times10^1$; (b) $p = 0.3000\times
10^{-3}$ by $p^* = 0.3100\times10^{-3}$; (c) $p = 0.3000\times10^4$ by $p^* = 0.3100\times10^4$.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
In all three cases the relative error is $\frac{0.0100}{0.3000} = 0.\overline{3}\times10^{-1}$, a percentage error of $3.\overline{3}\%$. The
absolute errors are $0.1$, $0.1\times10^{-4}$ and $0.1\times10^3 = 100$. The absolute error depends on the size of the numbers; the
relative error does not. For this reason the relative error is the better measure of accuracy, and it is the one floating-point
arithmetic controls.
\end{solution}

\begin{defn}
The number $p^*$ approximates $p$ to $t$ \textbf{significant digits} if $t$ is the largest non-negative integer for which
$$\frac{|p-p^*|}{|p|}\leq5\times10^{-t}.$$
\end{defn}

\begin{exa}
How many significant digits do $\frac{22}{7}$, $3.14$ and $3.1416$ give for $\pi$?
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
The relative errors are
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\hline
$p^*$ & relative error & significant digits\\
\hline
$22/7 = 3.142857\ldots$ & $4.02\times10^{-4}$ & $3$\\
$3.14$ & $5.07\times10^{-4}$ & $3$\\
$3.1416$ & $2.34\times10^{-6}$ & $5$\\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
For example $4.02\times10^{-4}\leq5\times10^{-3}$ but $4.02\times10^{-4}>5\times10^{-4}$, so $\frac{22}{7}$ gives three significant
digits. The familiar fraction is no better than $3.14$.
\end{solution}

\begin{thm}
\label{thm:fl}
Let $y\neq0$ and let $\fl(y)$ be its $k$-digit decimal floating-point form. Then
$$\frac{|y-\fl(y)|}{|y|}\leq\begin{cases}10^{-k+1}, & \text{chopping},\\[2pt] \tfrac12\times10^{-k+1}, & \text{rounding}.\end{cases}$$
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
Write $y = 0.d_1d_2\ldots d_kd_{k+1}\ldots\times10^n$ with $d_1\neq0$. For chopping,
$$\frac{|y-\fl(y)|}{|y|} = \frac{0.d_{k+1}d_{k+2}\ldots\times10^{n-k}}{0.d_1d_2\ldots\times10^n} = \frac{0.d_{k+1}d_{k+2}\ldots}{0.d_1d_2\ldots}\times10^{-k}.$$
The numerator is less than $1$ and, since $d_1\geq1$, the denominator is at least $0.1$. So the ratio is at most $\frac{1}{0.1}\times10^{-k}
= 10^{-k+1}$. For rounding, $\fl(y)$ is the nearer of the two $k$-digit neighbours of $y$, which are $10^{n-k}$ apart, so
$|y-\fl(y)|\leq\frac12\times10^{n-k}$, and dividing by $|y|\geq0.1\times10^n$ gives the bound $\frac12\times10^{-k+1}$.
\end{proof}

The number $u = \frac12\beta^{1-t}$ (here $\frac12\times10^{1-k}$) is the \textbf{unit round-off}. The theorem says exactly that
$$\fl(y) = y(1+\delta),\qquad|\delta|\leq u,$$
and computer arithmetic is designed so that the same holds for each operation: if $\odot$ is $+$, $-$, $\times$ or $\div$, then
$\fl(x\odot y) = (x\odot y)(1+\delta)$ with $|\delta|\leq u$. In this model each single operation is accurate; errors arise from
accumulating many operations and, above all, from the next phenomenon.

\subhead{Finite-digit arithmetic and loss of significance}

To model a $k$-digit computer we replace each arithmetic operation by
\begin{align*}
x\oplus y &= \fl(\fl(x)+\fl(y)), & x\ominus y &= \fl(\fl(x)-\fl(y)),\\
x\otimes y &= \fl(\fl(x)\times\fl(y)), & x\oslash y &= \fl(\fl(x)\div\fl(y)).
\end{align*}

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:fivedigit}
Let $x = \frac57$, $y = \frac13$ and $u = 0.714251$, $v = 98765.9$, $w = 0.111111\times10^{-4}$. Use five-digit chopping to compute
$x\oplus y$, $x\ominus u$ and $(x\ominus u)\oslash w$, and find the relative errors.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
Here $\fl(x) = 0.71428$, $\fl(y) = 0.33333$ and $\fl(u) = 0.71425$.

$x\oplus y = \fl(1.04761) = 0.10476\times10^1$. The true value is $\frac{22}{21} = 1.047619\ldots$, so the relative error is
$1.8\times10^{-5}$: well within the bound $10^{-4}$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:fl}.

$x\ominus u = 0.71428 - 0.71425 = 0.30000\times10^{-4}$. The true value is $x - u = 0.347143\times10^{-4}$, so the relative error is
$$\frac{0.347143-0.30000}{0.347143} = 0.136 .$$
A single subtraction has produced an answer that is $14\%$ wrong, although both operands were correct to five digits.

$(x\ominus u)\oslash w = \frac{0.30000\times10^{-4}}{0.11111\times10^{-4}} = 0.27000\times10^1$, against the true value $3.1243$: the
large relative error of the difference is carried into the quotient.
\end{solution}

What happened in the subtraction is called \textbf{loss of significance} or \textbf{cancellation}. The leading four digits of
$\fl(x) = 0.71428$ and $\fl(u) = 0.71425$ agree, so they cancel, and the result has only one significant digit: the digit that came from
the rounding errors already present in $x$ and $u$. Subtracting nearly equal numbers does not create error, but it exposes the errors
the numbers already carried, by making them large relative to the answer. Good numerical practice rearranges a calculation to avoid
such subtractions.

\begin{exa}[The quadratic formula]
\label{exa:quadratic}
The roots of $x^2+62.10x+1 = 0$ are $x_1 = -0.01610724$ and $x_2 = -62.08390$, to seven significant digits. Compute them by the
quadratic formula in four-digit rounding arithmetic, and show how to avoid the loss of accuracy.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
In four-digit rounding,
$$b^2 - 4ac = 3856 - 4 = 3852,\qquad \sqrt{3852} = 62.06 .$$
The usual formula gives
\begin{gather*}
x_1 = \frac{-62.10+62.06}{2} = \frac{-0.04000}{2} = -0.02000,\\
x_2 = \frac{-62.10-62.06}{2} = -62.08 .
\end{gather*}
The relative error in $x_2$ is $2.6\times10^{-4}$, but in $x_1$ it is
$$\frac{|-0.01611+0.02000|}{0.01611} = 0.24 ,$$
because $-b+\sqrt{b^2-4ac}$ subtracts nearly equal numbers. Rationalizing the numerator removes the subtraction:
$$x_1 = \frac{-b+\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\cdot\frac{-b-\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{-b-\sqrt{b^2-4ac}} = \frac{-2c}{b+\sqrt{b^2-4ac}} = \frac{-2}{62.10+62.06} = \frac{-2}{124.2} = -0.01610,$$
with relative error $4.5\times10^{-4}$. The rule is: when $b>0$ compute $x_2$ from the usual formula and $x_1$ from the rationalized
one (or from $x_1x_2 = c/a$); when $b<0$, do the opposite.
\end{solution}

\begin{exa}[Nested multiplication]
\label{exa:horner}
Evaluate $f(x) = x^3-6.1x^2+3.2x+1.5$ at $x = 4.71$ using three-digit arithmetic, both directly and in the nested form
$f(x) = ((x-6.1)x+3.2)x+1.5$. The exact value is $f(4.71) = -14.263899$.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
The intermediate results are:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccccc}
\hline
 & $x^2$ & $x^3$ & $6.1x^2$ & $3.2x$ & direct & nested\\
\hline
chopping & $22.1$ & $104$ & $134$ & $15.0$ & $-13.5$ & $-14.2$\\
rounding & $22.2$ & $105$ & $135$ & $15.1$ & $-13.4$ & $-14.3$\\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
With chopping, the direct evaluation has relative error $5.4\%$ and the nested one $0.45\%$; with rounding, $6.1\%$ and $0.25\%$. The
nested form uses two multiplications instead of four, so it is cheaper, and it avoids the subtraction of the large nearly equal numbers
$104$ and $134$. Nested evaluation of polynomials is called \textbf{Horner's method}; we return to it in the section on zeros of polynomials.
\end{solution}


\subsection{Algorithms and convergence}
\label{sec:alg}

An \textbf{algorithm} is a finite sequence of unambiguous steps that produces an approximate solution of a problem. Algorithms in this
course are written in a plain step-by-step form, giving the input, the output and the steps.

Two questions are asked of every algorithm: does the error introduced at one stage grow or die out at later stages, and how fast does
the approximation approach the answer?

\begin{defn}
Suppose an error of size $E_0>0$ is introduced at some stage of a calculation, and $E_n$ denotes its size after $n$ further operations.
If $E_n\approx CnE_0$ for a constant $C$, the growth is \textbf{linear}; if $E_n\approx C^nE_0$ with $C>1$, it is \textbf{exponential}.
An algorithm is \textbf{stable} if small changes in the initial data produce correspondingly small changes in the results, and
\textbf{unstable} otherwise. Linear growth is usually acceptable; exponential growth is not.
\end{defn}

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:recurrence}
For any constants $c_1, c_2$ the sequence $p_n = c_1\left(\frac13\right)^n+c_23^n$ satisfies the recurrence
$$p_n = \tfrac{10}{3}p_{n-1}-p_{n-2},\qquad n = 2,3,\dots$$
With $p_0 = 1$ and $p_1 = \frac13$ the solution is $p_n = \left(\frac13\right)^n$. Compute $p_n$ from the recurrence in five-digit
rounding arithmetic.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
That every such $p_n$ satisfies the recurrence is checked by substitution: $\frac{10}3\cdot\frac13 - 1 = \frac19$ and $\frac{10}3\cdot3-1 =
9$, and these are exactly the ratios $\left(\frac13\right)^2$ and $3^2$. The computed values are:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rlll}
\hline
$n$ & computed $\hat p_n$ & exact $p_n$ & error\\
\hline
$0$ & $1.0000$ & $1.0000$ & $0$\\
$1$ & $0.33333$ & $0.33333\ldots$ & $3.3\times10^{-6}$\\
$2$ & $0.11110$ & $0.11111$ & $1.1\times10^{-5}$\\
$3$ & $0.037000$ & $0.037037$ & $3.7\times10^{-5}$\\
$4$ & $0.012230$ & $0.012346$ & $1.2\times10^{-4}$\\
$5$ & $0.0037660$ & $0.0041152$ & $3.5\times10^{-4}$\\
$6$ & $0.00032300$ & $0.0013717$ & $1.0\times10^{-3}$\\
$7$ & $-0.0026893$ & $0.00045725$ & $3.1\times10^{-3}$\\
$8$ & $-0.0092872$ & $0.00015242$ & $9.4\times10^{-3}$\\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The rounding of $p_1$ (and of $\frac{10}{3}$) means the computation actually follows a solution with a tiny non-zero $c_2$. The term
$c_23^n$ triples at each step, so the error grows exponentially, by a factor of about $3$ per step, and by $n = 7$ the computed values
have the wrong sign. The recurrence is unstable for this problem. By contrast the recurrence $p_n = 2p_{n-1}-p_{n-2}$, whose solutions
$c_1+c_2n$ grow only linearly, started from the same values gives errors that grow only linearly: after eight steps in the same
arithmetic the error is $1.7\times10^{-4}$.
\end{solution}

\subhead{Rates of convergence}

\begin{defn}
Suppose $\beta_n\longrightarrow0$ and $\alpha_n\longrightarrow\alpha$. If there is a constant $K$ such that $|\alpha_n-\alpha|\leq
K|\beta_n|$ for all large $n$, then $\alpha_n$ converges to $\alpha$ with \textbf{rate of convergence} $O(\beta_n)$, and we write
$\alpha_n = \alpha+O(\beta_n)$. Usually $\beta_n = \frac{1}{n^p}$, and we look for the largest $p$.

Similarly, if $G(h)\longrightarrow0$ and $F(h)\longrightarrow L$ as $h\longrightarrow0$, and $|F(h)-L|\leq K|G(h)|$ for all sufficiently
small $h$, we write $F(h) = L+O(G(h))$. Usually $G(h) = h^p$.
\end{defn}

\begin{exa}
Show that $\alpha_n = \frac{n+1}{n^2}$ and $\hat\alpha_n = \frac{n+3}{n^3}$ both converge to $0$, with rates $O\left(\frac1n\right)$ and
$O\left(\frac1{n^2}\right)$.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
For $n\geq1$, $\alpha_n = \frac1n+\frac1{n^2}\leq\frac2n$ and $\hat\alpha_n = \frac1{n^2}+\frac3{n^3}\leq\frac4{n^2}$, so $K = 2$ and
$K = 4$ will do. At $n = 100$, $\alpha_n = 0.0101$ and $\hat\alpha_n = 0.000103$: the second sequence is about $100$ times closer to
its limit, as the rates predict.
\end{solution}

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:sinh}
Show that $\dfrac{\sin h}{h} = 1+O(h^2)$ and $\cos h + \frac12h^2 = 1+O(h^4)$ as $h\longrightarrow0$.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
By Taylor's Theorem, $\sin h = h - \frac{h^3}{6}+\frac{h^5}{120}\cos\xi$, so
$$\left|\frac{\sin h}{h}-1\right| = \left|-\frac{h^2}{6}+\frac{h^4}{120}\cos\xi\right|\leq\frac{h^2}{6}+\frac{h^4}{120}\leq\frac{h^2}{5}$$
for $|h|\leq1$. Similarly $\cos h = 1-\frac{h^2}{2}+\frac{h^4}{24}\cos\tilde\xi$, so $\left|\cos h+\frac{h^2}{2}-1\right|\leq\frac{h^4}{24}$. Numerically,
$\frac{\sin 0.1}{0.1} = 0.998334$ and $\frac{\sin 0.01}{0.01} = 0.99998333$: reducing $h$ by a factor of $10$ reduces the error by a
factor of $100$, as $O(h^2)$ predicts.
\end{solution}

\begin{defn}
\label{defn:order}
Suppose $p_n\longrightarrow p$ with $p_n\neq p$ for all $n$. If there are constants $\lambda>0$ and $\alpha\geq1$ with
$$\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|p_{n+1}-p|}{|p_n-p|^\alpha} = \lambda,$$
then $\{p_n\}$ converges to $p$ with \textbf{order} $\alpha$ and \textbf{asymptotic error constant} $\lambda$. Order $\alpha = 1$ (with
$\lambda<1$) is \textbf{linear} convergence and $\alpha = 2$ is \textbf{quadratic} convergence.
\end{defn}

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:linquad}
Compare a linearly convergent sequence with $\frac{|p_{n+1}-p|}{|p_n-p|} = 0.5$ and a quadratically convergent sequence with
$\frac{|p_{n+1}-p|}{|p_n-p|^2} = 0.5$, both starting with $|p_0-p| = 1$. How many terms does each need to reach an error of
$10^{-14}$?
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
For the linear sequence $|p_n-p| = 0.5^n$, which first falls below $10^{-14}$ when $n = 47$. For the quadratic one,
$|p_n-p| = 0.5|p_{n-1}-p|^2$, which gives $|p_n-p| = 0.5^{2^n-1}$:
$$0.5,\quad 0.125,\quad 7.8\times10^{-3},\quad 3.1\times10^{-5},\quad 4.7\times10^{-10},\quad 1.1\times10^{-19},$$
so six steps suffice, against $47$. Quadratic convergence roughly doubles the number of correct digits at each step. This difference is why
Newton's method, which converges quadratically, is so widely used.
\end{solution}


\subsection{Practice Problems}

\begin{problem}
Find the third Taylor polynomial $P_3$ of $f(x) = \ln(1+x)$ about $x_0 = 0$, use it to approximate $\ln 1.1$, and find a bound for the
error. Compare it with the actual error.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
$f'(x) = (1+x)^{-1}$, $f''(x) = -(1+x)^{-2}$, $f'''(x) = 2(1+x)^{-3}$, $f^{(4)}(x) = -6(1+x)^{-4}$. So $P_3(x) = x-\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac{x^3}{3}$
and $P_3(0.1) = 0.1-0.005+0.000\overline{3} = 0.095\overline{3}$. The remainder is $R_3(x) = -\frac{6}{(1+\xi)^4}\frac{x^4}{24}$ with
$0<\xi<0.1$, so $|R_3(0.1)|\leq\frac{(0.1)^4}{4} = 2.5\times10^{-5}$. Since $\ln1.1 = 0.0953102$, the actual error is $2.3\times10^{-5}$:
the bound is nearly sharp, because $\xi$ is close to $0$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
Use four-digit rounding arithmetic to compute $\frac{13}{14}-\frac67$ and $\left(\frac{13}{14}-\frac67\right)/(2e-5.4)$, and find the
relative errors.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
$\fl\left(\frac{13}{14}\right) = 0.9286$ and $\fl\left(\frac67\right) = 0.8571$, so the difference is $0.07150$; the true value is
$\frac1{14} = 0.0714286$, a relative error of $1.0\times10^{-3}$. For the denominator, $\fl(2e) = \fl(5.43656) = 5.437$ and
$5.437-5.4 = 0.03700$, while the true value is $0.0365637$: relative error $1.2\times10^{-2}$, because the subtraction cancels three
digits. The quotient is $\fl(0.07150/0.03700) = 1.932$, against the true value $1.953540$, a relative error of $1.1\%$. Each subtraction
of nearly equal numbers costs digits.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
The function $f(x) = \frac{1-\cos x}{x^2}$ tends to $\frac12$ as $x\longrightarrow0$. Explain why computing it directly for $x = 10^{-5}$
in double precision gives a poor answer, and give an equivalent formula that does not.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
For small $x$, $\cos x\approx1-\frac{x^2}{2}$ is very close to $1$, so $1-\cos x$ subtracts nearly equal numbers. At $x = 10^{-5}$,
$1-\cos x\approx5\times10^{-11}$, while the absolute round-off error in $\cos x$ is about $10^{-16}$; the relative error of the difference can therefore be as large as $10^{-16}/(5\times10^{-11}) = 2\times10^{-6}$. In fact double precision
gives $f(10^{-5}) = 0.50000004$, correct to only seven of its sixteen digits, and for $x = 10^{-8}$ it gives $0$, since $\cos x$ rounds
to exactly $1$. Using $1-\cos x = 2\sin^2\frac{x}{2}$,
$$f(x) = \frac{2\sin^2(x/2)}{x^2} = \frac12\left(\frac{\sin(x/2)}{x/2}\right)^2,$$
which involves no subtraction and is accurate for all small $x$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
Show that the $k$-digit rounding of $\frac23$ and of $0.\overline{6}$ are the same, and find the smallest $k$ for which $\fl(\pi)$ (by
rounding) approximates $\pi$ with relative error less than $10^{-6}$.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
They are the same number, $0.666\ldots$; its $k$-digit rounding is $0.66\ldots67$ (with $k-1$ sixes). For $\pi$, Theorem~\ref{thm:fl}
guarantees a relative error at most $\frac12\times10^{1-k}$, which is below $10^{-6}$ when $k\geq7$. In fact $k = 6$ already works:
$\fl(\pi) = 3.14159$ has relative error $8.4\times10^{-7}$. The theorem gives a guarantee for every number, not the error for a
particular one.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
Show that $\frac{e^h-1}{h} = 1+O(h)$ as $h\longrightarrow0$, and find the rate of convergence of $\frac{e^h-e^{-h}}{2h}$ to $1$.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
By Taylor's Theorem $e^h = 1+h+\frac{h^2}{2}e^{\xi}$, so $\frac{e^h-1}{h}-1 = \frac h2e^\xi$, which is at most $\frac{e}{2}|h|$ for $|h|\leq
1$: the rate is $O(h)$. For the symmetric quotient, $e^h-e^{-h} = 2h+\frac{h^3}{3}+\frac{h^5}{60}+\cdots$, so $\frac{e^h-e^{-h}}{2h} =
1+\frac{h^2}{6}+O(h^4)$: the rate is $O(h^2)$. This is the first sign of a fact used in numerical differentiation: a centred difference is
more accurate than a one-sided one.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}
A sequence satisfies $p_n = p+\frac{1}{n}$. Show that it converges to $p$ sublinearly, that is, $\frac{|p_{n+1}-p|}{|p_n-p|}\longrightarrow
1$, and estimate how many terms are needed for an error below $10^{-6}$. Contrast this with $q_n = p+10^{-n}$.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
$\frac{|p_{n+1}-p|}{|p_n-p|} = \frac{n}{n+1}\longrightarrow1$, so the error ratio approaches $1$ and each step gains less and less. An error
below $10^{-6}$ needs $n>10^6$ terms. For $q_n$ the ratio is exactly $\frac{1}{10}$: linear convergence with $\lambda = 0.1$, and seven
terms suffice. Sublinear convergence is far too slow for practical use.
\end{solution}

\section{Solutions of Equations in One Variable}
\label{sec:roots}

One of the oldest problems of numerical analysis is to find a \textbf{root}, or \textbf{zero}, of an equation $f(x) = 0$: a number $p$
with $f(p) = 0$. Except for special equations (linear and quadratic ones, for instance) there is no formula for the root, and it must
be approximated by a sequence $p_0, p_1, p_2,\dots$ that converges to it. This section develops the main methods, proves when and how
fast they converge, and compares them on the same problems.

Throughout the section we use the equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:test}
f(x) = x^3+4x^2-10 = 0
\end{equation}
as a common test problem. Since $f(1) = -5<0$ and $f(2) = 14>0$, the Intermediate Value Theorem gives a root in $(1,2)$; and since
$f'(x) = 3x^2+8x>0$ there, the root is unique. Its value is $p = 1.365230013$ to ten significant digits.


\subsection{The bisection method}
\label{sec:bisection}

Suppose $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ have opposite signs. Then $f$ has a root in $(a,b)$. The \textbf{bisection
method} halves the interval, keeps the half on which $f$ still changes sign, and repeats.

\begin{alg}[Bisection]
\label{alg:bisection}
To find a solution of $f(x) = 0$, given $f$ continuous on $[a,b]$ with $f(a)f(b)<0$.

\emph{Input:} end points $a, b$; tolerance $\varepsilon$; maximum number of iterations $N$.\\
\emph{Output:} an approximate solution $p$, or a message of failure.
\begin{enumerate}[label=Step \arabic*:,leftmargin=*]
\item Set $a_1 = a$, $b_1 = b$, $n = 1$.
\item While $n\leq N$ do Steps 3--5.
\item Set $p_n = a_n+\frac{b_n-a_n}{2}$, the midpoint.
\item If $f(p_n) = 0$ or $\frac{b_n-a_n}{2}<\varepsilon$, output $p_n$ and stop.
\item If $\sgn f(a_n)\cdot\sgn f(p_n)>0$, set $a_{n+1} = p_n$, $b_{n+1} = b_n$; otherwise set $a_{n+1} = a_n$, $b_{n+1} = p_n$. Increase $n$
by $1$.
\item Output ``method failed after $N$ iterations'' and stop.
\end{enumerate}
\end{alg}

\begin{note}
Two details of the algorithm are deliberate. The midpoint is computed as $a_n+\frac{b_n-a_n}{2}$ rather than $\frac{a_n+b_n}{2}$: when $a_n$ and
$b_n$ are close, rounding can make the second form fall outside $[a_n,b_n]$, while the first adds a small correction to a good
approximation. And the sign test uses $\sgn f(a_n)\cdot\sgn f(p_n)$ rather than the product $f(a_n)f(p_n)$, which can underflow to $0$
or overflow when the values are very small or very large.
\end{note}

\begin{thm}
\label{thm:bisection}
Suppose $f\in C[a,b]$ and $f(a)f(b)<0$. Then the bisection method generates a sequence $\{p_n\}$ converging to a zero $p$ of $f$, with
$$|p_n-p|\leq\frac{b-a}{2^n},\qquad n\geq1 .$$
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
By construction each interval $[a_n,b_n]$ has $f(a_n)f(b_n)\leq0$ and length
$$b_n-a_n = \frac{b-a}{2^{n-1}} .$$
The left end points $a_n$ form an increasing sequence bounded above by $b$, and the right end points $b_n$ a decreasing sequence bounded
below by $a$; both converge, and since $b_n-a_n\longrightarrow0$ they have the same limit $p$. By continuity
$f(p)^2 = \lim f(a_n)f(b_n)\leq0$, so $f(p) = 0$. Finally $p$ and $p_n = \frac{a_n+b_n}{2}$ both lie in $[a_n,b_n]$, and $p_n$ is its
midpoint, so
$$|p_n-p|\leq\frac{b_n-a_n}{2} = \frac{b-a}{2^n}.$$
\end{proof}

The theorem says that $p_n = p+O(2^{-n})$: each iteration gains one binary digit, so about $3.3$ iterations are needed per decimal digit.
It also tells us in advance how many iterations are needed.

\begin{exa}
How many bisection iterations on $[1,2]$ guarantee an error less than $10^{-3}$ for the root of~\eqref{eq:test}? How many are needed to
find $\sqrt3$ to within $10^{-4}$ by solving $x^2-3 = 0$ on $[1,2]$?
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
We need $2^{-N}(2-1)<10^{-3}$, that is, $N>\log_2 10^3 = 9.97$; so $N = 10$ iterations suffice. This is a guarantee, not a prediction: the table above shows errors
$1.95\times10^{-3}$, $4.4\times10^{-6}$ and $9.7\times10^{-4}$ at $n = 8, 9, 10$, so the actual error does not decrease at every step, but it is
below $10^{-3}$ from $n = 10$ on, as promised.
For $\sqrt3$ we need $N>\log_2 10^4 = 13.3$, so $N = 14$. Indeed $p_{14} = 1.73199463$, with error $5.6\times10^{-5}$.
\end{solution}

\subhead{Stopping criteria}
In practice $p$ is unknown, and one of the following tests is used to stop, with a tolerance $\varepsilon>0$:
\begin{align*}
&\text{(i)} && |p_N-p_{N-1}|<\varepsilon,\\
&\text{(ii)} && \frac{|p_N-p_{N-1}|}{|p_N|}<\varepsilon\quad (p_N\neq0),\\
&\text{(iii)} && |f(p_N)|<\varepsilon .
\end{align*}
None of them is foolproof, and it is worth knowing why.

\begin{exa}
Show by examples that (i) and (iii) can stop far from a root.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
For (i): the partial sums $p_n = \sum_{k=1}^n\frac1k$ satisfy $|p_n-p_{n-1}| = \frac1n\longrightarrow0$, yet $p_n\longrightarrow\infty$. Small
steps do not mean that the sequence is close to its limit, or even that it has one.

For (iii): let $f(x) = (x-1)^{10}$, whose only root is $p = 1$. At $x = 1.1$, $f(x) = 10^{-10}$, so (iii) with $\varepsilon = 10^{-9}$
accepts $1.1$, which is wrong in the first decimal. When $f$ is very flat near the root, $|f|$ is small over a wide interval. The opposite
can also happen: if $f$ is very steep, $|f(p_N)|$ can be large although $p_N$ is very close to $p$.

When nothing is known about $f$, test (ii) is the best of the three, because it measures the step relative to the size of the answer.
For bisection we can do better than all three: the bracket half-width $\frac{b_n-a_n}{2}$, used in Step 4, is a guaranteed bound.
\end{solution}

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:ex3x}
Show that $e^x = 3x$ has two roots, and locate the smaller one by six steps of bisection.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
Let $g(x) = e^x-3x$. Then $g(0) = 1>0$, $g(1) = e-3<0$ and $g(2) = e^2-6>0$, so there is a root in each of $(0,1)$ and $(1,2)$. There are
no others: $g''(x) = e^x>0$, so $g$ is convex and can cross zero at most twice. Bisection on $[0,1]$ gives
$$p_1 = 0.5,\ p_2 = 0.75,\ p_3 = 0.625,\ p_4 = 0.5625,\ p_5 = 0.59375,\ p_6 = 0.609375,$$
with signs of $g(p_n)$: $+,-,-,+,+,+$. So the root lies in $[0.609375, 0.625]$; it is $0.6190613$, within the guaranteed
$2^{-6} = 0.016$ of $p_6$. The other root is $1.5121346$.
\end{solution}

\begin{note}
Bisection always converges once a sign change has been found, which makes it the safest method in this section. Its weaknesses are that
it is slow (linear in the sense of the bound, gaining one bit per step), that it uses only the signs of $f$, and that it needs a sign
change to start. A root at which $f$ touches the axis without crossing, such as the root $1$ of $(x-1)^2$, cannot be found by bisection
at all. In practice bisection is often used to find a good starting point for the faster methods of the next subsections.
\end{note}


\subsection{The method of false position}
\label{sec:falsepos}

Bisection uses only the signs of $f(a_n)$ and $f(b_n)$. If $|f(a_n)|$ is much smaller than $|f(b_n)|$, the root is probably closer to
$a_n$, and the \textbf{method of false position} (\emph{regula falsi}) uses this. It replaces the midpoint by the point where the
straight line through $(a_n,f(a_n))$ and $(b_n,f(b_n))$ crosses the axis:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:falsepos}
p_n = b_n-f(b_n)\frac{b_n-a_n}{f(b_n)-f(a_n)} = \frac{a_nf(b_n)-b_nf(a_n)}{f(b_n)-f(a_n)},
\end{equation}
and then keeps, as bisection does, the subinterval on which $f$ changes sign.

\begin{alg}[False position]
\label{alg:falsepos}
To find a solution of $f(x) = 0$, given $f$ continuous on $[a,b]$ with $f(a)f(b)<0$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=Step \arabic*:,leftmargin=*]
\item Set $a_1 = a$, $b_1 = b$, $n = 1$.
\item While $n\leq N$: compute $p_n$ from~\eqref{eq:falsepos}.
\item If $|p_n-p_{n-1}|<\varepsilon$ (for $n\geq2$), output $p_n$ and stop.
\item If $\sgn f(a_n)\cdot\sgn f(p_n)>0$, set $a_{n+1} = p_n$, $b_{n+1} = b_n$; otherwise $a_{n+1} = a_n$, $b_{n+1} = p_n$. Increase $n$ by $1$.
\item Output ``method failed'' and stop.
\end{enumerate}
\end{alg}

\begin{thm}
\label{thm:falsepos}
Let $f\in C^2[a,b]$ with $f(a)<0<f(b)$ and $f''>0$ on $[a,b]$. Then $f$ has exactly one root $p$ in $(a,b)$, and the method of false
position started on $[a,b]$ keeps $b_n = b$ for every $n$, produces increasing iterates $a<p_1<p_2<\dots<p$, and converges to $p$. The
convergence is linear:
$$\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{p_{n+1}-p}{p_n-p} = 1-\frac{(b-p)f'(p)}{f(b)} = \lambda,\qquad 0<\lambda<1 .$$
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Uniqueness.} A root exists by the Intermediate Value Theorem. If there were two roots $p<q$ in $(a,b)$, write
$p = \theta a+(1-\theta)q$ with $\theta\in(0,1)$. Convexity gives $f(p)\leq\theta f(a)+(1-\theta)f(q) = \theta f(a)<0$, contradicting
$f(p) = 0$.

\emph{The right end point is fixed.} Suppose $a_n<p<b_n = b$ with $f(a_n)<0$. Since $f''>0$, the graph of $f$ on $[a_n,b]$ lies below
the chord joining its end points. At the point $p_n$ where the chord is zero, therefore, $f(p_n)<0$. So $p_n$ lies to the left of $p$,
the sign test keeps $b_{n+1} = b$ and moves the left end point to $a_{n+1} = p_n$. Also $p_n>a_n$, since the chord is negative at
$a_n$. By induction $a<p_1<p_2<\dots<p$.

\emph{Convergence.} The sequence $\{p_n\}$ is increasing and bounded above by $p$, so it converges to some $q\leq p$. Since
$p_{n+1} = b-f(b)\frac{b-p_n}{f(b)-f(p_n)}$ and $f$ is continuous, letting $n\longrightarrow\infty$ gives
$q = b-f(b)\frac{b-q}{f(b)-f(q)}$, which simplifies to $f(q)(b-q) = 0$. As $q\leq p<b$, $f(q) = 0$, and by uniqueness $q = p$.

\emph{Rate.} Write $e_n = p_n-p$. From the second form of~\eqref{eq:falsepos} with $a_n = p_n$, $b_n = b$,
$$e_{n+1} = \frac{p_nf(b)-bf(p_n)}{f(b)-f(p_n)}-p = \frac{e_nf(b)-(b-p)f(p_n)}{f(b)-f(p_n)} .$$
Dividing by $e_n$ and using $\frac{f(p_n)}{e_n} = \frac{f(p_n)-f(p)}{p_n-p}\longrightarrow f'(p)$ and $f(p_n)\longrightarrow0$,
$$\frac{e_{n+1}}{e_n} = \frac{f(b)-(b-p)\frac{f(p_n)}{e_n}}{f(b)-f(p_n)}\longrightarrow\frac{f(b)-(b-p)f'(p)}{f(b)} .$$
Since $f'' > 0$, $f'$ is increasing. By the Mean Value Theorem, $f(p)-f(a) = f'(\zeta)(p-a)$ for some $\zeta\in(a,p)$, so
$f'(p)>f'(\zeta) = \frac{-f(a)}{p-a}>0$; and $f(b) = f(b)-f(p) = f'(\eta)(b-p)$ for some $\eta\in(p,b)$, with $f'(\eta)>f'(p)$. Hence
$0<(b-p)f'(p)<f(b)$, and $0<\lambda<1$.
\end{proof}

For the test problem, $p = 1.36523$, $f'(p) = 3p^2+8p = 16.51$ and $f(2) = 14$, so $\lambda = 1-\frac{0.63477\times16.51}{14} =
0.2513$: exactly the ratio seen in the table. The same reasoning applies with $f''<0$ or with $f(a)>0$; in each case one end point stays
fixed. False position is therefore usually faster than bisection when $\lambda<\frac12$. It can also be much slower.

\label{exa:x10}

\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
Here $f$ is convex, $f(1.3) = 12.79$ is large and $f$ is very flat on most of $[0,1]$. The chords are almost horizontal, so the fixed end
point $b = 1.3$ pulls the iterates only slowly towards $1$:

\subhead{A modified method}
The slowness comes from the end point that never moves. The \textbf{Illinois modification} removes it: whenever the same end point is
retained twice in a row, the stored value of $f$ at that end point is halved before the next chord is drawn. This tilts the chord
towards the stale end point, and the next iterate usually lands on the other side of the root, so both end points move.

\begin{exa}
Apply the Illinois modification to $x^{10}-1$ on $[0,1.3]$ and to $x^3+4x^2-10$ on $[1,2]$.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
For $x^{10}-1$ the iterates are
$$0.0943,\ 0.1818,\ 0.3330,\ 0.5634,\ 0.8464,\ 1.0749,\ 0.9455,\ 0.9828,\ 1.0041,\ 0.99968,\ 0.9999941,$$
reaching an error below $10^{-5}$ after $11$ iterations instead of $52$; from the sixth step on they fall alternately on both sides of the
root. For the test problem the errors after steps $4$ to $7$ are $1.5\times10^{-4}$, $9.0\times10^{-7}$, $8.9\times10^{-7}$ and
$3.9\times10^{-13}$. The Illinois method still brackets the root at every step, so it keeps the safety of bisection, and its convergence is
superlinear (of order about $1.44$).
\end{solution}


\subsection{Fixed-point iteration}
\label{sec:fixedpoint}

\begin{defn}
A number $p$ is a \textbf{fixed point} of a function $g$ if $g(p) = p$.
\end{defn}

Root-finding problems and fixed-point problems are equivalent. If $f(p) = 0$, then $p$ is a fixed point of
$$g(x) = x-f(x),\qquad\text{or more generally of}\qquad g(x) = x-\varphi(x)f(x)$$
for any function $\varphi$; conversely, if $g(p) = p$ then $p$ is a root of $f(x) = x-g(x)$. Fixed-point problems are easier to
analyse, and the choice of $\varphi$ turns out to control everything about the speed of the resulting method.

\begin{exa}
Find the fixed points of $g(x) = x^2-2$.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
A fixed point satisfies $x^2-2 = x$, that is, $(x-2)(x+1) = 0$. So $g$ has two fixed points, $-1$ and $2$: the points where the graph
$y = g(x)$ meets the line $y = x$.
\end{solution}

\begin{thm}
\label{thm:fpexist}
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] If $g\in C[a,b]$ and $g(x)\in[a,b]$ for all $x\in[a,b]$, then $g$ has at least one fixed point in $[a,b]$.
\item[(ii)] If, in addition, $g'(x)$ exists on $(a,b)$ and there is a constant $0<k<1$ with $|g'(x)|\leq k$ for all $x\in(a,b)$, then the
fixed point in $[a,b]$ is unique.
\end{enumerate}
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
(i) If $g(a) = a$ or $g(b) = b$ we are done. Otherwise $g(a)>a$ and $g(b)<b$, since $g$ maps into $[a,b]$. The function $h(x) = g(x)-x$
is continuous on $[a,b]$ with $h(a)>0>h(b)$, so by the Intermediate Value Theorem $h(p) = 0$, that is $g(p) = p$, for some
$p\in(a,b)$.

(ii) Suppose $p\neq q$ are both fixed points in $[a,b]$. By the Mean Value Theorem there is $\xi$ between them with
$$|p-q| = |g(p)-g(q)| = |g'(\xi)|\,|p-q|\leq k|p-q|<|p-q|,$$
a contradiction. So $p = q$.
\end{proof}

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:fpexist}
(a) Show that $g(x) = \frac{x^2-1}{3}$ has a unique fixed point in $[-1,1]$. (b) Show that $g(x) = 3^{-x}$ has a unique fixed point in
$[0,1]$, although part (ii) of the theorem does not apply.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
(a) On $[-1,1]$, $g$ has its minimum $-\frac13$ at $x = 0$ and its maximum $0$ at $x = \pm1$, so $g$ maps $[-1,1]$ into
$\left[-\frac13,0\right]\subseteq[-1,1]$. Also $|g'(x)| = \frac{2|x|}{3}\leq\frac23<1$. By the theorem there is exactly one fixed point; solving
$x^2-3x-1 = 0$ gives $p = \frac{3-\sqrt{13}}{2} = -0.3028$. (The other solution, $\frac{3+\sqrt{13}}2 = 3.3028$, is a fixed point outside
$[-1,1]$; on $[3,4]$ the theorem does not apply, since $g(4) = 5$, yet the fixed point exists. The conditions are sufficient, not
necessary.)

(b) The function $g(x) = 3^{-x}$ is decreasing, with $g(0) = 1$ and $g(1) = \frac13$, so it maps $[0,1]$ into itself and has a fixed point
there. But $g'(x) = -3^{-x}\ln3$, and $|g'(0)| = \ln 3 = 1.0986>1$, so (ii) does not apply. Uniqueness still holds, for a different reason:
$g(x)-x$ is strictly decreasing, so it vanishes only once. The fixed point is $p = 0.5478$.
\end{solution}

To approximate a fixed point we choose $p_0$ and compute $p_n = g(p_{n-1})$ for $n\geq1$. If $p_n\longrightarrow p$ and $g$ is continuous,
then
$$p = \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}p_n = \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}g(p_{n-1}) = g\left(\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}p_{n-1}\right) = g(p),$$
so the limit is a fixed point. This is \textbf{fixed-point iteration}, or \textbf{functional iteration}.

\begin{alg}[Fixed-point iteration]
\label{alg:fixedpoint}
To find a solution of $p = g(p)$, given an initial approximation $p_0$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=Step \arabic*:,leftmargin=*]
\item Set $n = 1$.
\item While $n\leq N$: set $p = g(p_0)$.
\item If $|p-p_0|<\varepsilon$, output $p$ and stop.
\item Set $p_0 = p$ and increase $n$ by $1$.
\item Output ``method failed after $N$ iterations'' and stop.
\end{enumerate}
\end{alg}

horizontally to the line $y = x$, arriving at $(p_1,p_1)$; then vertically to the graph at $(p_1,g(p_1))$; and so on. The resulting
``cobweb'' shows at once whether the iteration converges, and how.

\begin{thm}[Fixed-Point Theorem]
\label{thm:fixedpoint}
Let $g\in C[a,b]$ with $g(x)\in[a,b]$ for all $x\in[a,b]$. Suppose that $g'$ exists on $(a,b)$ and that there is a constant $0<k<1$ with
$|g'(x)|\leq k$ for all $x\in(a,b)$. Then for every $p_0\in[a,b]$ the sequence $p_n = g(p_{n-1})$ converges to the unique fixed point
$p$ in $[a,b]$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fpbound1}
|p_n-p|\leq k^n\max\{p_0-a,\ b-p_0\},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fpbound2}
|p_n-p|\leq\frac{k^n}{1-k}|p_1-p_0| .
\end{equation}
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:fpexist} the fixed point $p$ exists and is unique, and every $p_n$ lies in $[a,b]$ since $g$ maps $[a,b]$ into
itself. By the Mean Value Theorem, for each $n$ there is $\xi_n$ between $p_{n-1}$ and $p$ with
$$|p_n-p| = |g(p_{n-1})-g(p)| = |g'(\xi_n)|\,|p_{n-1}-p|\leq k|p_{n-1}-p| .$$
Applying this $n$ times gives $|p_n-p|\leq k^n|p_0-p|$. Since $0<k<1$, $k^n\longrightarrow0$ and $p_n\longrightarrow p$; and since $p\in[a,b]$,
$|p_0-p|\leq\max\{p_0-a,b-p_0\}$, which gives~\eqref{eq:fpbound1}.

For~\eqref{eq:fpbound2}, the same argument gives $|p_{n+1}-p_n| = |g(p_n)-g(p_{n-1})|\leq k|p_n-p_{n-1}|\leq\dots\leq k^n|p_1-p_0|$. For
$m>n$, by the triangle inequality,
\begin{align*}
|p_m-p_n|&\leq\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}|p_{j+1}-p_j|\leq k^n|p_1-p_0|\left(1+k+k^2+\dots+k^{m-n-1}\right)\\
&\leq\frac{k^n}{1-k}|p_1-p_0| .
\end{align*}
Letting $m\longrightarrow\infty$ gives~\eqref{eq:fpbound2}.
\end{proof}

Bound~\eqref{eq:fpbound2} is the more useful one in practice, because it uses only the first step, which is computed anyway. Both bounds
show that the smaller $k$ is, the faster the convergence.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:g3g4}
Use the Fixed-Point Theorem to explain the behaviour of $g_1$, $g_3$ and $g_4$ in the previous example, and bound the number of
iterations of $g_3$ and $g_4$ needed for an error below $10^{-3}$.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
\emph{$g_1$.} $g_1'(x) = 1-3x^2-8x$, and on $[1,2]$ this is at most $-10$. At the root $|g_1'(p)| = 15.5$, so the iteration cannot converge
(see the corollary below).

\emph{$g_3$.} On $[1,2]$, $g_3(2) = 0.707$ lies outside the interval, so we use $[1,1.5]$. There $g_3$ is decreasing, with $g_3(1) = 1.5$
and $g_3(1.5) = 1.287$, so it maps $[1,1.5]$ into itself. Its derivative $g_3'(x) = -\frac{3x^2}{4\sqrt{10-x^3}}$ satisfies
$|g_3'(x)|\leq|g_3'(1.5)| = 0.656 = k$. The theorem applies, and~\eqref{eq:fpbound2} with $|p_1-p_0| = 0.213$ requires
$$\frac{0.656^n}{1-0.656}(0.213)<10^{-3},\qquad\text{that is}\qquad n>15.2,$$
so $16$ iterations suffice. (In practice $8$ are enough, because near the root the slope is only $|g_3'(p)| = 0.512$.)

\emph{$g_4$.} $g_4'(x) = -\frac{5}{\sqrt{10}(4+x)^{3/2}}$, so on $[1,2]$
$$|g_4'(x)|\leq\frac{5}{\sqrt{10}\cdot5^{3/2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{50}} = 0.141 = k,$$
and $g_4$ maps $[1,2]$ into $[1.291,1.414]\subseteq[1,2]$. Bound~\eqref{eq:fpbound2} with $|p_1-p_0| = 0.152$ gives $n>2.6$, so three
iterations suffice; indeed $|p_3-p| = 2.7\times10^{-4}$. The far smaller value of $k$ for $g_4$ is the reason for its speed.
\end{solution}

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:cosfp}
Solve $x = \cos x$ by fixed-point iteration from $p_0 = 1$, and compare the actual number of iterations needed for an error below $10^{-6}$
with the bound~\eqref{eq:fpbound2}.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
The cosine maps $[0,1]$ into $[\cos1,1] = [0.540,1]\subseteq[0,1]$, and $|g'(x)| = |\sin x|\leq\sin1 = 0.841 = k$ there. The iterates
\begin{gather*}
0.540302,\ 0.857553,\ 0.654290,\ 0.793480,\\
0.701369,\ 0.763960,\ 0.722102,\ 0.750418,\ \dots
\end{gather*}
alternate about the fixed point $p = 0.7390851$, as $g'(p) = -\sin p = -0.674<0$ predicts (Figure~\ref{fig:cobweb}, middle). The bound
needs $\frac{0.841^n}{0.159}(0.4597)<10^{-6}$, that is $n>86.2$; the iteration actually reaches an error below $10^{-6}$ after $32$ steps.
The bound uses the worst slope on the whole interval, $0.841$, while the iterates soon feel only the slope $0.674$ at the fixed point.
\end{solution}

The last remark is made precise by the next result: the rate of convergence is governed by $|g'(p)|$, not by the worst slope.

\begin{coro}
\label{coro:fprate}
Let $g\in C^1$ near a fixed point $p$, with $p_n\longrightarrow p$ and $p_n\neq p$ for all $n$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] If $0<|g'(p)|<1$, the convergence is linear with asymptotic error constant $|g'(p)|$:
$\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|p_{n+1}-p|}{|p_n-p|} = |g'(p)|$.
\item[(ii)] If $|g'(p)|>1$, no sequence of iterates converges to $p$ except one that reaches $p$ exactly after finitely many steps.
\end{enumerate}
\end{coro}

\begin{proof}
(i) By the Mean Value Theorem, $p_{n+1}-p = g(p_n)-g(p) = g'(\xi_n)(p_n-p)$ with $\xi_n$ between $p_n$ and $p$. Since $\xi_n\longrightarrow p$
and $g'$ is continuous, $\frac{|p_{n+1}-p|}{|p_n-p|} = |g'(\xi_n)|\longrightarrow|g'(p)|$.

(ii) By continuity $|g'(x)|\geq K>1$ on some interval $(p-\delta,p+\delta)$. If $p_n\longrightarrow p$ with $p_n\neq p$, then for all large $n$,
$p_n$ lies in that interval and $|p_{n+1}-p| = |g'(\xi_n)|\,|p_n-p|\geq K|p_n-p|>|p_n-p|$: the distances to $p$ increase, which
contradicts $p_n\longrightarrow p$.
\end{proof}

For the five functions of Example~\ref{exa:fivegs}: $g_1'(p) = -15.5$ (divergence), $g_3'(p) = -0.512$ (slow linear convergence, error
roughly halved each step), $g_4'(p) = -0.127$ (fast linear convergence, about $0.9$ of a decimal digit per step). For $g_5$, a short
calculation gives $g_5'(p) = 0$, and convergence is faster than linear.

\begin{thm}
\label{thm:fpquad}
Let $p$ be a fixed point of $g$, with $g'(p) = 0$ and $g''$ continuous and $|g''(x)|<M$ on an open interval $I$ containing $p$. Then there
is a $\delta>0$ such that, for $p_0\in[p-\delta,p+\delta]$, the iterates $p_n = g(p_{n-1})$ converge to $p$ at least quadratically, and
for large $n$
$$|p_{n+1}-p|<\frac{M}{2}|p_n-p|^2 .$$
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
Since $g'$ is continuous and $g'(p) = 0$, choose $\delta>0$ with $[p-\delta,p+\delta]\subseteq I$ and $|g'(x)|\leq k<1$ there. For $x$ in
this interval, $|g(x)-p| = |g(x)-g(p)|\leq k|x-p|<\delta$, so $g$ maps $[p-\delta,p+\delta]$ into itself, and by the Fixed-Point Theorem
$p_n\longrightarrow p$. Taylor's Theorem about $p$ gives
\begin{align*}
p_{n+1} = g(p_n) &= g(p)+g'(p)(p_n-p)+\frac{g''(\xi_n)}{2}(p_n-p)^2\\
&= p+\frac{g''(\xi_n)}{2}(p_n-p)^2,
\end{align*}
with $\xi_n$ between $p_n$ and $p$. Hence $|p_{n+1}-p|\leq\frac M2|p_n-p|^2$, and $\frac{|p_{n+1}-p|}{|p_n-p|^2}\longrightarrow\frac{|g''(p)|}{2}$, so the
order is at least $2$.
\end{proof}

This suggests how to design a fast method for $f(x) = 0$. Look for $g$ of the form $g(x) = x-\varphi(x)f(x)$ with $g'(p) = 0$. Since
$f(p) = 0$,
$$g'(p) = 1-\varphi'(p)f(p)-\varphi(p)f'(p) = 1-\varphi(p)f'(p),$$
which vanishes when $\varphi(p) = \frac{1}{f'(p)}$. The natural choice is $\varphi(x) = \frac1{f'(x)}$, giving
$$g(x) = x-\frac{f(x)}{f'(x)},$$
which is exactly $g_5$ above. This is Newton's method, the subject of the next subsection.

\begin{exa}
\label{exa:relax}
A compromise between $g_1$ and Newton's method is the \textbf{relaxed} iteration $g(x) = x-\lambda f(x)$ with a constant $\lambda$. For the
test equation, choose $\lambda$ using the slope at $p_0 = 1.5$ and compare it with $g_1$ (which is the case $\lambda = 1$).
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
Here $g'(p) = 1-\lambda f'(p)$, and the ideal value $\lambda = \frac1{f'(p)}$ is unknown; so we use $\lambda = \frac{1}{f'(1.5)} = \frac1{18.75} =
0.05333$. Then $g'(p) = 1-0.05333\times16.51 = 0.119$, and the errors of the iterates from $1.5$ are
\begin{gather*}
8.1\times10^{-3},\ 9.4\times10^{-4},\ 1.1\times10^{-4},\ 1.3\times10^{-5},\\
1.6\times10^{-6},\ 1.9\times10^{-7},\ 2.3\times10^{-8}:
\end{gather*}
linear convergence, each error about $0.119$ times the previous one, as Corollary~\ref{coro:fprate} predicts. With $\lambda = 1$, $|g'(p)|
= 15.5$ and the iteration diverges. A single derivative evaluation, used once, turns a divergent iteration into a fast one.
\end{solution}

\begin{exa}[Heron's method]
\label{exa:heron}
To compute $\sqrt a$ ($a>0$), compare the iterations $g(x) = \frac ax$ and $g(x) = \frac12\left(x+\frac ax\right)$, both of which have the fixed
point $\sqrt a$.
\end{exa}

\begin{solution}
For $g(x) = \frac ax$, $g'(\sqrt a) = -\frac{a}{a} = -1$, and the iteration just oscillates: $p_0, \frac a{p_0}, p_0, \dots$ It never
converges unless $p_0 = \sqrt a$. For Heron's iteration, $g'(x) = \frac12\left(1-\frac a{x^2}\right)$, so $g'(\sqrt a) = 0$ and by
Theorem~\ref{thm:fpquad} the convergence is quadratic. With $a = 2$ and $p_0 = 1$:
$$p_1 = 1.5,\quad p_2 = 1.416667,\quad p_3 = 1.414215686,\quad p_4 = 1.41421356237469,$$
with errors $8.6\times10^{-2}$, $2.5\times10^{-3}$, $2.1\times10^{-6}$, $1.6\times10^{-12}$: the number of correct digits roughly doubles
at each step. This method, known in Babylon and described by Heron of Alexandria, is Newton's method applied to $x^2-a = 0$.
\end{solution}
