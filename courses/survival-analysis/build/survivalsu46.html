8.7 Practice Problems

Problem 8.7.1. A factor with four levels is replaced in a Cox model by three indicators. The models with and without it have log partial likelihoods \(-612.4\) and \(-618.9\). Test whether the factor is needed.

Show solution

Solution. \(2(-612.4 + 618.9) = 13.0\) on \(3\) degrees of freedom; the \(5\%\) critical value is \(7.81\) and \(p = 0.0046\). The factor is needed, even if some of the individual indicators are not significant.

Problem 8.7.2. In Example 8.1.1, find the hazard ratio of an adenocarcinoma patient with Karnofsky score \(50\) against a squamous-cell patient with score \(80\), on the same regimen.

Show solution

Solution. \(3.17 \times 0.969^{-30} = 3.17 \times e^{30 \times 0.0315} = 3.17 \times 2.57 = 8.2\), using \(\log 0.969 = -0.0315\). The combination of tumour type and poorer performance gives about eight times the hazard.

Problem 8.7.3. Why can tied event times not be handled by simply listing the tied events in any order in the partial likelihood?

Show solution

Solution. The order affects the risk sets: whichever tied subject is placed first remains in the risk set for the others. Different arbitrary orders give different partial likelihoods. The Breslow method avoids choosing by keeping all tied subjects in every denominator; Efron’s and the exact method average over the possible orders.

Problem 8.7.4. A study has \(60\) deaths. How many covariates (counting each indicator as one) can sensibly be included in a Cox model?

Show solution

Solution. At \(10\) to \(20\) events per coefficient, \(3\) to \(6\) coefficients. More risks over-fitting: coefficients that are too large, unstable selection, and optimistic predictions.

Problem 8.7.5. Explain the difference between adjusting for a covariate and stratifying by it in a Cox model, and when to prefer stratification.

Show solution

Solution. Adjusting includes the covariate in \(e^{\beta ^{\mathsf T}x}\), assuming that its effect multiplies the hazard by a constant factor and estimating that factor. Stratifying gives each level its own baseline hazard, making no assumption about the shape of its effect over time, but estimating no hazard ratio for it. Prefer stratification when the covariate violates proportional hazards or is a nuisance factor with many levels, such as hospital.

Problem 8.7.6. Two patients have predicted linear predictors \(\hat \beta ^{\mathsf T}x\) of \(0.2\) and \(1.1\). If the first has a predicted one-year survival of \(0.6\), what is the second’s, and which is more likely to die first?

Show solution

Solution. \(\hat S_2 = \hat S_1^{\exp (1.1 - 0.2)} = 0.6^{e^{0.9}} = 0.6^{2.46} = 0.285\). The second patient, with the higher linear predictor, has the higher hazard at all times and the lower survival, and a concordant model predicts that this patient dies first.
