3.1 Matrix results we shall need

Vectors are columns and are printed in bold: \(\mathbf {y}\), \(\boldsymbol \beta \). The transpose of \(\mathbf {A}\) is \(\mathbf {A}^\top \), and \((\mathbf {AB})^\top = \mathbf {B}^\top \mathbf {A}^\top \). The trace of a square matrix is the sum of its diagonal entries, \(\operatorname {tr}(\mathbf {A}) = \sum _i a_{ii}\).

Lemma 3.1.1 (Trace). If \(\mathbf {A}\) is \(m \times n\) and \(\mathbf {B}\) is \(n \times m\), then \(\operatorname {tr}(\mathbf {AB}) = \operatorname {tr}(\mathbf {BA})\).

Proof. \(\operatorname {tr}(\mathbf {AB}) = \sum _{i=1}^m (\mathbf {AB})_{ii} = \sum _{i=1}^m \sum _{j=1}^n a_{ij} b_{ji}\) and \(\operatorname {tr}(\mathbf {BA}) = \sum _{j=1}^n \sum _{i=1}^m b_{ji} a_{ij}\). These are the same sum taken in a different order. □

Lemma 3.1.2 (Differentiation). For a vector \(\boldsymbol \beta = (\beta _0, \dots , \beta _k)^\top \), let \(\partial f / \partial \boldsymbol \beta \) denote the vector of partial derivatives \(\partial f / \partial \beta _j\). Then, for a constant vector \(\mathbf {a}\) and a constant symmetric matrix \(\mathbf {A}\), \[ \frac {\partial (\mathbf {a}^\top \boldsymbol \beta )}{\partial \boldsymbol \beta } = \mathbf {a}, \qquad \frac {\partial (\boldsymbol \beta ^\top \mathbf {A} \boldsymbol \beta )}{\partial \boldsymbol \beta } = 2\mathbf {A}\boldsymbol \beta . \]

Proof. \(\mathbf {a}^\top \boldsymbol \beta = \sum _j a_j \beta _j\), whose derivative with respect to \(\beta _m\) is \(a_m\). Next, \[ \boldsymbol \beta ^\top \mathbf {A} \boldsymbol \beta = \sum _i \sum _j a_{ij} \beta _i \beta _j . \] Differentiating with respect to \(\beta _m\), the terms with \(i = m\) give \(\sum _j a_{mj}\beta _j\) and the terms with \(j = m\) give \(\sum _i a_{im}\beta _i\). Since \(a_{im} = a_{mi}\), both equal the \(m\)th entry of \(\mathbf {A}\boldsymbol \beta \), so the derivative is \(2(\mathbf {A}\boldsymbol \beta )_m\). □

For a random vector \(\mathbf {y} = (y_1, \dots , y_n)^\top \), the mean vector \(E(\mathbf {y})\) has entries \(E(y_i)\), and the covariance matrix \[ \operatorname {Var}(\mathbf {y}) = E\left [ (\mathbf {y} - E\mathbf {y})(\mathbf {y} - E\mathbf {y})^\top \right ] \] has \((i,j)\) entry \(\operatorname {Cov}(y_i, y_j)\), and so has the variances \(\operatorname {Var}(y_i)\) down its diagonal.

Lemma 3.1.3 (Linear functions of a random vector). If \(\mathbf {A}\) is a constant matrix and \(\mathbf {b}\) a constant vector, then \[ E(\mathbf {A}\mathbf {y} + \mathbf {b}) = \mathbf {A}E(\mathbf {y}) + \mathbf {b}, \qquad \operatorname {Var}(\mathbf {A}\mathbf {y} + \mathbf {b}) = \mathbf {A} \operatorname {Var}(\mathbf {y}) \mathbf {A}^\top . \]

Proof. The \(i\)th entry of \(\mathbf {Ay} + \mathbf {b}\) is \(\sum _j a_{ij} y_j + b_i\), whose expectation is \(\sum _j a_{ij} E(y_j) + b_i\), the \(i\)th entry of \(\mathbf {A}E(\mathbf {y}) + \mathbf {b}\). Writing \(\boldsymbol \mu = E(\mathbf {y})\), we have \(\mathbf {Ay} + \mathbf {b} - E(\mathbf {Ay} + \mathbf {b}) = \mathbf {A}(\mathbf {y} - \boldsymbol \mu )\), so \[ \operatorname {Var}(\mathbf {Ay} + \mathbf {b}) = E\left [ \mathbf {A}(\mathbf {y} - \boldsymbol \mu )(\mathbf {y} - \boldsymbol \mu )^\top \mathbf {A}^\top \right ] = \mathbf {A} \operatorname {Var}(\mathbf {y}) \mathbf {A}^\top , \] taking the constant matrices outside the expectation. □

Lemma 3.1.4 (Expected quadratic form). If \(E(\mathbf {y}) = \boldsymbol \mu \), \(\operatorname {Var}(\mathbf {y}) = \mathbf {V}\) and \(\mathbf {A}\) is a constant matrix, then \[ E(\mathbf {y}^\top \mathbf {A} \mathbf {y}) = \operatorname {tr}(\mathbf {AV}) + \boldsymbol \mu ^\top \mathbf {A} \boldsymbol \mu . \]

Proof. Write \(\mathbf {y} = \boldsymbol \mu + \mathbf {z}\), where \(E(\mathbf {z}) = \mathbf {0}\) and \(E(\mathbf {z}\mathbf {z}^\top ) = \mathbf {V}\). Then \[ \mathbf {y}^\top \mathbf {A} \mathbf {y} = \boldsymbol \mu ^\top \mathbf {A} \boldsymbol \mu + \boldsymbol \mu ^\top \mathbf {A} \mathbf {z} + \mathbf {z}^\top \mathbf {A} \boldsymbol \mu + \mathbf {z}^\top \mathbf {A} \mathbf {z} . \] The two middle terms have expectation \(0\). The last is a \(1 \times 1\) matrix, so equals its own trace, and by Lemma 3.1.1 \(\mathbf {z}^\top \mathbf {A} \mathbf {z} = \operatorname {tr}(\mathbf {z}^\top \mathbf {A} \mathbf {z}) = \operatorname {tr}(\mathbf {A} \mathbf {z}\mathbf {z}^\top )\). Since the trace is a sum of entries, it can be taken through the expectation: \(E\operatorname {tr}(\mathbf {A}\mathbf {z}\mathbf {z}^\top ) = \operatorname {tr}(\mathbf {A}\,E(\mathbf {z}\mathbf {z}^\top )) = \operatorname {tr}(\mathbf {AV})\). □

Finally, from linear algebra: a square matrix \(\mathbf {A}\) has an inverse exactly when \(\det \mathbf {A} \neq 0\), and then \[ \mathbf {A}^{-1} = \frac {1}{\det \mathbf {A}} \operatorname {adj}(\mathbf {A}), \] where the adjugate \(\operatorname {adj}(\mathbf {A})\) is the transpose of the matrix of cofactors. The cofactor of \(a_{ij}\) is \((-1)^{i+j}\) times the determinant of the matrix left when row \(i\) and column \(j\) are deleted. For a symmetric matrix, such as the ones met in regression, the matrix of cofactors is itself symmetric and no transposing is needed.
