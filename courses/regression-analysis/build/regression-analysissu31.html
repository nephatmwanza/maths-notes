3.13 Practice problems

Problem 3.13.1. A \(2^2\) experiment with two centre points gave

\(x_1\) \(-1\) \(1\) \(-1\) \(1\) \(0\) \(0\)
\(x_2\) \(-1\) \(-1\) \(1\) \(1\) \(0\) \(0\)
\(y\) 20 30 26 38 29 31

(a)
Form \(\mathbf {X}^\top \mathbf {X}\) and \(\mathbf {X}^\top \mathbf {y}\), and fit \(y = \beta _0 + \beta _1 x_1 + \beta _2 x_2 + \varepsilon \).
(b)
Find the residuals, \(\hat \sigma ^2\) and the standard errors.
(c)
Show that the three estimates are uncorrelated. What feature of the design causes this?

Show solution

Solution. (a) \(\sum x_{i1} = \sum x_{i2} = \sum x_{i1}x_{i2} = 0\) and \(\sum x_{i1}^2 = \sum x_{i2}^2 = 4\), so \[ \mathbf {X}^\top \mathbf {X} = \begin {bmatrix} 6 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end {bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf {X}^\top \mathbf {y} = \begin {bmatrix} 174 \\ 22 \\ 14 \end {bmatrix} . \] Here \(\sum x_{i1}y_i = -20 + 30 - 26 + 38 = 22\) and \(\sum x_{i2}y_i = -20 - 30 + 26 + 38 = 14\). A diagonal matrix is inverted by inverting its diagonal, so \[ \hat {\boldsymbol \beta } = \begin {bmatrix} 174/6 \\ 22/4 \\ 14/4 \end {bmatrix} = \begin {bmatrix} 29 \\ 5.5 \\ 3.5 \end {bmatrix}, \qquad \hat y = 29 + 5.5x_1 + 3.5x_2 . \] (b) The fitted values are \(20, 31, 27, 38, 29, 29\), and the residuals \(0, -1, -1, 0, 0, 2\). \(SS_{\mathrm {Res}} = 0 + 1 + 1 + 0 + 0 + 4 = 6\) (check: \(\mathbf {y}^\top \mathbf {y} - \hat {\boldsymbol \beta }^\top \mathbf {X}^\top \mathbf {y} = 5222 - 5216 = 6\)), so \(\hat \sigma ^2 = 6/3 = 2\). Then \(\operatorname {se}(\hat \beta _0) = \sqrt {2/6} = 0.577\) and \(\operatorname {se}(\hat \beta _1) = \operatorname {se}(\hat \beta _2) = \sqrt {2/4} = 0.707\).

(c) \(\operatorname {Var}(\hat {\boldsymbol \beta }) = \sigma ^2\operatorname {diag}(1/6, 1/4, 1/4)\) has zeros off the diagonal. The cause is that the columns of \(\mathbf {X}\) are orthogonal: each pair has zero inner product. This is the main reason designed experiments use such patterns of \(x\) values: each effect is estimated separately from the others, and the calculation is trivial.

Problem 3.13.2. Show that \(\mathbf {e} = (\mathbf {I} - \mathbf {H})\boldsymbol \varepsilon \), and deduce that under A1–A3, \(E(\mathbf {e}) = \mathbf {0}\) and \(\operatorname {Var}(\mathbf {e}) = \sigma ^2(\mathbf {I} - \mathbf {H})\). For the crop data, what is \(\operatorname {Var}(e_6)\), and why is it so much smaller than \(\sigma ^2\)?

Show solution

Solution. \(\mathbf {e} = (\mathbf {I} - \mathbf {H})\mathbf {y} = (\mathbf {I} - \mathbf {H})(\mathbf {X}\boldsymbol \beta + \boldsymbol \varepsilon ) = (\mathbf {I} - \mathbf {H})\boldsymbol \varepsilon \), since \((\mathbf {I} - \mathbf {H})\mathbf {X} = \mathbf {0}\). By Lemma 3.1.3, \(E(\mathbf {e}) = (\mathbf {I} - \mathbf {H})\mathbf {0} = \mathbf {0}\) and \[ \operatorname {Var}(\mathbf {e}) = (\mathbf {I} - \mathbf {H})\sigma ^2\mathbf {I}(\mathbf {I} - \mathbf {H})^\top = \sigma ^2(\mathbf {I} - \mathbf {H})^2 = \sigma ^2(\mathbf {I} - \mathbf {H}) . \] So \(\operatorname {Var}(e_i) = \sigma ^2(1 - h_{ii})\). For plot 6, \(h_{66} = 7/9\), so \(\operatorname {Var}(e_6) = \frac 29\sigma ^2\). Plot 6 has high leverage: the fitted plane is pulled close to it, so its residual is small whatever its error was. Residuals do not all have the same variance, even though the errors do. This is why, in the section on model adequacy, residuals are scaled before they are compared.

Problem 3.13.3. For simple regression, use \((\mathbf {X}^\top \mathbf {X})^{-1}\) from Example 3.3.2 to show that (a) \(\hat \beta _0 = \bar y - \hat \beta _1\bar x\), and (b) \(\sigma ^2(\mathbf {X}^\top \mathbf {X})^{-1}\) has entries \(\operatorname {Var}(\hat \beta _0) = \sigma ^2\bigl (\frac 1n + \frac {\bar x^2}{S_{xx}}\bigr )\), \(\operatorname {Var}(\hat \beta _1) = \sigma ^2/S_{xx}\) and \(\operatorname {Cov}(\hat \beta _0,\hat \beta _1) = -\bar x\sigma ^2/S_{xx}\).

Show solution

Solution. (a) The first entry of \((\mathbf {X}^\top \mathbf {X})^{-1}\mathbf {X}^\top \mathbf {y}\) is \[ \hat \beta _0 = \frac {\sum x_i^2 \sum y_i - \sum x_i \sum x_i y_i}{nS_{xx}} . \] Put \(\sum x_i^2 = S_{xx} + n\bar x^2\) and \(\sum x_i y_i = S_{xy} + n\bar x\bar y\), with \(\sum x_i = n\bar x\) and \(\sum y_i = n\bar y\). The numerator becomes \[ (S_{xx} + n\bar x^2)n\bar y - n\bar x(S_{xy} + n\bar x\bar y) = nS_{xx}\bar y - n\bar x S_{xy}, \] and dividing by \(nS_{xx}\) gives \(\bar y - \bar x S_{xy}/S_{xx} = \bar y - \hat \beta _1\bar x\).

(b) The \((1,1)\) entry is \(\sigma ^2\sum x_i^2/(nS_{xx}) = \sigma ^2(S_{xx} + n\bar x^2)/(nS_{xx}) = \sigma ^2\bigl (\frac 1n + \frac {\bar x^2}{S_{xx}}\bigr )\); the \((2,2)\) entry is \(\sigma ^2 n/(nS_{xx}) = \sigma ^2/S_{xx}\); and the off-diagonal entry is \(-\sigma ^2\sum x_i/(nS_{xx}) = -\sigma ^2 n\bar x/(nS_{xx}) = -\bar x\sigma ^2/S_{xx}\).

Problem 3.13.4. Suppose each regressor is measured in different units, so that \(\mathbf {X}\) is replaced by \(\mathbf {XD}\), where \(\mathbf {D}\) is an invertible diagonal \(p \times p\) matrix (with \(D_{00} = 1\), so the column of ones is unchanged). Show that the fitted values and residuals are unchanged, and that the new coefficient vector is \(\mathbf {D}^{-1}\hat {\boldsymbol \beta }\).

Show solution

Solution. The new hat matrix is \[ \mathbf {XD}\bigl ((\mathbf {XD})^\top \mathbf {XD}\bigr )^{-1}(\mathbf {XD})^\top = \mathbf {XD}\,\mathbf {D}^{-1}(\mathbf {X}^\top \mathbf {X})^{-1}(\mathbf {D}^\top )^{-1}\mathbf {D}^\top \mathbf {X}^\top = \mathbf {H}, \] using \((\mathbf {ABC})^{-1} = \mathbf {C}^{-1}\mathbf {B}^{-1}\mathbf {A}^{-1}\). So \(\hat {\mathbf {y}} = \mathbf {Hy}\) and \(\mathbf {e} = (\mathbf {I} - \mathbf {H})\mathbf {y}\) are unchanged. The new coefficients are \[ \bigl ((\mathbf {XD})^\top \mathbf {XD}\bigr )^{-1}(\mathbf {XD})^\top \mathbf {y} = \mathbf {D}^{-1}(\mathbf {X}^\top \mathbf {X})^{-1}\mathbf {X}^\top \mathbf {y} = \mathbf {D}^{-1}\hat {\boldsymbol \beta } . \] Measuring fertiliser in kg instead of 100 kg multiplies its column by \(100\) and divides its coefficient by \(100\); the fit itself is untouched.

Problem 3.13.5. Show that \(SS_T = \mathbf {y}^\top (\mathbf {I} - \bar {\mathbf {J}})\mathbf {y} = \sum (y_i - \bar y)^2\), and deduce the identity \(SS_T = SS_R + SS_{\mathrm {Res}}\).

Show solution

Solution. \(\mathbf {y}^\top \bar {\mathbf {J}}\mathbf {y} = \frac 1n(\mathbf {1}^\top \mathbf {y})^2 = \frac 1n\left (\sum y_i\right )^2 = n\bar y^2\), so \(\mathbf {y}^\top (\mathbf {I} - \bar {\mathbf {J}})\mathbf {y} = \sum y_i^2 - n\bar y^2 = \sum (y_i - \bar y)^2\) by Lemma 2.2.3. Then, writing \(\mathbf {I} - \bar {\mathbf {J}} = (\mathbf {H} - \bar {\mathbf {J}}) + (\mathbf {I} - \mathbf {H})\), \[ SS_T = \mathbf {y}^\top (\mathbf {H} - \bar {\mathbf {J}})\mathbf {y} + \mathbf {y}^\top (\mathbf {I} - \mathbf {H})\mathbf {y} = SS_R + SS_{\mathrm {Res}} , \] using the forms of \(SS_R\) and \(SS_{\mathrm {Res}}\) found in the proofs of Theorems 3.8.1 and 3.6.1.

Problem 3.13.6. Continue with the designed experiment of Problem 3.13.1, assuming A1–A4.

(a)
Set out the ANOVA table and test significance of regression at the 5% level.
(b)
Test each of \(\beta _1\) and \(\beta _2\) separately at the 5% level.
(c)
Find \(R^2\) and \(R^2_{\mathrm {adj}}\).
(d)
Find \(SS_R(\beta _2 \mid \beta _1)\) and \(SS_R(\beta _1 \mid \beta _2)\). Why, unlike in the crop data, does the order not matter?
(e)
At \(x_1 = x_2 = 0.5\), give a 95% confidence interval for the mean response and a 95% prediction interval for a new observation.

Show solution

Solution. From Problem 3.13.1: \(\hat y = 29 + 5.5x_1 + 3.5x_2\), \(SS_{\mathrm {Res}} = 6\), \(\hat \sigma ^2 = 2\) with \(3\) degrees of freedom, \(\mathbf {C} = \operatorname {diag}(1/6, 1/4, 1/4)\), \(\mathbf {y}^\top \mathbf {y} = 5222\) and \(\hat {\boldsymbol \beta }^\top \mathbf {X}^\top \mathbf {y} = 5216\).

(a) \((\sum y_i)^2/n = 174^2/6 = 5046\), so \(SS_T = 5222 - 5046 = 176\) and \(SS_R = 5216 - 5046 = 170\).

Residual 6 3 2
Total 176 5

\(42.5 > F_{0.05,2,3} = 9.55\): reject \(H_0\colon \beta _1 = \beta _2 = 0\).

(b) \(t_1 = 5.5/0.7071 = 7.78\) and \(t_2 = 3.5/0.7071 = 4.95\). Both exceed \(t_{0.025,3} = 3.182\), so both regressors are significant.

(c) \(R^2 = 170/176 = 0.966\), and \(R^2_{\mathrm {adj}} = 1 - \dfrac {2}{176/5} = 1 - 0.0568 = 0.943\).

(d) For the model with \(x_1\) alone, \(\mathbf {X}_1\) has orthogonal columns too, and \(\hat \beta _1 = 22/4 = 5.5\) as before, so \(SS_R(\beta _1) = \hat \beta _1 \sum x_{i1}y_i = 5.5 \times 22 = 121\). Then \(SS_R(\beta _2 \mid \beta _1) = 170 - 121 = 49\). In the same way \(SS_R(\beta _2) = 3.5 \times 14 = 49\), so \(SS_R(\beta _1 \mid \beta _2) = 170 - 49 = 121\). Each regressor contributes the same amount whatever else is in the model, because with orthogonal columns the estimate of one coefficient does not change when another regressor is added or removed. In the crop data, fertiliser and rainfall are correlated, and they share credit for the yield.

(e) \(\mathbf {x}_0 = (1, 0.5, 0.5)^\top \), \(\hat y_0 = 29 + 2.75 + 1.75 = 33.5\), and \(\mathbf {x}_0^\top \mathbf {C}\mathbf {x}_0 = \frac 16 + \frac {0.25}{4} + \frac {0.25}{4} = 0.2917\).

  • Mean: \(33.5 \pm 3.182\sqrt {2 \times 0.2917} = 33.5 \pm 2.431\), that is, \((31.07,\ 35.93)\).
  • New observation: \(33.5 \pm 3.182\sqrt {2 \times 1.2917} = 33.5 \pm 5.115\), that is, \((28.38,\ 38.62)\).

Problem 3.13.7. For the crop data, the supplier of the fertiliser claims that each extra 100 kg per hectare raises the mean yield by more than \(1.5\) tonnes per hectare, at fixed rainfall. Test the claim at the 5% level.

Show solution

Solution. \(H_0\colon \beta _1 = 1.5\) against \(H_1\colon \beta _1 > 1.5\). \(t_0 = (2 - 1.5)/0.3412 = 1.466\). The test is one-sided with \(3\) degrees of freedom, so the critical value is \(t_{0.05,3} = 2.353\). As \(1.466 < 2.353\), do not reject \(H_0\): six plots are not enough to establish the claim, although the estimate \(\hat \beta _1 = 2\) is in its favour.

Problem 3.13.8.

(a)
Show that \(R^2_{\mathrm {adj}} = 1 - \dfrac {n-1}{n-p}\,(1 - R^2)\), and deduce that \(R^2_{\mathrm {adj}} \le R^2\), and that \(R^2_{\mathrm {adj}}\) can be negative.
(b)
For the crop data, compare \(R^2\) and \(R^2_{\mathrm {adj}}\) for the model with \(x_1\) alone and the model with both regressors.

Show solution

Solution. (a) \(R^2_{\mathrm {adj}} = 1 - \dfrac {SS_{\mathrm {Res}}/(n-p)}{SS_T/(n-1)} = 1 - \dfrac {n-1}{n-p}\cdot \dfrac {SS_{\mathrm {Res}}}{SS_T}\) and \(SS_{\mathrm {Res}}/SS_T = 1 - R^2\). Since \(\frac {n-1}{n-p} \ge 1\), we subtract at least \(1 - R^2\) from \(1\), so \(R^2_{\mathrm {adj}} \le R^2\). With \(n = 6\), \(p = 3\) and \(R^2 = 0.1\), \(R^2_{\mathrm {adj}} = 1 - \frac 53 \times 0.9 = -0.5\): a weak model with many parameters scores below zero.

(b) With \(x_1\) alone (Example 3.11.3), \(SS_{\mathrm {Res}} = 7.9375\) on \(4\) degrees of freedom, so \(R^2 = 85.5625/93.5 = 0.915\) and \(R^2_{\mathrm {adj}} = 1 - \frac 54(0.0849) = 0.894\). With both, \(R^2 = 0.957\) and \(R^2_{\mathrm {adj}} = 0.929\). Both measures prefer the larger model here, even though the \(t\)-test did not find \(x_2\) significant.

In fact \(R^2_{\mathrm {adj}}\) rises exactly when the partial \(F\) statistic for the added term exceeds \(1\). Adding one term to a model with \(p\) parameters takes \(SS_{\mathrm {Res}}\) from \(S_0\) to \(S_1\), and \(R^2_{\mathrm {adj}}\) rises exactly when the residual mean square falls: \begin {align*} \frac {S_1}{n-p-1} < \frac {S_0}{n-p} &\iff (n-p)S_1 < (n-p-1)S_0 \\ &\iff S_1 < (n-p-1)(S_0 - S_1) \\ &\iff F_0 = \frac {S_0 - S_1}{S_1/(n-p-1)} > 1 . \end {align*}

Here \(F_0 = 2.95 > 1\). That is a much weaker requirement than significance, which needed \(F_0 > 10.13\).
