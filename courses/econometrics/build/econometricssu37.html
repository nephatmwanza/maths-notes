6.2 Least squares

Theorem 6.2.1. Under (A4), the residual sum of squares \(S(b) = (Y - Xb)'(Y - Xb)\) has a unique minimum at \[ b = (X'X)^{-1}X'Y , \] the solution of the normal equations \(X'Xb = X'Y\).

Proof. Expand \(S(b) = Y'Y - 2b'X'Y + b'X'Xb\), using \(Y'Xb = b'X'Y\), which is a scalar. By Lemma 5.1.8, \[ \frac {\partial S}{\partial b} = -2X'Y + 2X'Xb , \] which is zero exactly when \(X'Xb = X'Y\). The matrix \(X'X\) is psd, since \(c'X'Xc = (Xc)'(Xc) \geq 0\), and \(Xc = 0\) only for \(c = 0\) because the columns of \(X\) are linearly independent. So \(X'X\) is pd and therefore invertible, giving the unique solution \(b = (X'X)^{-1}X'Y\). The Hessian \(2X'X\) is pd, so \(S\) is strictly convex and the stationary point is the global minimum. A direct argument: for any \(\tilde b\), \begin {align*} S(\tilde b) &= \bigl (e + X(b - \tilde b)\bigr )'\bigl (e + X(b - \tilde b)\bigr )\\ &= S(b) + (b - \tilde b)'X'X(b - \tilde b) \geq S(b) , \end {align*}

where \(e = Y - Xb\) and the cross terms vanish because \(X'e = X'Y - X'Xb = 0\). □

With \(P = X(X'X)^{-1}X'\) and \(M = I - P\) as in Example 5.1.7, the fitted values and residuals are \[ \hat Y = Xb = PY, \qquad e = Y - \hat Y = MY = MU . \]

Theorem 6.2.2. The least squares residuals satisfy \(X'e = 0\): they are orthogonal to every regressor. Consequently \(\hat Y'e = 0\), and if the model contains an intercept, \(\iota 'e = \sum e_i = 0\), the fitted plane passes through the point of means, and \(\bar {\hat Y} = \bar Y\).

Proof. \(X'e = X'MY = (MX)'Y = 0\). Then \(\hat Y'e = b'X'e = 0\). If \(\iota \) is the first column of \(X\), the first row of \(X'e = 0\) is \(\iota 'e = 0\). Dividing \(\iota 'Y = \iota 'Xb + \iota 'e = \iota 'Xb\) by \(n\) gives \(\bar Y = b_1 + b_2\bar X_2 + \dots + b_k\bar X_k\). □

Example 6.2.3. Five observations on \(Y\) and two regressors:

\(X_2\) 1 2 3 4 5
\(X_3\) 1 3 2 5 4
\(Y\) 3 6 7 11 13

The means are \(\bar X_2 = \bar X_3 = 3\) and \(\bar Y = 8\). In deviations, \(x_2 = (-2, -1, 0, 1, 2)\), \(x_3 = (-2, 0, -1, 2, 1)\) and \(y = (-5, -2, -1, 3, 5)\), and \begin {gather*} \sum x_2^2 = 10, \quad \sum x_3^2 = 10, \quad \sum x_2x_3 = 8,\\ \sum x_2y = 25, \quad \sum x_3y = 22, \quad \sum y^2 = 64 . \end {gather*} The slope coefficients solve the normal equations in deviation form (Theorem 6.4.2 below): \[ \begin {pmatrix} 10 & 8 \\ 8 & 10 \end {pmatrix}\begin {pmatrix} b_2 \\ b_3 \end {pmatrix} = \begin {pmatrix} 25 \\ 22 \end {pmatrix} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \begin {pmatrix} b_2 \\ b_3 \end {pmatrix} = \frac {1}{36}\begin {pmatrix} 10 & -8 \\ -8 & 10 \end {pmatrix}\begin {pmatrix} 25 \\ 22 \end {pmatrix} = \begin {pmatrix} 74/36 \\ 20/36 \end {pmatrix} . \] So \(b_2 = 2.0556\), \(b_3 = 0.5556\) and \(b_1 = \bar Y - b_2\bar X_2 - b_3\bar X_3 = 8 - 3(2.6111) = 0.1667\). Notice that \(X_3\) alone is strongly related to \(Y\) (\(\sum x_3y = 22\), nearly as large as \(\sum x_2y\)), yet its coefficient is small once \(X_2\) is in the equation, because \(X_2\) and \(X_3\) are themselves highly correlated. Multiple regression credits each variable only with what it adds beyond the others.
