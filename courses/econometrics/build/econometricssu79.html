11.8 Practice Problems

Problem 11.8.1. A regression with an intercept and two other regressors is fitted to \(n = 20\) annual observations. The residuals give \(\sum e_t^2 = 48.6\), \(\sum _{t=2}^{20}(e_t - e_{t-1})^2 = 42.3\), \(e_1 = 1.2\) and \(e_{20} = -2.1\).

(a)
Compute \(d\) and the first-order residual autocorrelation \(\hat r\), and check the identity of Theorem 11.3.2(i).
(b)
With \(d_L = 1.10\) and \(d_U = 1.54\) at the \(5\%\) level, test for positive first-order autocorrelation.

Show solution

Solution. (a) \(d = 42.3/48.6 = 0.870\). From the expansion in the proof of Theorem 11.3.2, \begin {align*} 2\sum e_te_{t-1} &= 2\sum e_t^2 - e_1^2 - e_n^2 - \sum (e_t - e_{t-1})^2\\ &= 97.2 - 1.44 - 4.41 - 42.3 = 49.05 , \end {align*}

so \(\sum e_te_{t-1} = 24.525\) and \(\hat r = 24.525/48.6 = 0.505\). Then \[ 2(1 - \hat r) - \frac {e_1^2 + e_n^2}{\sum e_t^2} = 0.991 - \frac {5.85}{48.6} = 0.991 - 0.120 = 0.870 = d . \] The end correction is not negligible with \(n = 20\).

(b) \(d = 0.870 < d_L = 1.10\), so the hypothesis of no autocorrelation is rejected in favour of positive autocorrelation.

Problem 11.8.2. For \(n = 3\) and AR(1) disturbances, write out \(\Omega \) from (11.1) and \(P\) from Theorem 11.1.2, and verify directly that \(P'P\,\Omega = I\).

Show solution

Solution. \[ \Omega = \frac {1}{1 - \rho ^2}\begin {bmatrix} 1 & \rho & \rho ^2\\ \rho & 1 & \rho \\ \rho ^2 & \rho & 1\end {bmatrix}, \] \[ P'P = \begin {bmatrix} 1 & -\rho & 0\\ -\rho & 1 + \rho ^2 & -\rho \\ 0 & -\rho & 1\end {bmatrix} . \] The first row of \(P'P\) times the columns of \((1 - \rho ^2)\Omega \) gives \[ (1 - \rho ^2,\ \rho - \rho ,\ \rho ^2 - \rho ^2) = (1 - \rho ^2, 0, 0) . \] The second row gives \begin {align*} &(-\rho + \rho + \rho ^3 - \rho ^3,\ -\rho ^2 + 1 + \rho ^2 - \rho ^2,\ -\rho ^3 + \rho + \rho ^3 - \rho )\\ &\qquad = (0, 1 - \rho ^2, 0), \end {align*}

and the third row, by symmetry, \((0, 0, 1 - \rho ^2)\). Dividing by \(1 - \rho ^2\) gives \(I\).

Problem 11.8.3. A consumption function \(C_t = \alpha + \beta Y_t + \gamma C_{t-1} + u_t\) is estimated from \(n = 50\) quarterly observations. The Durbin–Watson statistic is \(1.62\) and the standard error of \(\hat \gamma \) is \(0.09\).

(a)
Why is the Durbin–Watson bounds test inappropriate here?
(b)
Compute Durbin’s \(h\) and test for autocorrelation at the \(5\%\) level.
(c)
What would you do if the standard error of \(\hat \gamma \) were \(0.15\)?

Show solution

Solution. (a) The regressors include the lagged dependent variable, so \(d\) is biased towards \(2\) (Theorem 11.2.2) and the bounds do not apply.

(b) \(\hat r \approx 1 - d/2 = 0.19\) and \(n\widehat \Var (\hat \gamma ) = 50(0.0081) = 0.405\), so \(h = 0.19\sqrt {50/0.595} = 1.74\). Against the two-sided \(5\%\) point \(1.96\) this is not significant, but against the one-sided alternative \(\rho > 0\), with critical value \(1.645\), it is. The evidence is borderline.

(c) Then \(n\widehat \Var (\hat \gamma ) = 50(0.0225) = 1.125 > 1\), and \(h\) cannot be computed. Use the Breusch–Godfrey test: regress the residuals on a constant, \(Y_t\), \(C_{t-1}\) and \(e_{t-1}\), and refer \(nR^2\) to \(\chi ^2_1\).

Problem 11.8.4. Let the disturbances follow the MA(1) scheme \(u_t = \varepsilon _t + \theta \varepsilon _{t-1}\), with \(\varepsilon _t\) independent with variance \(\sigma _\varepsilon ^2\).

(a)
Find \(\Var (u_t)\) and \(\Corr (u_t, u_{t-s})\) for \(s \geq 1\), and describe the matrix \(\Omega \).
(b)
Show that the first-order autocorrelation cannot exceed \(\frac 12\) in absolute value. What does this imply about the probability limit of \(d\) when the regressors are exogenous?

Show solution

Solution. (a) \(\Var (u_t) = \sigma _\varepsilon ^2(1 + \theta ^2)\). \(\Cov (u_t, u_{t-1}) = E\bigl ((\varepsilon _t + \theta \varepsilon _{t-1})(\varepsilon _{t-1} + \theta \varepsilon _{t-2})\bigr ) = \theta \sigma _\varepsilon ^2\), and the covariance is zero for \(s \geq 2\), because \(u_t\) and \(u_{t-s}\) then share no \(\varepsilon \). So \(\Corr (u_t, u_{t-1}) = \theta /(1 + \theta ^2)\), and \(\Omega \) is tridiagonal: \(1 + \theta ^2\) on the diagonal and \(\theta \) next to it. Its inverse, unlike that of the AR(1) matrix, is full.

(b) \(1 + \theta ^2 - 2|\theta | = (1 - |\theta |)^2 \geq 0\), so \(|\theta |/(1 + \theta ^2) \leq \frac 12\), with equality at \(\theta = \pm 1\). With exogenous regressors \(\hat r\) converges to \(\theta /(1 + \theta ^2)\), so \(\plim d = 2\bigl (1 - \theta /(1 + \theta ^2)\bigr )\) lies between \(1\) and \(3\). A value of \(d\) well below \(1\) in a large sample therefore points to an autoregressive rather than a first-order moving average scheme.

Problem 11.8.5. The ARCH(1) model \(u_t = \varepsilon _t\sqrt {h_t}\), \(h_t = 0.5 + 0.4u_{t-1}^2\) is fitted to daily returns.

(a)
Find the unconditional variance and kurtosis of \(u_t\).
(b)
Yesterday’s return was \(u_{t-1} = 3\). Find the conditional variance of today’s return, and the probability, under the model, that today’s return exceeds \(3\) in absolute value. Compare with the unconditional normal approximation.
(c)
For what values of \(\alpha _1\) does the fourth moment exist?

Show solution

Solution. (a) \(\sigma ^2 = 0.5/(1 - 0.4) = 0.833\); kurtosis \(3(1 - 0.16)/(1 - 0.48) = 2.52/0.52 = 4.85\).

(b) \(h_t = 0.5 + 0.4(9) = 4.1\), a conditional standard deviation of \(2.025\). Given yesterday, \(u_t \sim N(0, 4.1)\), so \[ P(|u_t| > 3) = 2\bigl (1 - \Phi (3/2.025)\bigr ) = 2\bigl (1 - \Phi (1.48)\bigr ) = 0.139 . \] A normal distribution with the unconditional variance would give \[ 2\bigl (1 - \Phi (3/0.913)\bigr ) = 2\bigl (1 - \Phi (3.29)\bigr ) = 0.001 . \] After a large shock, another large move is a hundred times more likely than the unconditional model says.

(c) \(3\alpha _1^2 < 1\), that is \(0 \leq \alpha _1 < 1/\sqrt 3 = 0.577\). For \(0.577 \leq \alpha _1 < 1\) the variance is finite but the kurtosis is infinite.

Problem 11.8.6. The model \(Y_t = \beta _1 + \beta _2X_t + u_t\) with \(u_t = \rho u_{t-1} + \varepsilon _t\) is to be compared with the unrestricted dynamic model \(Y_t = \delta _0 + \delta _1Y_{t-1} + \delta _2X_t + \delta _3X_{t-1} + \varepsilon _t\).

(a)
Express the \(\delta \)s in terms of \(\beta _1\), \(\beta _2\) and \(\rho \), and find the restriction that the AR(1) error model imposes.
(b)
The unrestricted model, estimated by least squares from \(n = 80\) observations, gives \(\text {RSS}_U = 12.4\); the AR(1) error model, estimated by maximum likelihood, gives \(\text {RSS}_R = 13.9\). Test the restriction.

Show solution

Solution. (a) By Theorem 11.4.2, \(\delta _0 = \beta _1(1 - \rho )\), \(\delta _1 = \rho \), \(\delta _2 = \beta _2\) and \(\delta _3 = -\rho \beta _2\). The four \(\delta \)s are functions of three parameters, and the restriction is the common factor restriction \(\delta _3 = -\delta _1\delta _2\), that is \(\delta _3 + \delta _1\delta _2 = 0\).

(b) As in Theorem 9.3.1, \[ \text {LR} = n\log \frac {\text {RSS}_R}{\text {RSS}_U} = 80\log \frac {13.9}{12.4} = 80(0.1142) = 9.14 , \] compared with \(\chi ^2_1\), whose \(5\%\) point is \(3.84\) (and \(1\%\) point \(6.63\)). The common factor restriction is rejected: the dynamics are not those of a static relation with AR(1) disturbances, and the dynamic model should be kept.

Problem 11.8.7. For a single regressor, write \(g_t = e_tx_t\) and \(\hat \gamma _j = \sum _{t=j+1}^ng_tg_{t-j}\).

(a)
Show that the unweighted estimate \(\hat \gamma _0 + 2\hat \gamma _1\) can be negative, by taking \(n = 3\) and \(g = (1, -1, 1)\).
(b)
Show that the Newey–West estimate with \(L = 1\), \(\hat \gamma _0 + \hat \gamma _1\), equals \(\frac 12\sum _t(g_t + g_{t+1})^2\) with \(g_0 = g_{n+1} = 0\), and is therefore never negative.

Show solution

Solution. (a) \(\hat \gamma _0 = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3\) and \(\hat \gamma _1 = g_2g_1 + g_3g_2 = -1 - 1 = -2\), so \(\hat \gamma _0 + 2\hat \gamma _1 = -1 < 0\): a negative “variance”.

(b) Summing over \(t = 0, \dots , n\), \[ \sum _t(g_t + g_{t+1})^2 = \sum _tg_t^2 + \sum _tg_{t+1}^2 + 2\sum _tg_tg_{t+1} = 2\hat \gamma _0 + 2\hat \gamma _1 , \] since each \(g_t^2\) appears once in each of the first two sums. Hence \(\hat \gamma _0 + \hat \gamma _1 = \frac 12\sum _t(g_t + g_{t+1})^2 \geq 0\). This is the case \(L = 1\) of the proof of Theorem 11.4.3, where the weight on \(\hat \gamma _1\) is \(1 - \frac 12 = \frac 12\), applied to both \(\hat \gamma _1\) and its transpose.
