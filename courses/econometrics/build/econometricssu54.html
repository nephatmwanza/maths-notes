8.3 The Chow test of structural change

When both sets are large enough to estimate the model separately (\(n_1 > k\) and \(n_2 > k\)), a sharper question can be asked: are the coefficients the same in both? The unrestricted model allows different coefficients, \[ Y_{(1)} = X_{(1)}\beta _{(1)} + U_{(1)}, \qquad Y_{(2)} = X_{(2)}\beta _{(2)} + U_{(2)} , \] and \(H_0\colon \beta _{(1)} = \beta _{(2)}\) imposes \(k\) restrictions.

Theorem 8.3.1 (Chow test). If the disturbances are \(N(0, \sigma ^2I_n)\) in both sets, with the same \(\sigma ^2\), then under \(H_0\) \[ F = \frac {\bigl (\text {RSS} - (\text {RSS}_1 + \text {RSS}_2)\bigr )/k}{(\text {RSS}_1 + \text {RSS}_2)/(n - 2k)} \sim F_{k,\ n-2k} , \] where \(\text {RSS}_1\) and \(\text {RSS}_2\) come from the separate regressions and RSS from the pooled regression.

Proof. Stack the unrestricted model as one regression with the block-diagonal regressor matrix \(\begin {pmatrix} X_{(1)} & 0 \\ 0 & X_{(2)} \end {pmatrix}\) and \(2k\) coefficients. Its normal equations separate into those of the two subsamples, so its residual sum of squares is \(\text {RSS}_1 + \text {RSS}_2\) with \(n - 2k\) degrees of freedom. The restricted model, with \(\beta _{(1)} = \beta _{(2)}\), is the pooled regression. Apply Theorem 7.4.1 with \(q = k\). □

The equal-variance assumption is essential and easily overlooked: the denominator pools the two residual variances. If the disturbance variance also changed between the periods, the test’s size is wrong. A first check compares \(s_1^2 = \text {RSS}_1/(n_1 - k)\) with \(s_2^2 = \text {RSS}_2/(n_2 - k)\) by their ratio, which is \(F_{n_2-k,\,n_1-k}\) under equal variances (the Goldfeld–Quandt test of the section on heteroscedasticity).

Example 8.3.2. Per capita food consumption \(q\) was estimated by \[ \log q = \alpha + \beta _1\log p + \beta _2\log Y + u , \] using annual averages for two periods of fifteen years each, before and after a wave of privatisation of state-owned companies. The residual sums of squares are

Period \(n\) RSS
Before privatisation 15 0.1151
After privatisation 15 0.0544
Both combined 30 0.2866

Did the coefficients remain stable after privatisation? Use the \(5\%\) level.

Solution. With \(k = 3\) and \(n = 30\), \(\text {RSS}_1 + \text {RSS}_2 = 0.1695\) and \[ F = \frac {(0.2866 - 0.1695)/3}{0.1695/24} = \frac {0.03903}{0.007063} = 5.53 . \] The \(5\%\) point of \(F_{3,24}\) is \(3.01\), so stability is rejected (\(p = 0.005\)). The demand relation shifted between the two periods.

The equal-variance assumption is plausible here: \(s_1^2 = 0.1151/12 = 0.00959\) and \(s_2^2 = 0.0544/12 = 0.00453\), a ratio of \(2.12\), below the \(5\%\) point of \(F_{12,12}\), which is \(2.69\). The test cannot say which coefficients changed, the intercept, the price elasticity or the income elasticity. Dummy variables answer that.
