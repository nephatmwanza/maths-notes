6.3 The decomposition of the sum of squares and \(R^2\)

Theorem 6.3.1. If the model contains an intercept, then with \(M_0\) the centring matrix, \[ \underbrace {Y'M_0Y}_{\text {TSS}} = \underbrace {\hat Y'M_0\hat Y}_{\text {ESS}} + \underbrace {e'e}_{\text {RSS}}, \qquad \text {ESS} = b'X'Y - n\bar Y^2 . \]

Proof. \(Y = \hat Y + e\), so \(M_0Y = M_0\hat Y + M_0e = M_0\hat Y + e\), because \(\sum e_i = 0\) gives \(M_0e = e\). Then \[ Y'M_0Y = (M_0Y)'(M_0Y) = \hat Y'M_0\hat Y + 2\hat Y'M_0e + e'e , \] and \(\hat Y'M_0e = \hat Y'e = 0\) by Theorem 6.2.2. For the second formula, \(\hat Y'M_0\hat Y = \hat Y'\hat Y - n\bar Y^2\) since \(\bar {\hat Y} = \bar Y\), and \(\hat Y'\hat Y = b'X'Xb = b'X'Y\) by the normal equations. □

As before, \(R^2 = \text {ESS}/\text {TSS} = 1 - \text {RSS}/\text {TSS}\). Without an intercept, \(M_0e \neq e\) in general, the decomposition fails, and \(1 - \text {RSS}/\text {TSS}\) can be negative (compare Problem 2.7.4).

Theorem 6.3.2. Adding a regressor to a model never reduces \(R^2\): if the regressors of model 1 are a subset of those of model 2, both with an intercept and the same dependent variable, then \(R_2^2 \geq R_1^2\).

Proof. Model 2 minimises the residual sum of squares over a set of coefficient vectors that includes every fit available to model 1, namely those with the extra coefficients set to zero. Its minimum can be no larger: \(\text {RSS}_2 \leq \text {RSS}_1\). Both have the same TSS, so \(R_2^2 = 1 - \text {RSS}_2/\text {TSS} \geq R_1^2\). □

So a reported \(R^2_{1\cdot 23} = 0.87\) together with \(R^2_{1\cdot 234} = 0.82\), for the same data, is impossible, whatever the variables. The other side of the coin is that \(R^2\) rewards adding variables whether or not they matter: even a column of random numbers raises it (Software 6.7.3). The adjusted \(R^2\) corrects each sum of squares for its degrees of freedom, \[ \bar R^2 = 1 - \frac {\text {RSS}/(n - k)}{\text {TSS}/(n - 1)} = 1 - (1 - R^2)\frac {n - 1}{n - k} , \] and can fall when a regressor is added. It rises precisely when the added variable’s \(t\) ratio exceeds one in absolute value (Problem 6.8.2). \(\bar R^2\) can be negative, and it is a crude device. Its proper successors are the formal tests and information criteria introduced later.
