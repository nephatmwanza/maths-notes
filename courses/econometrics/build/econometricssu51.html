7.8 Practice Problems

Problem 7.8.1. Consider \(Y = X\beta + U\) with \(k - 1\) regressors, where the elements of \(U\) are independent and identically distributed with mean \(0\) and variance \(\sigma ^2\).

(i)
Let \(c\) be a known \(k\)-vector and \(a\) a known \(n\)-vector. Find the condition on \(a\) and \(c\) for \(a'Y\) to be an unbiased estimator of \(c'\beta \).
(ii)
Find \(\Var (a'Y)\).
(iii)
Find the vector \(a\) that minimises \(a'a\) subject to \(X'a = c\).

Show solution

Solution. This is Theorem 7.2.1. (i) \(E(a'Y) = a'X\beta = c'\beta \) for all \(\beta \) requires \(X'a = c\). (ii) \(\sigma ^2a'a\). (iii) The Lagrangian \(a'a - 2\lambda '(X'a - c)\) gives \(a = X\lambda \); then \(X'X\lambda = c\), so \(a = X(X'X)^{-1}c\). The resulting estimator is \(a'Y = c'(X'X)^{-1}X'Y = c'b\), so the minimum variance linear unbiased estimator of \(c'\beta \) is \(c'b\), with variance \(\sigma ^2c'(X'X)^{-1}c\).

Problem 7.8.2. In the model \(Y = X\beta + U\) with \(U \sim N(0, \sigma ^2I)\), \(\beta \) a \(k\)-vector and \(b = (X'X)^{-1}X'Y\):

(i)
find \(E(b)\) and \(\Cov (b)\);
(ii)
determine the distributions of \(Y\) and of \(b\);
(iii)
for a known \(q \times k\) matrix \(R\) of rank \(q\) and \(q\)-vector \(r\), find \(\Var (Rb - r)\) and the distribution of \(Rb - r\);
(iv)
show that under \(H_0\colon R\beta = r\), \((Rb - r)'\bigl [\sigma ^2R(X'X)^{-1}R'\bigr ]^{-1}(Rb - r)\) has a \(\chi ^2\) distribution with \(q\) degrees of freedom.

Show solution

Solution. (i) \(E(b) = \beta \) and \(\Cov (b) = \sigma ^2(X'X)^{-1}\) (Theorem 7.1.1). (ii) \(Y \sim N(X\beta , \sigma ^2I)\) and \(b \sim N(\beta , \sigma ^2(X'X)^{-1})\), since both are linear in \(U\). (iii) \(\Var (Rb - r) = R\Var (b)R' = \sigma ^2R(X'X)^{-1}R'\), and \(Rb - r \sim N(R\beta - r, \sigma ^2R(X'X)^{-1}R')\). (iv) Under \(H_0\) the mean is zero, and a \(N(0, \Sigma )\) vector \(z\) with \(\Sigma \) pd has \(z'\Sigma ^{-1}z \sim \chi ^2_q\) (Theorem 5.4.2); here \(\Sigma = \sigma ^2R(X'X)^{-1}R'\) is pd because \(R\) has full row rank.

Problem 7.8.3. Consider a regression with \(k - 1\) regressors for which all the classical assumptions hold. We wish to test \(H_0\colon \beta _2 = \dots = \beta _k = 0\) against the alternative that at least one slope is non-zero.

(i)
Derive the test statistic, using the restricted and unrestricted regressions.
(ii)
State its distribution and degrees of freedom.

Show solution

Solution. See Theorem 7.4.2. The unrestricted regression has \(\text {RSS}_U = e'e\) on \(n - k\) degrees of freedom. The restricted model is \(Y_i = \beta _1 + u_i\), fitted by \(\bar Y\), with \(\text {RSS}_R = \sum (Y_i - \bar Y)^2\). There are \(q = k - 1\) restrictions, so \[ F = \frac {(\text {RSS}_R - \text {RSS}_U)/(k - 1)}{\text {RSS}_U/(n - k)} = \frac {R^2/(k - 1)}{(1 - R^2)/(n - k)}, \] which under \(H_0\) has the \(F\) distribution with \(k - 1\) and \(n - k\) degrees of freedom. The independence of numerator and denominator comes from the independence of \(b\) and \(e'e\) (Theorem 5.5.1). Reject for large \(F\).

Problem 7.8.4. In the production function of Example 7.5.1, the estimated covariance of \(b_L\) and \(b_K\) is \(-0.0061\).

(a)
Test the hypothesis of constant returns to scale at the \(5\%\) level.
(b)
Repeat the calculation ignoring the covariance, and explain why the two answers differ.
(c)
Describe how the restricted model would be estimated, and how the test could then be carried out with residual sums of squares.

Show solution

Solution. (a) \(H_0\colon \beta _L + \beta _K = 1\). \(b_L + b_K = 0.9\), and \[ \widehat \Var (b_L + b_K) = 0.102^2 + 0.102^2 + 2(-0.0061) = 0.0208 - 0.0122 = 0.0086 , \] so the standard error is \(0.0928\) and \(t = (0.9 - 1)/0.0928 = -1.08\). Since \(|t| < 2.026\), constant returns are not rejected.

(b) Ignoring the covariance gives a standard error of \(\sqrt {0.0208} = 0.144\) and \(t = -0.69\). The conclusion is the same here, but the standard error is \(55\%\) too large. The covariance is negative because labour and capital move together in the data. An overestimate of one elasticity tends to come with an underestimate of the other, so their sum is estimated more precisely than either alone. Ignoring a negative covariance makes a test of the sum too conservative; ignoring a positive covariance would make it too liberal.

(c) Substitute \(\beta _K = 1 - \beta _L\): \(\log Q - \log K = \alpha + \beta _L(\log L - \log K) + u\), so regress \(\log (Q/K)\) on \(\log (L/K)\). Its residual sum of squares is \(\text {RSS}_R\), and \(F = (\text {RSS}_R - \text {RSS}_U)/(\text {RSS}_U/37)\) with \((1, 37)\) degrees of freedom; this \(F\) equals \(t^2\) from (a).

Problem 7.8.5. In a regression with an intercept and two regressors, show that \[ \Corr (b_2, b_3) = -r_{23}, \] where \(r_{23}\) is the sample correlation of \(X_2\) and \(X_3\). Explain why the estimates in Software 7.7.2 are positively correlated although pop15 and pop75 are negatively correlated, and why this result does not apply exactly there.

Show solution

Solution. By Corollary 6.4.2, the slope estimates come from the deviation form, so their covariance matrix is \(\sigma ^2\) times the inverse of \(\begin {pmatrix} S_{22} & S_{23} \\ S_{23} & S_{33} \end {pmatrix}\), which is \(\frac {1}{D}\begin {pmatrix} S_{33} & -S_{23} \\ -S_{23} & S_{22} \end {pmatrix}\) with \(D = S_{22}S_{33} - S_{23}^2\). Hence \[ \Corr (b_2, b_3) = \frac {-S_{23}}{\sqrt {S_{33}S_{22}}} = -r_{23} . \] Positively correlated regressors have negatively correlated estimates, and vice versa: if the two regressors move together, the data can pin down their combined effect but not how to divide it, so an overestimate of one coefficient goes with an underestimate of the other. pop15 and pop75 are correlated \(-0.91\), so their estimates are positively correlated. In Software 7.7.2 the correlation is \(0.76\) rather than \(0.91\) because the model has two further regressors. The relevant correlation is then the partial correlation of pop15 and pop75 given dpi and ddpi, with the sign reversed.

Problem 7.8.6. Show that in a regression with an intercept, \[ \Var (b_j) = \frac {\sigma ^2}{\sum _i (X_{ji} - \bar X_j)^2\,(1 - R_j^2)} , \] where \(R_j^2\) is the coefficient of determination from regressing \(X_j\) on all the other regressors. Interpret the factor \(1/(1 - R_j^2)\).

Show solution

Solution. By Theorem 6.4.1, \(b_j = \tilde x'Y/\tilde x'\tilde x\), where \(\tilde x = M_1X_j\) is the residual vector from regressing \(X_j\) on the other regressors (including the intercept). Then \(b_j = \beta _j + \tilde x'U/\tilde x'\tilde x\) and \(\Var (b_j) = \sigma ^2/\tilde x'\tilde x\). But \(\tilde x'\tilde x\) is the residual sum of squares of that auxiliary regression, which is \((1 - R_j^2)\) times its total sum of squares \(\sum (X_{ji} - \bar X_j)^2\). The factor \(1/(1 - R_j^2)\) is the variance inflation factor: it is \(1\) when \(X_j\) is uncorrelated with the other regressors, and it grows without bound as \(X_j\) becomes nearly a linear combination of them. That is the multicollinearity problem, quantified.

Problem 7.8.7. For the savings regression of Software 6.7.1, a country has pop15 \(= 35\), pop75 \(= 2.5\), dpi \(= 1000\) and ddpi \(= 4\). Compute the point prediction of its savings rate. Given that \(x_0'(X'X)^{-1}x_0 = 0.0249\), give a \(95\%\) prediction interval.

Show solution

Solution. \(\hat Y_0 = 28.566 - 0.4612(35) - 1.6915(2.5) - 0.000337(1000) + 0.4097(4) = 28.566 - 16.142 - 4.229 - 0.337 + 1.639 = 9.497\). The standard error of prediction is \(s\sqrt {1 + 0.0249} = 3.803(1.0124) = 3.850\), and with \(t_{0.025,45} = 2.014\) the interval is \(9.50 \pm 7.75 = (1.74,\ 17.25)\), which is what predict(fit, new, interval = "prediction") returns. Almost all of the width comes from the disturbance variance, the \(1\) in \(1 + x_0'(X'X)^{-1}x_0\): the regression line is estimated far more precisely than an individual country’s savings rate can be predicted.
