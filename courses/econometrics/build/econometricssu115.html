16.8 Practice Problems

Problem 16.8.1. Three firms are observed in two years:

\(x_{i1}\) \(x_{i2}\) \(y_{i1}\) \(y_{i2}\)
Firm 1 \(1\) \(2\) \(2\) \(3\)
Firm 2 \(2\) \(4\) \(3\) \(6\)
Firm 3 \(3\) \(3\) \(5\) \(5\)

Compute the first-difference estimate of \(\beta \) in \(y_{it} = \beta x_{it} + \alpha _i + \varepsilon _{it}\), and confirm that the within estimate is the same.

Show solution

Solution. The differences are \(\Delta x = (1, 2, 0)\) and \(\Delta y = (1, 3, 0)\), so \(b_{\text {FD}} = (1 + 6 + 0)/(1 + 4 + 0) = 7/5 = 1.4\). For the within estimator, the deviations from firm means are \(\pm \frac 12\Delta x\) and \(\pm \frac 12\Delta y\), so \(\sum \tilde x\tilde y = 2 \times \frac 14(1 + 6 + 0) = 3.5\) and \(\sum \tilde x^2 = 2 \times \frac 14(1 + 4 + 0) = 2.5\), and \(b_{\text {FE}} = 3.5/2.5 = 1.4\). Firm 3, whose \(x\) did not change, contributes nothing to either estimate.

Problem 16.8.2. In a random effects model, \(\sigma _\varepsilon ^2 = 1\) and \(\sigma _\alpha ^2 = 0.5\).

(a)
Find the intraclass correlation and \(\theta \) for \(T = 4\) and \(T = 20\).
(b)
Explain why random effects moves towards fixed effects as \(T\) increases.

Show solution

Solution. (a) The intraclass correlation is \(0.5/1.5 = 0.33\). For \(T = 4\), \(\theta = 1 - 1/\sqrt {1 + 4(0.5)} = 1 - 1/\sqrt 3 = 0.423\); for \(T = 20\), \(\theta = 1 - 1/\sqrt {11} = 0.698\).

(b) The unit mean \(\bar y_i\) contains \(\alpha _i\) plus an average of \(T\) disturbances, whose variance \(\sigma _\varepsilon ^2/T\) falls with \(T\). As \(T\) grows the mean becomes a precise measure of \(\alpha _i\), and GLS removes more of it, approaching complete demeaning. In the limit the between variation carries no information about \(\beta \) that is not contaminated by \(\alpha _i\), and only the within variation is used.

Problem 16.8.3. Explain why a fixed effects earnings regression cannot estimate the return to years of schooling for adults who have completed their education, and what alternatives are available.

Show solution

Solution. Completed schooling is constant over time for each adult, so it is collinear with the individual intercepts and is removed by the within transformation; there is no within variation to identify its coefficient. Alternatives are random effects (if ability is uncorrelated with schooling, which is doubtful), Mundlak’s regression, which estimates the coefficient of time-invariant variables under the weaker assumption that they are uncorrelated with the part of the effect not explained by the means of the time-varying regressors, the Hausman–Taylor estimator, which uses time-varying exogenous regressors as instruments, or a different design, such as twins or siblings, where differences between family members provide the variation.

Problem 16.8.4. A fixed effects estimate is \(0.52\) (s.e. \(0.028\)) and the random effects estimate is \(0.82\) (s.e. \(0.026\)). Carry out the Hausman test, and say which estimate you would report.

Show solution

Solution. \(H = (0.52 - 0.82)^2/(0.028^2 - 0.026^2) = 0.09/0.000108 = 833\), on one degree of freedom, far above \(3.84\). Random effects is rejected; the effects are correlated with the regressor, and the fixed effects estimate \(0.52\) should be reported, with cluster-robust standard errors. (Software 16.7.2, with unrounded values, gives \(673\).)

Problem 16.8.5. Using Theorem 16.6.1, find the probability limit of the fixed effects estimator for \(\rho = 0.8\) and \(T = 10\), and compare with the large-\(T\) approximation.

Show solution

Solution. \(A = 1 - (1 - 0.8^{10})/(10 \times 0.2) = 1 - 0.8926/2 = 0.5537\). Then \(-(1.8/9)A = -0.1107\), and the correction factor is \(1 - (1.6/(0.2 \times 9))(0.5537) = 1 - 0.4922 = 0.5078\). The bias is \(-0.1107/0.5078 = -0.218\), so \(\plim \hat \rho _{\text {FE}} = 0.582\). The approximation \(-(1 + \rho )/(T - 1) = -0.2\) gives \(0.6\). With persistent series the bias remains large even with ten periods.

Problem 16.8.6. A state-level policy dummy \(d_s\) is used in a regression of student test scores on \(d_s\), with \(30\) students in each of \(50\) states. The intraclass correlation of the disturbances within states is \(0.05\). By what factor do conventional standard errors understate the true standard error of the coefficient of \(d_s\)?

Show solution

Solution. The regressor is constant within states, so \(\rho _x = 1\), and the Moulton factor is \(1 + (30 - 1)(1)(0.05) = 2.45\). The true variance is about \(2.45\) times the conventional one, and the standard error \(\sqrt {2.45} = 1.57\) times as large. A seemingly small intraclass correlation has a large effect when clusters are big and the regressor varies only between clusters. Clustering by state, with \(50\) clusters, is the remedy.
