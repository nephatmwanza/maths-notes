13.6 Practice Problems

Problem 13.6.1. A survey measures household income with error. The variance of true income is \(36\) and the variance of the measurement error is \(12\). The true marginal propensity to spend on food is \(0.15\).

(a)
Find the probability limit of the least squares estimate.
(b)
A second, independent measurement of income is available. Explain how to use it to obtain a consistent estimate.

Show solution

Solution. (a) By Theorem 13.1.1, \(\lambda = 36/(36 + 12) = 0.75\), so \(\plim b = 0.75 \times 0.15 = 0.1125\).

(b) Use the second measurement \(Z\) as an instrument for the first. It is correlated with true income, so it is relevant, and its error is independent of the first measurement’s error and of the disturbance, so it is uncorrelated with \(\varepsilon - \beta m\). Then \(b_{\text {IV}} = \sum zy/\sum zx \xrightarrow {p} 0.15\).

Problem 13.6.2. In Haavelmo’s model \(C_t = \alpha + \beta Y_t + u_t\), \(Y_t = C_t + I_t\), suppose \(\beta = 0.8\), \(\sigma _u^2 = 4\) and \(\sigma _I^2 = 36\).

(a)
Find the probability limit of the least squares estimate of \(\beta \).
(b)
Show that IV with \(I_t\) as the instrument is consistent, and write the estimator.

Show solution

Solution. (a) By Theorem 13.1.2, \(\plim b = 0.8 + 0.2 \times 4/40 = 0.82\).

(b) \(I_t\) is uncorrelated with \(u_t\) by assumption and correlated with \(Y_t\) through \(Y_t = (\alpha + I_t + u_t)/(1 - \beta )\). So \(b_{\text {IV}} = \sum i_tc_t/\sum i_ty_t\), in deviations, and \(\plim b_{\text {IV}} = \Cov (I, C)/\Cov (I, Y)\). Since \(C = Y - I\), \(\Cov (I, C) = \Cov (I, Y) - \sigma _I^2\), with \(\Cov (I, Y) = \sigma _I^2/(1 - \beta )\), so \(\plim b_{\text {IV}} = 1 - (1 - \beta ) = \beta \).

Problem 13.6.3. For five observations \(z = (1, 2, 3, 4, 5)\), \(x = (2, 3, 5, 4, 6)\) and \(y = (4, 5, 9, 8, 11)\), compute the least squares and IV estimates of the slope and intercept in \(y = \alpha + \beta x + u\), with \(z\) as the instrument for \(x\).

Show solution

Solution. The means are \(\bar z = 3\), \(\bar x = 4\) and \(\bar y = 7.4\). In deviations, \(\sum zx = 9\), \(\sum zy = 17\), \(\sum x^2 = 10\) and \(\sum xy = 18\). So \(b = 18/10 = 1.8\) with intercept \(7.4 - 1.8(4) = 0.2\), and \(b_{\text {IV}} = 17/9 = 1.889\) with intercept \(7.4 - 1.889(4) = -0.156\). Both lines pass through the means; they differ in the slope, which IV computes from the covariation with \(z\) instead of with \(x\) itself.

Problem 13.6.4. An earnings equation \(\log w = \beta _1 + \beta _2S + \beta _3\text {exper} + \beta _4\text {exper}^2 + u\) is estimated with schooling \(S\) treated as endogenous. Instruments available are parents’ education (two variables) and distance to the nearest college.

(a)
Is the equation identified? How many overidentifying restrictions are there?
(b)
Least squares gives \(\hat \beta _2 = 0.074\) (s.e. \(0.004\)) and 2SLS gives \(0.101\) (s.e. \(0.013\)). Carry out the Hausman test.
(c)
The Sargan statistic is \(6.9\). What do you conclude, and what are the limits of the conclusion?

Show solution

Solution. (a) There is one endogenous regressor and three excluded instruments, so the order condition holds with \(\ell - k = 2\) overidentifying restrictions. (Experience and its square are treated as exogenous and are their own instruments.) The rank condition requires the instruments to be jointly significant in the first stage.

(b) \(H = (0.101 - 0.074)^2/(0.013^2 - 0.004^2) = 0.000729/0.000153 = 4.76\), which exceeds the \(\chi ^2_1\) \(5\%\) point \(3.84\): exogeneity of schooling is rejected at \(5\%\) (but not at \(1\%\), \(6.63\)).

(c) Against \(\chi ^2_2\), with \(5\%\) point \(5.99\) and \(1\%\) point \(9.21\), the overidentifying restrictions are rejected at \(5\%\) but not at \(1\%\): there is some evidence that at least one instrument is invalid. Parents’ education may affect earnings directly, through networks or inherited ability, and so is suspect. But the test cannot say which instrument is at fault, and it would not detect a problem common to all three.

Problem 13.6.5. Prove the Wald estimator formula of Example 13.2.3: with a binary instrument, \(b_{\text {IV}} = (\bar y_1 - \bar y_0)/(\bar x_1 - \bar x_0)\). In a study, the \(n_1\) people with \(z = 1\) have mean outcome \(12.4\) and mean regressor \(14.1\), and the \(n_0\) people with \(z = 0\) have \(11.2\) and \(12.6\). Find \(b_{\text {IV}}\).

Show solution

Solution. With \(\bar z = n_1/n\), \begin {align*} \sum (z_i - \bar z)y_i &= \sum _{z_i = 1}y_i - \bar z\sum y_i = n_1\bar y_1 - \frac {n_1}{n}(n_1\bar y_1 + n_0\bar y_0)\\ &= \frac {n_1n_0}{n}(\bar y_1 - \bar y_0) . \end {align*}

The same calculation for \(x\) gives \(\frac {n_1n_0}{n}(\bar x_1 - \bar x_0)\), and the ratio is the Wald estimator. Here \(b_{\text {IV}} = (12.4 - 11.2)/(14.1 - 12.6) = 1.2/1.5 = 0.8\).

Problem 13.6.6. In the model \(y = \beta x + u\) with one endogenous regressor and first stage \(x = \pi z + v\), let \(b\) be the 2SLS estimate, \(\hat x = \hat \pi z\) and \(\hat v = x - \hat x\).

(a)
Show that the second-stage residuals are \(y - \hat xb = \hat u + \hat vb\), where \(\hat u = y - xb\).
(b)
Find the probability limit of \(\frac 1n\sum (y_i - \hat x_ib)^2\), and explain why the second stage run by hand gives the wrong standard error.

Show solution

Solution. (a) \(y - \hat xb = (y - xb) + (x - \hat x)b = \hat u + \hat vb\).

(b) As \(n \to \infty \), \(b \to \beta \), \(\hat \pi \to \pi \), \(\hat u_i\) behaves like \(u_i\) and \(\hat v_i\) like \(v_i\), so \[ \frac 1n\sum (y_i - \hat x_ib)^2 \xrightarrow {p} E(u + \beta v)^2 = \sigma _u^2 + 2\beta \sigma _{uv} + \beta ^2\sigma _v^2 . \] This is the variance of \(u + \beta v = y - \beta \pi z\), the disturbance of the reduced form, not \(\sigma _u^2\). The hand-run second stage therefore uses the wrong residual variance; the right one uses \(\hat u = y - xb\), because the structural disturbance is defined with the actual regressor \(x\). In Software 13.5.1 the error made the standard error a quarter too small, but it can go either way, depending on the sign of \(\sigma _{uv}\).
