7.2 The weak law of large numbers and the central limit theorem

Theorem 7.2.1 (The weak law of large numbers). Let \(X_1, X_2, \dots \) be independent, identically distributed random variables, each with finite mean \(\mu \) and finite variance \(\sigma ^2\). Then for any \(\varepsilon > 0\), \[ P\Bigl (\Bigl |\frac {X_1 + X_2 + \dots + X_n}{n} - \mu \Bigr | \ge \varepsilon \Bigr ) \to 0 \qquad (n \to \infty ) . \]

Proof. Write \(\overline X = (X_1 + \dots + X_n)/n\), so \(E(\overline X) = \mu \) and \(\Var (\overline X) = \sigma ^2/n\). By Chebyshev’s inequality, \[ P\bigl (|\overline X - \mu | \ge \varepsilon \bigr ) \le \frac {\Var (\overline X)}{\varepsilon ^2} = \frac {\sigma ^2}{n\varepsilon ^2} \to 0 \qquad (n \to \infty ) , \] and a probability cannot be negative, so the limit is \(0\). □

Lemma 7.2.2 (Continuity of moment generating functions). Let \(Y_1, Y_2, \dots \) be random variables such that \(Y_n\) has distribution function \(F_n\) and moment generating function \(M_n(t)\), and let \(Y\) have distribution function \(F\) and moment generating function \(M(t)\). If \(M_n(t) \to M(t)\) for all \(t\) in an interval about \(0\), then \(F_n(y) \to F(y)\) at every \(y\) where \(F\) is continuous; that is, \(Y_n \to Y\) in distribution (in law).

Theorem 7.2.3 (The central limit theorem). Let \(X_1, X_2, \dots \) be independent and identically distributed, each with mean \(\mu \) and variance \(\sigma ^2\). Then the distribution of \[ \frac {X_1 + X_2 + \dots + X_n - n\mu }{\sigma \sqrt n} \] tends to the standard normal as \(n \to \infty \). That is, for every \(a \in (-\infty , \infty )\), \[ P\Bigl (\frac {X_1 + X_2 + \dots + X_n - n\mu }{\sigma \sqrt n} \le a\Bigr ) \to \int _{-\infty }^a\frac {1}{\sqrt {2\pi }}e^{-y^2/2}\,dy = \Phi (a) . \]

Proof. We assume that the moment generating function \(M(t)\) of the \(X_i\) exists near \(0\). Note first that \[ \frac {X_1 + \dots + X_n - n\mu }{\sigma \sqrt n} = \frac {\overline X - \mu }{\sigma /\sqrt n} = \frac {\sqrt n(\overline X - \mu )}{\sigma } . \] Suppose first that \(\mu = 0\) and \(\sigma ^2 = 1\), so that this quantity is \(Z_n = \sqrt n\,\overline X = \sum _{i=1}^nX_i/\sqrt n\). Then \begin {align*} M_{Z_n}(t) &= E\bigl (e^{tZ_n}\bigr ) = E\Bigl (\prod _{i=1}^ne^{tX_i/\sqrt n}\Bigr ) \\ &= \prod _{i=1}^nE\bigl (e^{tX_i/\sqrt n}\bigr ) \qquad \text {(independence)} \\ &= \bigl (M(t/\sqrt n)\bigr )^n \qquad \text {(identical distributions).} \end {align*}

Let \(L(t) = \ln M(t)\). Since \(M(0) = 1\), \(M'(0) = \mu = 0\) and \(M''(0) = E(X^2) = 1\), \begin {gather*} L(0) = 0 , \qquad L'(0) = \frac {M'(0)}{M(0)} = 0 , \\ L''(0) = \frac {M''(0)M(0) - \bigl (M'(0)\bigr )^2}{\bigl (M(0)\bigr )^2} = 1 . \end {gather*} By Lemma 7.2.2 it is enough to show that \(M_{Z_n}(t) \to e^{t^2/2}\), the moment generating function of \(N(0, 1)\), that is, that \(\ln M_{Z_n}(t) = nL(t/\sqrt n) \to t^2/2\). Writing \(nL(t/\sqrt n) = L(t/\sqrt n)/(1/n)\), the numerator and the denominator both tend to \(0\), so by L’Hôpital’s rule (differentiating with respect to \(n\)), \begin {align*} \lim _{n\to \infty }\frac {L(t/\sqrt n)}{1/n} &= \lim _{n\to \infty }\frac {L'(t/\sqrt n)\cdot \bigl (-\frac 12tn^{-3/2}\bigr )}{-n^{-2}} = \lim _{n\to \infty }\frac {t\,L'(t/\sqrt n)}{2n^{-1/2}} . \end {align*}

This is again of the form \(0/0\), because \(L'(0) = 0\), and applying the rule once more, \begin {align*} \lim _{n\to \infty }\frac {t\,L'(t/\sqrt n)}{2n^{-1/2}} &= \lim _{n\to \infty }\frac {t\,L''(t/\sqrt n)\cdot \bigl (-\frac 12tn^{-3/2}\bigr )}{-n^{-3/2}} \\ &= \frac {t^2}{2}\lim _{n\to \infty }L''(t/\sqrt n) = \frac {t^2}{2}L''(0) = \frac {t^2}{2} . \end {align*}

Therefore \(M_{Z_n}(t) \to e^{t^2/2}\).

For general \(\mu \) and \(\sigma ^2\), apply this to the standardised variables \(Y_i = (X_i - \mu )/\sigma \), which have mean \(0\) and variance \(1\). Since \[ \frac {1}{\sqrt n}\sum _{i=1}^nY_i = \frac {\sum _{i=1}^n(X_i - \mu )}{\sigma \sqrt n} = \frac {X_1 + \dots + X_n - n\mu }{\sigma \sqrt n} , \] the result follows. □

Note. The proof shows that the moment generating function of \(Z_n\) converges to \(e^{t^{2}/2}\), which is that of the standard normal. That alone is a statement about functions; what turns it into a statement about distributions is the continuity theorem of the chapter on generating functions. Without that theorem the calculation above would establish nothing about \(P(Z_n\leq z)\), and it is worth being explicit that the last step of the proof is an appeal to it rather than an observation.

The argument also assumes the moment generating function exists in a neighbourhood of the origin, which is more than the theorem needs. The general proof replaces \(M\) by the characteristic function \(\varphi (t)=E(e^{itX})\), which always exists, and appeals to Lévy’s continuity theorem in its place. The structure is identical.

Remark. The theorem says that for each fixed \(a\), \[ \lim _{n\to \infty }P\Bigl (\frac {\sqrt n(\overline X - \mu )}{\sigma } \le a\Bigr ) = \Phi (a) = \int _{-\infty }^a\frac {1}{\sqrt {2\pi }}e^{-y^2/2}\,dy . \] It can be shown that the convergence is in fact uniform in \(a\). (A sequence of functions \(f_n(a) \to f(a)\) uniformly in \(a\) if for each \(\varepsilon > 0\) there is an \(N\) such that \(|f_n(a) - f(a)| < \varepsilon \) for all \(a\) whenever \(n > N\).)

Example 7.2.4. The number of tourists who sign up for a trip to the Victoria Falls is a Poisson random variable with mean \(60\). The tour operator will hire two buses if \(80\) or more sign up, and only one bus otherwise. What is the probability that the operator will have to hire two buses?

Solution. Let \(X\) be the number of tourists who sign up. The exact probability is \[ P(X \ge 80) = \sum _{x=80}^\infty \frac {60^xe^{-60}}{x!} = 1 - \sum _{x=0}^{79}\frac {60^xe^{-60}}{x!} , \] which is tedious to compute by hand, so we approximate it with the central limit theorem. A Poisson variable with mean \(60\) is the sum of \(60\) independent Poisson variables each with mean \(1\), \(X = \sum _{i=1}^{60}X_i\) with \(X_i \sim P(1)\), so \(X\) has mean \(60\) and variance \(60\), and \begin {align*} P(X \ge 80) &= P\Bigl (\frac {X - 60}{\sqrt {60}} \ge \frac {80 - 60}{\sqrt {60}}\Bigr ) \\ &\approx P(Z \ge 2.582) = 1 - \Phi (2.58) = 0.0049 . \end {align*}

The exact value is \(0.0078\). The approximation captures the order of magnitude, but in the far tail of a skewed distribution like the Poisson it is only rough.

Example 7.2.5. If \(10\) fair dice are rolled, find the approximate probability that the sum obtained is between \(40\) and \(50\).

Solution. Let \(X_i\) be the score on the \(i\)th die and \(X = \sum _{i=1}^{10}X_i\) the sum. For each die, \[ E(X_i) = \sum _{j=1}^6j\cdot \frac 16 = \frac 16\cdot \frac {6 \times 7}{2} = \frac 72 , \qquad E(X_i^2) = \sum _{j=1}^6j^2\cdot \frac 16 = \frac {91}{6} , \] using \(\sum _{x=1}^Nx^2 = N(N + 1)(2N + 1)/6 = 91\) for \(N = 6\). So \[ \Var (X_i) = \frac {91}{6} - \Bigl (\frac 72\Bigr )^2 = \frac {91}{6} - \frac {49}{4} = \frac {35}{12} , \] and \(E(X) = 10 \times \frac 72 = 35\), \(\Var (X) = 10 \times \frac {35}{12} = \frac {175}{6}\), so \(\operatorname {sd}(X) = \sqrt {175/6} = 5.401\). By the central limit theorem, \begin {align*} P(40 < X < 50) &\approx P\Bigl (\frac {40 - 35}{5.401} < Z < \frac {50 - 35}{5.401}\Bigr ) = \Phi (2.777) - \Phi (0.926) \\ &= 0.9973 - 0.8228 = 0.175 . \end {align*}

Theorem 7.2.6 (Central limit theorem for independent random variables). Let \(X_1, X_2, \dots \) be independent random variables with means \(\mu _i = E(X_i)\) and variances \(\sigma _i^2 = \Var (X_i)\). Suppose that

(a)
the \(X_i\) are uniformly bounded: there is an \(M > 0\) such that \(P(|X_i| < M) = 1\) for all \(i\); and
(b)
\(\sum _{i=1}^\infty \sigma _i^2 = \infty \).

Then \[ \lim _{n\to \infty }P\Biggl (\frac {\sum _{i=1}^n(X_i - \mu _i)}{\sqrt {\sum _{i=1}^n\sigma _i^2}} \le a\Biggr ) = \Phi (a) . \]

Probably the best-known result in probability theory is the strong law of large numbers. It states that the average of a sequence of independent random variables with a common distribution will, with probability \(1\), converge to the mean of that distribution.

Theorem 7.2.7 (The strong law of large numbers). Let \(X_1, X_2, \dots \) be independent, identically distributed random variables, each with finite mean \(\mu \). Then, with probability \(1\), \[ \frac {X_1 + X_2 + \dots + X_n}{n} \to \mu \qquad (n \to \infty ) ; \] that is, \[ P\Bigl (\lim _{n\to \infty }\frac {X_1 + X_2 + \dots + X_n}{n} = \mu \Bigr ) = 1 . \]

Proof. We prove the theorem under the extra assumption that the fourth moment is finite, \(E(X_i^4) = K < \infty \). Suppose first that \(\mu = 0\), and let \(S_n = X_1 + X_2 + \dots + X_n\). Expanding \[ E(S_n^4) = E\bigl [(X_1 + \dots + X_n)^4\bigr ] \] gives terms of five types, with \(i, j, l, k\) distinct: \[ X_i^4 , \qquad X_i^3X_j , \qquad X_i^2X_j^2 , \qquad X_i^2X_jX_l , \qquad X_iX_jX_lX_k . \] By independence and \(E(X_i) = 0\), three of these types have expectation zero: \begin {gather*} E(X_i^3X_j) = E(X_i^3)E(X_j) = 0 , \\ E(X_i^2X_jX_l) = E(X_i^2)E(X_j)E(X_l) = 0 , \\ E(X_iX_jX_lX_k) = E(X_i)E(X_j)E(X_l)E(X_k) = 0 . \end {gather*} There are \(n\) terms \(X_i^4\), and for each of the \(\binom n2\) pairs \(i < j\) there are \(\binom 42 = 6\) terms \(X_i^2X_j^2\). Therefore \[ E(S_n^4) = nK + 6\binom n2\bigl (E(X^2)\bigr )^2 = nK + 3n(n - 1)\bigl (E(X^2)\bigr )^2 . \qquad (*) \] Since \(0 \le \Var (X^2) = E(X^4) - \bigl (E(X^2)\bigr )^2 = K - \bigl (E(X^2)\bigr )^2\), we have \(\bigl (E(X^2)\bigr )^2 \le K\), and \((*)\) gives \(E(S_n^4) \le nK + 3n(n - 1)K\). Hence \[ E\Bigl (\frac {S_n^4}{n^4}\Bigr ) \le \frac {K}{n^3} + \frac {3(n - 1)K}{n^3} \le \frac {K}{n^2} + \frac {3K}{n^2} = \frac {4K}{n^2} , \] and so \[ E\Bigl (\sum _{n=1}^\infty \frac {S_n^4}{n^4}\Bigr ) = \sum _{n=1}^\infty E\Bigl (\frac {S_n^4}{n^4}\Bigr ) \le 4K\sum _{n=1}^\infty \frac {1}{n^2} < \infty . \] A non-negative random variable whose expectation is finite is itself finite with probability \(1\), so \(\sum _n S_n^4/n^4 < \infty \) with probability \(1\). The terms of a convergent series tend to zero, so with probability \(1\), \((S_n/n)^4 = S_n^4/n^4 \to 0\), and hence \(S_n/n \to 0\).

For general \(\mu \), apply this to \(Y_i = X_i - \mu \), which have mean \(0\): with probability \(1\), \[ \frac {Y_1 + \dots + Y_n}{n} = \frac {X_1 + \dots + X_n}{n} - \mu \to 0 , \] that is, \((X_1 + \dots + X_n)/n \to \mu \). □
