2 Simple comparative experiments

The simplest experiment compares two treatments. It is worth treating carefully, because every idea of the later sections appears here in its plainest form: a model for the observations, an estimate of experimental error, a test and a confidence interval, a check of the assumptions, a choice of sample size, and the gain from blocking.

Example 2.0.1. A chemical engineer compares the yield (%) of a process run with two catalysts. Sixteen batches of raw material are available; eight are chosen at random for catalyst \(A\) and the other eight are run with catalyst \(B\), in random order. The yields are

batch catalyst \(A\) catalyst \(B\)
\(1\) \(91.5\) \(89.2\)
\(2\) \(94.2\) \(91.0\)
\(3\) \(92.2\) \(90.5\)
\(4\) \(95.4\) \(93.2\)
\(5\) \(91.8\) \(88.9\)
\(6\) \(89.1\) \(87.6\)
\(7\) \(94.7\) \(90.1\)
\(8\) \(89.2\) \(89.9\)
\(\bar y_i\) \(92.2625\) \(90.0500\)
\(s_i^2\) \(5.6741\) \(2.7286\)

Catalyst \(A\) gives the higher mean yield in these data. Is the difference real, or could it have arisen from batch-to-batch variation alone?
