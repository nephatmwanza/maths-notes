4.5 Practice problems

Problem 4.5.1. Define orthogonal contrasts. In a balanced experiment with five treatments, show that the contrasts with coefficients \[ (1, -1, 0, 0, 0) , \quad (1, 1, -2, 0, 0) , \quad (1, 1, 1, -3, 0) , \quad (1, 1, 1, 1, -4) \] are mutually orthogonal, and explain what Theorem 4.2.2 says about them.

Show solution

Solution. Two contrasts \(\sum c_i\mu _i\) and \(\sum d_i\mu _i\) are orthogonal if \(\sum c_id_i/n_i = 0\), which for equal \(n_i\) is \(\sum c_id_i = 0\). Each vector sums to zero, so each is a contrast. For the pairs: \(1 - 1 = 0\); \(1 - 1 = 0\); \(1 - 1 = 0\); \(1 + 1 - 2 = 0\); \(1 + 1 - 2 = 0\); \(1 + 1 + 1 - 3 = 0\). They are four mutually orthogonal contrasts among five treatments (the Helmert contrasts, each comparing a treatment with the average of those before it), so their four sums of squares add up to \(SS_{\mathrm {Treatments}}\) and are independent of one another and of \(SSE\).

Problem 4.5.2. For the coating data, find the sum of squares of the contrast comparing coating types \(1\), \(2\) and \(3\) with types \(4\) and \(5\), test it at \(\alpha = 0.05\), and construct a \(95\%\) confidence interval for the difference between the average of the first three means and the average of the last two.

Show solution

Solution. With integer coefficients \((2, 2, 2, -3, -3)\), \(\sum c_iy_{i\cdot } = 2(331 + 308 + 287) - 3(260 + 251) = 1852 - 1533 = 319\) and \[ SS_C = \frac {319^2}{4(4 + 4 + 4 + 9 + 9)} = \frac {101761}{120} = 848.01 , \] so \(F_0 = 848.01/8.15 = 104.05 > 4.54\), and the contrast is significant. With coefficients \((\frac 13, \frac 13, \frac 13, -\frac 12, -\frac 12)\), \(\hat C = 77.167 - 63.875 = 13.29\) and \[ \operatorname {se}(\hat C) = \sqrt {\frac {8.15}{4}\Bigl (\frac 39 + \frac 24\Bigr )} = 1.303 , \] so the interval is \(13.29 \pm 2.131(1.303)\), that is \((10.51,\ 16.07)\).

Problem 4.5.3. For the rations data, use Tukey’s method at \(\alpha = 0.05\) to find which pairs of rations differ in mean organ damage. (\(q_{0.05}(4, 12) = 4.199\).)

Show solution

Solution. \(HSD = 4.199\sqrt {0.4881/4} = 1.467\). The means are \(5.350\) (ration \(2\)), \(6.575\) (\(1\)), \(6.700\) (\(4\)) and \(9.200\) (\(3\)). Ration \(3\) differs from each of the others (by \(2.500\), \(2.625\) and \(3.850\), all above \(1.467\)), while the differences among rations \(1\), \(2\) and \(4\) (\(1.225\), \(1.350\), \(0.125\)) are not significant. So ration \(3\), animal fat in a large proportion, causes more damage than the rest, which form a single group. This is consistent with the factorial contrasts of Example 4.2.3: both main effects push ration \(3\) upwards.

Problem 4.5.4. Five coatings give \(10\) pairwise comparisons. For this family, and for a family of \(m = 50\) pre-specified contrasts in the same experiment (\(t_{0.0005,15} = 4.073\)), compare the multipliers of the Bonferroni and Scheffé methods. When should each be used?

Show solution

Solution. For \(m = 10\) the Bonferroni multiplier is \(t_{0.0025,15} = 3.286\), smaller than Scheffé’s \(3.496\), so Bonferroni gives shorter intervals. For \(m = 50\) it is \(4.073\), larger than \(3.496\), and Scheffé’s intervals are shorter even though they cover every contrast. Bonferroni is preferable for a modest number of contrasts fixed in advance; Scheffé for many contrasts or for contrasts suggested by the data. (For the pairwise comparisons alone, Tukey’s \(q/\sqrt 2 = 3.088\) beats both.)

Problem 4.5.5. For the lack-of-fit data of Section 3 (two observations at each of \(x = 1, \dots , 5\), with totals \(6.6\), \(8.8\), \(11.2\), \(13.2\), \(15.0\) and \(MSE = 0.088\)), partition the treatment sum of squares into orthogonal polynomial components and confirm the conclusion of the lack-of-fit test.

Show solution

Solution. With \(r = 2\) and the coefficients for \(t = 5\):

component \(\sum c_iy_{i\cdot }\) \(SS_C\) \(F_0\)
linear \(21.2\) \(21.2^2/(2 \times 10) = 22.472\) \(255.4\)
quadratic \(-1.2\) \(1.2^2/(2 \times 14) = 0.051\) \(0.58\)
cubic \(-0.4\) \(0.4^2/(2 \times 10) = 0.008\) \(0.09\)
quartic \(0.8\) \(0.8^2/(2 \times 70) = 0.005\) \(0.05\)

The total is \(22.536 = SS_{\mathrm {Treatments}}\). Only the linear component is significant (\(F_{0.05,1,5} = 6.61\)), and the three higher components together give \(0.064\) on \(3\) degrees of freedom, which is exactly the lack-of-fit sum of squares of the straight line.

Problem 4.5.6. In a balanced design, show that the maximum of \(SS_C\) over all contrasts is \(SS_{\mathrm {Treatments}}\), and find the contrast that attains it.

Show solution

Solution. With \(n_i = r\), \(SS_C = r\bigl (\sum c_i\bar y_{i\cdot }\bigr )^2/\sum c_i^2 = r\bigl (\sum c_i(\bar y_{i\cdot } - \bar y_{\cdot \cdot })\bigr )^2/\sum c_i^2\), since \(\sum c_i = 0\). By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality this is at most \(r\sum (\bar y_{i\cdot } - \bar y_{\cdot \cdot })^2 = SS_{\mathrm {Treatments}}\), with equality when \(c_i \propto \bar y_{i\cdot } - \bar y_{\cdot \cdot }\). The best single contrast compares the treatments in proportion to their observed deviations, which is a contrast chosen by the data; this is why testing it as if it had been planned would be wrong, and why Scheffé’s critical value \((t - 1)F_\alpha \), rather than \(F_\alpha \), is the right yardstick for it.
