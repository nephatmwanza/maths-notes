11.3 The two-factor random effects model

Suppose both factors of a two-factor experiment are random: \(a\) levels of \(A\) and \(b\) levels of \(B\), each sampled from a population, with \(n\) replicates of every combination. The model is \[ y_{ijk} = \mu + \tau _i + \beta _j + (\tau \beta )_{ij} + \varepsilon _{ijk} , \] where \(\tau _i \sim N(0, \sigma ^2_\tau )\), \(\beta _j \sim N(0, \sigma ^2_\beta )\), \((\tau \beta )_{ij} \sim N(0, \sigma ^2_{\tau \beta })\) and \(\varepsilon _{ijk} \sim N(0, \sigma ^2)\), all independent. Then \(\Var (y_{ijk}) = \sigma ^2_\tau + \sigma ^2_\beta + \sigma ^2_{\tau \beta } + \sigma ^2\), and the hypotheses are \(\sigma ^2_\tau = 0\), \(\sigma ^2_\beta = 0\) and \(\sigma ^2_{\tau \beta } = 0\). The sums of squares are those of the fixed effects analysis. One lemma does most of the work.

Lemma 11.3.1. If the random vector \(w \in \mathbb R^m\) has \(E(w) = 0\) and \(\Var (w) = vI\), and \(P\) is an orthogonal projection, then \(E\|Pw\|^2 = v\operatorname {rank}(P)\).

Proof. \(E\|Pw\|^2 = E(w^\top Pw) = \operatorname {tr}(P\Var (w)) = v\operatorname {tr}(P)\), and the trace of a projection is its rank. □

Two cases are used repeatedly. If \(w_1, \dots , w_m\) are independent with variance \(v\), centring is a projection of rank \(m - 1\), so \(E\sum _i(w_i - \bar w)^2 = (m - 1)v\). For an \(a \times b\) array of independent \(w_{ij}\) of variance \(v\), the double centring \(w_{ij} - \bar w_{i\cdot } - \bar w_{\cdot j} + \bar w_{\cdot \cdot }\) is a projection of rank \((a - 1)(b - 1)\), and the map from \(w\) to the array of row-mean deviations \(\bar w_{i\cdot } - \bar w_{\cdot \cdot }\) (each repeated \(b\) times) is a projection of rank \(a - 1\), so \begin {gather*} E\sum _i\sum _j(w_{ij} - \bar w_{i\cdot } - \bar w_{\cdot j} + \bar w_{\cdot \cdot })^2 = (a - 1)(b - 1)v , \\ E\sum _i(\bar w_{i\cdot } - \bar w_{\cdot \cdot })^2 = \frac {(a - 1)v}b . \end {gather*}

Theorem 11.3.2. In the two-factor random effects model, \begin {align*} E(MS_A) &= \sigma ^2 + n\sigma ^2_{\tau \beta } + bn\sigma ^2_\tau , & E(MS_B) &= \sigma ^2 + n\sigma ^2_{\tau \beta } + an\sigma ^2_\beta , \\ E(MS_{AB}) &= \sigma ^2 + n\sigma ^2_{\tau \beta } , & E(MSE) &= \sigma ^2 . \end {align*}

Each \(SS/E(MS)\) is a \(\chi ^2\) variable on its degrees of freedom, and the four sums of squares are independent.

Proof. Averaging the model, \[ \bar y_{i\cdot \cdot } - \bar y_{\cdot \cdot \cdot } = (\tau _i - \bar \tau ) + \bigl (\overline {(\tau \beta )}_{i\cdot } - \overline {(\tau \beta )}_{\cdot \cdot }\bigr ) + (\bar \varepsilon _{i\cdot \cdot } - \bar \varepsilon _{\cdot \cdot \cdot }) , \] the \(\beta _j\) cancelling. The three terms are independent with mean zero, so the expected sum of squares over \(i\) is the sum of three expectations, which the lemma gives as \((a - 1)\sigma ^2_\tau \), \((a - 1)\sigma ^2_{\tau \beta }/b\) and \((a - 1)\sigma ^2/(bn)\) (the \(\bar \varepsilon _{i\cdot \cdot }\) are independent with variance \(\sigma ^2/(bn)\)). As \(SS_A = bn\sum _i(\bar y_{i\cdot \cdot } - \bar y_{\cdot \cdot \cdot })^2\), \[ E(SS_A) = (a - 1)(bn\sigma ^2_\tau + n\sigma ^2_{\tau \beta } + \sigma ^2) . \] \(B\) is the same with the roles of the factors exchanged. For the interaction, all main effects cancel from the double centring of the cell means \(\bar y_{ij\cdot }\), leaving the double centrings of \((\tau \beta )_{ij}\) and of \(\bar \varepsilon _{ij\cdot }\), so \[ E(SS_{AB}) = n\bigl [(a - 1)(b - 1)\sigma ^2_{\tau \beta } + (a - 1)(b - 1)\sigma ^2/n\bigr ] , \] and \(E(SSE) = ab(n - 1)\sigma ^2\) as before.

For the distributions, write \(\bar J_m = \frac 1m\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top \) and order \(y\) by \(i\), then \(j\), then \(k\). The covariance matrix of \(y\) is \[ \Var (y) = \sigma ^2_\tau \,I_a \otimes J_b \otimes J_n + \sigma ^2_\beta \,J_a \otimes I_b \otimes J_n + \sigma ^2_{\tau \beta }\,I_a \otimes I_b \otimes J_n + \sigma ^2 I , \] with \(J_m = m\bar J_m\). Writing each \(I_m = \bar J_m + (I_m - \bar J_m)\) and expanding expresses \(\Var (y)\) as \(\sum _X\lambda _XP_X\), where \(P_A = (I_a - \bar J_a) \otimes \bar J_b \otimes \bar J_n\) and the other \(P_X\) are the mutually orthogonal projections whose squared lengths \(\|P_Xy\|^2\) are the sums of squares, and the coefficient \(\lambda _X\) is exactly the expected mean square above. Then \(P_Xy\) is normal with covariance \(\lambda _XP_X\), the vectors \(P_Xy\) are uncorrelated and hence independent, and \(\|P_Xy\|^2/\lambda _X \sim \chi ^2_{\operatorname {rank}P_X}\). □

The expected mean squares determine the tests. The interaction is tested against error, but the main effects are tested against the interaction: \begin {gather*} H_0 : \sigma ^2_{\tau \beta } = 0 : \quad F_0 = \frac {MS_{AB}}{MSE} , \\ H_0 : \sigma ^2_\tau = 0 : \quad F_0 = \frac {MS_A}{MS_{AB}} , \qquad H_0 : \sigma ^2_\beta = 0 : \quad F_0 = \frac {MS_B}{MS_{AB}} . \end {gather*} In each case the two mean squares have equal expectations under \(H_0\) and are independent scaled \(\chi ^2\) variables, so \(F_0\) has an \(F\) distribution. Using \(MSE\) as the denominator for the main effects, as in the fixed effects analysis, would be wrong whenever \(\sigma ^2_{\tau \beta } > 0\): the test would reject too often. The reason is visible in the model: with new levels of \(B\), the apparent effect of a level of \(A\) changes by an amount of variance \(\sigma ^2_{\tau \beta }/b\), and that is the noise against which a main effect of \(A\) must be judged. The variance components are estimated by \begin {gather*} \hat \sigma ^2 = MSE , \qquad \hat \sigma ^2_{\tau \beta } = \frac {MS_{AB} - MSE}n , \\ \hat \sigma ^2_\tau = \frac {MS_A - MS_{AB}}{bn} , \qquad \hat \sigma ^2_\beta = \frac {MS_B - MS_{AB}}{an} . \end {gather*}

With \(a = 4\), \(b = 3\), \(n = 2\) and \(C = 1458^2/24 = 88573.5\), \begin {gather*} SS_A = \frac {401^2 + 367^2 + 374^2 + 316^2}6 - C = 630.1667 , \\ SS_B = \frac {456^2 + 479^2 + 523^2}8 - C = 289.75 , \end {gather*} and from the cell totals \(SS_{AB} = 77.5833\); the total is \(SST = 1182.5\), so \(SSE = 185\).

The interaction is not significant (\(F_0 = 12.93/15.42 = 0.84\), \(F_{0.05, 6, 12} = 3.00\)). Both main effects are, tested against \(MS_{AB}\): \(F_0 = 210.06/12.93 = 16.24\) for operators (\(F_{0.05, 3, 6} = 4.76\), \(P = 0.003\)) and \(F_0 = 144.88/12.93 = 11.20\) for filters (\(F_{0.05, 2, 6} = 5.14\), \(P = 0.009\)). Operators and filters both add to the variability of the process. The variance components are \begin {gather*} \hat \sigma ^2_\tau = \frac {210.06 - 12.93}6 = 32.85 , \qquad \hat \sigma ^2_\beta = \frac {144.88 - 12.93}8 = 16.49 , \\ \hat \sigma ^2 = 15.42 , \end {gather*} and \(\hat \sigma ^2_{\tau \beta } = (12.93 - 15.42)/2 = -1.24\).

The negative interaction estimate says that the interaction component is negligible, and it suggests a reduced model without it. Since the \(F\) statistic for the interaction is below \(1\) (\(P = 0.56\)), a common rule is to pool \(SS_{AB}\) with \(SSE\): the pooled mean square is \((77.58 + 185)/(6 + 12) = 14.59\) on \(18\) degrees of freedom, and in the reduced model \(E(MS_A) = \sigma ^2 + bn\sigma ^2_\tau \), so \begin {align*} \text {operators: } F_0 &= 210.06/14.59 = 14.40 , & \hat \sigma ^2_\tau &= 195.47/6 = 32.58 , \\ \text {filters: } F_0 &= 144.88/14.59 = 9.93 , & \hat \sigma ^2_\beta &= 130.29/8 = 16.29 . \end {align*}

The conclusions are unchanged, but the denominators now have \(18\) degrees of freedom instead of \(6\). Pooling is a decision made after looking at the data, and it is safe only when the evidence for dropping the term is strong: a large \(P\)-value (above about \(0.25\)) and a good reason to expect no interaction. Operators contribute the largest component, about twice the run-to-run error.
