3.6 Variance-stabilising transformations

When the error variance changes with the mean, a transformation of the response can often make it constant. The guide is the delta method.

Proposition 3.6.1. Suppose \(E(Y) = \mu \) and the standard deviation of \(Y\) is proportional to a power of the mean, \(\sigma _Y = \theta \mu ^\alpha \). For a smooth function \(g\), \[ \Var \bigl (g(Y)\bigr ) \approx g'(\mu )^2\,\theta ^2\mu ^{2\alpha } , \] which is constant in \(\mu \) when \(g(y) = y^{1-\alpha }\) for \(\alpha \ne 1\) and \(g(y) = \log y\) for \(\alpha = 1\).

Proof. By Taylor’s theorem, \(g(Y) \approx g(\mu ) + g'(\mu )(Y - \mu )\), so \(\Var \bigl (g(Y)\bigr ) \approx g'(\mu )^2\Var (Y) = g'(\mu )^2\theta ^2\mu ^{2\alpha }\). This does not depend on \(\mu \) when \(g'(\mu ) \propto \mu ^{-\alpha }\). Integrating, \(g(\mu ) \propto \mu ^{1-\alpha }\) if \(\alpha \ne 1\) and \(g(\mu ) = \log \mu \) if \(\alpha = 1\). □

relationship \(\alpha \) transformation
\(\sigma _Y\) constant \(0\) none
\(\sigma _Y^2 \propto \mu \) (Poisson counts) \(\frac 12\) square root, \(\sqrt y\)
\(\sigma _Y \propto \mu \) (constant coefficient of variation) \(1\) logarithm, \(\log y\)
\(\sigma _Y \propto \mu ^{3/2}\) \(\frac 32\) reciprocal square root
\(\sigma _Y \propto \mu ^2\) \(2\) reciprocal, \(1/y\)

For a binomial proportion \(\hat p\) based on \(m\) trials, \(\Var (\hat p) = p(1 - p)/m\), which is not a power of the mean, and the same argument gives the arcsine transformation \(\arcsin \sqrt {\hat p}\), with variance close to \(1/(4m)\) whatever \(p\).

The exponent \(\alpha \) can be estimated from the data. Taking logarithms of \(\sigma _i = \theta \mu _i^\alpha \) gives \(\log \sigma _i = \log \theta + \alpha \log \mu _i\), so the slope of a straight line fitted to the points \((\log \bar y_{i\cdot },\ \log s_i)\) estimates \(\alpha \).

Example 3.6.2. Four insecticides were each applied to five randomly chosen plots, and the number of surviving insects counted on each plot:

counts
insecticide \(\bar y_{i\cdot }\) \(s_i\)
\(1\) \(4\) \(7\) \(3\) \(6\) \(5\) \(5\) \(1.58\)
\(2\) \(12\) \(18\) \(15\) \(11\) \(19\) \(15\) \(3.54\)
\(3\) \(34\) \(47\) \(39\) \(31\) \(49\) \(40\) \(7.87\)
\(4\) \(78\) \(101\) \(92\) \(70\) \(109\) \(90\) \(16.05\)

The standard deviation grows with the mean, and Bartlett’s test on the raw counts gives \(\chi _0^2 = 16.61\) on \(3\) degrees of freedom (\(p = 0.0008\)). The fitted slope of \(\log s_i\) on \(\log \bar y_{i\cdot }\) is \(\hat \alpha = 0.80\), which suggests \(y^{0.2}\), between the square root (\(\alpha = \frac 12\), the Poisson value) and the logarithm (\(\alpha = 1\)). Both remove the problem:

group standard deviations
scale Bartlett \(p\)
\(y\) \(1.58\) \(3.54\) \(7.87\) \(16.05\) \(0.0008\)
\(\sqrt y\) \(0.36\) \(0.46\) \(0.62\) \(0.85\) \(0.40\)
\(\log y\) \(0.33\) \(0.24\) \(0.20\) \(0.18\) \(0.64\)

The logarithm stabilises the spread best, which agrees with \(\hat \alpha \) being nearer \(1\) than \(\frac 12\): these counts are more variable than Poisson counts would be, as insect counts usually are. On the log scale the analysis of variance gives \(F_0 = 131.1\) on \(3\) and \(16\) degrees of freedom, and conclusions are stated about the means of \(\log y\), that is, about ratios of typical counts.

Remark. The choice of \(\lambda \) in \(y^\lambda \) can be made formally by the method of Box and Cox, which maximises the likelihood of the transformed model over \(\lambda \). In practice the analyst rounds to a value with an interpretation, \(1\), \(\frac 12\), \(0\) (the logarithm) or \(-1\), and the plot of \(\log s_i\) against \(\log \bar y_{i\cdot }\) usually suffices. A transformation chosen for constant variance often improves normality as well.
