1.2 Principles of experimental design

Three principles underlie every design in this course.

Definition 1.2.1.

(i)
Replication is the repetition of the basic experiment: each treatment is applied independently to more than one experimental unit.
(ii)
Randomisation is the use of a chance mechanism to allocate the treatments to the experimental units, and to fix the order in which the individual runs of the experiment are performed.
(iii)
Blocking is the grouping of experimental units into sets (blocks) that are more alike than the units as a whole, and the comparison of treatments within the blocks. Equivalently, it fixes the levels of a nuisance factor while the factors of interest are varied.

Each principle has two jobs.

Replication

  • It allows the experimenter to obtain an estimate of the experimental error, which is the yardstick against which differences between treatments are judged. Without replication there is no way to tell a treatment difference from unit-to-unit variation.
  • It makes the estimates more precise: if \(\sigma ^2\) is the variance of a single observation, the mean of \(r\) replicates has variance \(\sigma ^2/r\).

Replication is not the same as repeated measurement. Weighing the same pig three times reduces measurement error but tells us nothing about variation between pigs.

Randomisation

  • It makes valid the assumption, required by the statistical methods, that the observations (or errors) are independently distributed random variables.
  • It helps average out the effects of factors that are unknown or not controlled, so that no treatment is systematically favoured.

Blocking

  • It improves the precision of comparisons between the factors of interest.
  • It reduces or eliminates the effect of known nuisance factors on those comparisons.

The rule that follows is short: block what you can, randomise what you cannot.
